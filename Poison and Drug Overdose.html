High-Yield Course Overview

Based on the lecture by Isagani C. Rodriguez, MD, FPCP, FPCCP. Designed as a comprehensive, exam-ready mastery module for Philippine medical students preparing for Medicine 2 Long Exams and the Physician Licensure Examination (PLE).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Evaluate patients presenting with toxic exposures using focused history and physical examination.
  • Analyze signs and symptoms due to unknown poison to identify probable toxins and responsible toxidromes.
  • Formulate systematic appropriate interventions among patients presenting with signs/symptoms of poisoning.
  • Assess and monitor response to therapeutic intervention and evaluate the need to liaise with subspecialties.
  • Outline preventive and immediate measures for accidental exposures at home, workplace, and environment.

⚡ Core Clinical Paradigms

  • Paracelsus Principle: "All substances are poisons... The right dose differentiates a poison and a remedy."
  • Resuscitation Priority: ABCD (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability) takes absolute priority over antidote search.
  • Dose Determines Response: Toxic response is a function of concentration, exposure pathway, and duration.
  • No Antidote for Caustics: Management is purely supportive; NEVER neutralize or induce vomiting.

1. Environmental Poisoning

Must Know

Dibromochloropropane (DBCP)

Historical Context & Application: Widely used since the mid-1950s as a potent nematicide against worms attacking banana roots. Commonly handled by banana plantation workers ("Bananeros") in South America and Mindanao, Philippines (e.g., Del Monte and Dole) under trade names Amazon or Nemagon.

Mechanism & Toxicity: Acts as an estrogen mimic. Pre-market animal testing revealed atrophied testes, but the chemical somehow passed licensing. Banned in 1979 in developed nations but continued to be sold in third-world countries.

  • Male Effects: Male sterility with azoospermia/oligospermia (observed in banana plantation workers and even sons of exposed mothers).
  • Female Effects: Increased incidence of breast cancer among female workers.
  • Antidote: NO KNOWN ANTIDOTE. Tissue damage is completely irreversible.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Philippine tropical farmers often do not comply with protective clothing regulations due to heat, leading to direct transdermal and inhalational absorption.
Must Know

Paralytic Shellfish Poisoning (PSP) & Harmful Algal Blooms

Etiology: Caused by Harmful Algal Blooms (HABs) or sudden proliferation of planktonic organisms, usually from the Gonyaulacoid dinoflagellate (Gonyaulax) family.

Terminology Clarification: "Red Tide" indicates an HAB, but color is not always red—it may appear yellow, brown, green, blue, or milky/misty.

Toxin & Mechanism: Produces Saxitoxin, a heat-stable neurotoxin (cannot be destroyed by cooking, boiling, or heating) that selectively blocks neuronal voltage-gated Na+ channels, preventing action potential propagation.

  • Vectors: Filter feeders (shellfish and bivalve mollusks) concentrate saxitoxin in their flesh and pose high risk to humans.
  • Fish Safety: Fish concentrate the toxin strictly in their viscera/intestines. Removing intestines/viscera before cooking spares humans from toxicity.
  • Clinical Manifestations: Perioral numbness/tingling, prickling sensation, dizziness, muscle weakness, paralysis of extremities and respiratory muscles, dyspnea, dysphagia, dyslexia, tachycardia, vomiting, diarrhea. All motor functions are affected in a dose-dependent manner.
  • Management: Purely supportive—fluid resuscitation and mechanical ventilatory support.

2. Principles of Toxicology & Dose-Response

💡 Paracelsus Foundational Principle: "All substances are poisons; there is none that is not a poison. The right dose differentiates a poison and a remedy."
Examples of Median Lethal Dose ($LD_{50}$): Sugar ($30,000\text{ mg/kg}$), Ethanol ($13,700\text{ mg/kg}$), Water ($>90,000\text{ mg/kg}$). Oxygen administered at $>50\%\text{ FiO}_2$ for $>48\text{ hours}$ leads to pulmonary toxicity and irreversible fibrosis.

Core Definitions

  • Toxicology: Study of adverse effects of toxicants on living organisms. Adverse effect = any deviation from normal homeostasis.
  • Toxicant (Poison): Any agent capable of producing a deleterious response in a biological system.
  • Poisoning: Overdose of drugs, medicaments, or biological substances; or wrong substance given/taken in error (excludes standard adverse drug reactions or hypersensitivity of correctly administered drugs).

Determinants of Toxic Response & Exposure Pathways

  • Exposure Pathway Onset Speed: Intravenous (IV) > Inhalation > Intraperitoneal > Intramuscular > Per Orem (PO) > Topical.
  • Topical Exceptions: Certain organophosphates (malathion, parathion) readily penetrate intact skin.
  • Exposure Duration Categories:
    • Acute: $< 24\text{ hours}$ (usually 1 single exposure).
    • Subacute: $\le 1\text{ month}$ (repeated doses).
    • Subchronic: $1\text{ to } 3\text{ months}$ (repeated doses).
    • Chronic: $> 3\text{ months}$ (repeated doses).

Dose-Response Curve & Sigmoidal Relationship

The response of the human body to a drug or toxin follows a sigmoidal (S-shaped) curve when plotted against log-dose.

Figure 2. Sigmoidal curve: Segment 0-1 is No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL); Segment 2-3 is linear graded response; Segment 4 is maximum response plateau.

Dose Metrics & Indices

  • Effective Dose ($ED_{50}$): Dose that produces the desired therapeutic effect in 50% of the test population.
  • Lethal Dose ($LD_{50}$): Dose of a chemical/poison that causes death in 50% of the test population (expressed in $\text{mg/kg}$).
  • Lethal Concentration ($LC_{50}$): Concentration in environment (air/water) causing death in 50% of test population in a specified timeframe ($\text{mg/L}$ for liquid, $\text{ppm}$ for gas).
  • Therapeutic Index ($TI$): Ratio of $LD_{50}$ to $ED_{50}$ ($TI = \frac{LD_{50}}{ED_{50}}$).
    • A substance is considered SAFE if $TI \ge 10$.
    • A low ratio indicates a narrow therapeutic window and high toxicity potential.
  • Therapeutic Window: Dose range between minimum effective dose and minimum toxic dose.
Exam Tip

Theophylline: Narrow Therapeutic Window Example

Theophylline demonstrates progressive multi-organ toxicity as blood levels rise along the dose-response curve:

  1. 0–10 mcg/mL (NOAEL): Therapeutic Bronchodilation.
  2. 15–20 mcg/mL: Gastrointestinal symptoms (Nausea, Vomiting).
  3. 20–30 mcg/mL: Cardiovascular symptoms (Tachycardia, Dysrhythmias).
  4. 30–40 mcg/mL: Metabolic derangements (Severe Hypokalemia).
  5. >40 mcg/mL: Central Nervous System excitation (Intractable Seizures, Coma, Death).

FDA Pregnancy Categories & PLLR (2015) Rule

  • Category A: Controlled human studies fail to show fetal risk in 1st or later trimesters (e.g., Amoxicillin, Cephalosporins). Most safe.
  • Category B: Animal studies show no risk, but no controlled human studies; OR animal studies show risk but human studies do not.
  • Category C: Animal studies show adverse effects, no human studies. Give only if benefit outweighs risk.
  • Category D: Positive evidence of human fetal risk. May be used in life-threatening emergency if benefits outweigh risk.
  • Category X: Fetal abnormalities demonstrated in animals/humans. Risks strictly outweigh any potential benefit. Absolute contraindication.
  • Category N: Unclassified (new drugs or Emergency Use Authorization like Remdesivir during pandemics).
  • Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Final Rule (PLLR, 2015): Replaced letter categories with narrative subsections: Pregnancy, Lactation, and Females and Males of Reproductive Potential.
    • Clinical Example: Dutasteride/Finasteride (5-alpha reductase inhibitors). Males taking this must avoid getting partners pregnant (anti-androgen causes abnormal male genital development). Transferred via semen; couples must use barrier protection or postpone pregnancy for 6 months after stopping.

3. Toxidromes & Clinical Evaluation

⭐ Toxidrome Concept: A constellation of signs and symptoms associated with specific classes of poisons. Essential when history is missing, unreliable, or patient is found unconscious. Relies heavily on autonomic physical exam findings: Eyes, Skin, Secretions, Vital Signs.

1. Anticholinergic Toxidrome

Mnemonic: "Mad as a hatter, blind as a bat, hot as hell, red as a beet, dry as a bone, seizing like a squirrel."

  • Vital Signs: Tachycardia, Hypertension, Febrile (Hyperthermia), Tachypnea.
  • Physical Exam: Mydriasis (dilated pupils), dry flushed skin, absent urinary/sweat secretions, urinary retention.
  • Culprits: 1st generation antihistamines (Diphenhydramine, Hydroxyzine), Antipsychotics (Phenothiazines, Haloperidol, Atypicals), Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs), Carbamazepine.

2. Cholinergic Toxidrome

Opposite of anticholinergic. Caused by inhibition of acetylcholinesterase, leading to acetylcholine overload.

  • Vital Signs: Bradycardia, Hypotension, Hypoventilation.
  • Physical Exam: Miosis (pinpoint pupils), drenched skin, diaphoresis, hyperactive bowel sounds.
  • Mnemonics:
    • SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Diarrhea/Defecation, GI Dysmotility, Emesis.
    • DUMBBELS: Diarrhea, Urination, Miosis/Muscle weakness, Bronchorrhea, Bradycardia, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation/Sweating.
  • Culprits: Organophosphates (Malathion, Parathion—classic kerosene + garlic breath smell), Carbamates.

3. Opioid Toxidrome

  • Triad: CNS depression (Coma), Respiratory depression (fatal), Universal Miosis (Pinpoint Pupils).
  • Vital Signs: Severe Bradycardia, Hypotension, Hypothermia.
  • Culprits: Morphine, Codeine, Fentanyl, Heroin.

4. Sympathomimetic Toxidrome

  • Vital Signs: Tachycardia, Tachyarrhythmias, Severe Hypertension, Hyperthermia.
  • Physical Exam: Mydriasis, Diaphoretic/Wet skin (distinguishes from Anticholinergic dry skin), normal secretions, hyperreflexia, agitation.
  • Culprits: Cocaine, Amphetamines ("poor man's cocaine"), Ephedrine, Pseudoephedrine, PPA, MAOIs, TCAs.

Diagnostics & Matthew-Rumack Nomogram

  • Specimen Collection:
    • Paracetamol Serum Levels: Blood drawn strictly at 4 hours post-ingestion (or later) to match peak plasma concentration for outcome prognosis.
    • Tox Screen Protocol: $5\text{--}10\text{ mL}$ heparinized & clotted blood on ice; $200\text{ mL}$ urine; $200\text{ mL}$ gastric aspirate (frozen for medico-legal evidence).
  • Matthew-Rumack Nomogram: Used to interpret 4-hour post-ingestion paracetamol levels to decide $N$-acetylcysteine treatment. If serum levels lie on or above the probable risk line, start NAC immediately.
    Clinical Rule: If history indicates toxic ingestion ($>150\text{ mg/kg}$), treat immediately with NAC without waiting for serum assay results if turnaround time is delayed ($>24\text{ hours}$).

4. General Approach & Resuscitation (ABCD Priority)

⭐ Resuscitation Rule: The ABCD sequence (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Electrolytes, Find Antidote) takes precedence over finding antidotes and can be executed simultaneously.
A — Airway Management & Indications for Intubation ▼
  • Action: Remove obstructions (dentures, vomit), suction secretions to prevent aspiration.
  • C-Spine Rule: Always rule out cervical spine fracture before intubating trauma/fall victims with altered sensorium. Immobilize neck.
  • Indications for Intubation: Acute respiratory failure, unconscious patient, loss of gag reflex, status epilepticus.
  • ⚠️ CONTRAINDICATION: DO NOT INTUBATE AN ACTIVELY SEIZING PATIENT! Administer IV Diazepam first, wait for convulsion to terminate, then secure the airway.
B — Breathing & Oxygenation ▼
  • Obtain Arterial Blood Gases (ABGs). Provide supplementary oxygen if hypoxemic.
  • Initiate mechanical ventilation if ventilatory drive or effort is compromised.
C — Circulation & Hemodynamic Support ▼
  • Establish prompt IV access. Hydrate with D5NSS or D5 Acetated/Lactated Ringer's Solution.
  • Place patient in Trendelenburg position (head down) if hypotensive.
  • Inotropes / Vasopressors: Norepinephrine, Dopamine, or Dobutamine if persistent hypotension occurs despite fluid resuscitation and positioning to maintain vital organ perfusion and urine output.
D — CNS Disability & Seizure Management ▼
  • Assess Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Baseline GCS non-responsive score is 3.
  • Seizure Drug of Choice: Diazepam 5 mg IV (or 0.3 mg/kg) or Phenytoin $10\text{--}20\text{ mg/kg}$ IV loading.
    Pitfall: NEVER give Diazepam or Phenytoin Intramuscularly (IM) due to erratic and poor absorption.
  • Isoniazid (INH) Exception: Seizures caused by INH toxicity are unrelenting and completely refractory to repeated doses of Diazepam. Etiology: INH depletes pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (active Vit B6), inhibiting GABA synthesis.
    Treatment: Administer Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) in a 1:1 gram-for-gram ratio with the ingested INH dose.

Grade 2 Agitated, confused, hallucinations; follows commands; vital signs mildly to moderately increased. Responds to pain but not voice; vocalizes but cannot converse; spontaneous motor activity present; intact brainstem reflexes.

Electrolyte & Metabolic Emergency Management

Hypokalemia

Causes: Theophylline overdose (stimulates $Na^+/K^+$ ATPase pump pushing $K^+$ into cells), forced alkaline diuresis, chronic corticosteroid use.

Treatment: Potassium Chloride (KCl) IV infusion at maximum rate of $40\text{ mEq/hr}$.

Hyperkalemia

Causes: Digitalis/Digoxin, Beta-blockers (inhibits $Na^+/K^+$ pump), ACE inhibitors, CO/Cyanide poisoning (inhibits ATP production stopping pump), Rhabdomyolysis.

Treatment (Cardioprotection):

  • Glucose-Insulin: $50\text{ mL D50W} + 10\text{ Units Regular Insulin}$ (shifts $K^+$ intracellularly).
  • Calcium Gluconate 10%: $5\text{--}10\text{ mL}$ slow IV push (stabilizes cardiac membrane potential if peaked T waves present).

Hypothermia vs. Hyperthermia

Hypothermia (Rectal $<35^\circ\text{C}$): EtOH, TCAs, Barbiturates, OHAs, Phenothiazines.
Management: Wrap in thick/thermal blankets. Do NOT rapidly heat up body externally.

Hyperthermia: Amphetamines, Atropine, Salicylates, 1st gen Antihistamines, INH, Phenytoin, Theophylline.
Management: Immediate ice bath immersion / wrap in cold wet towels + cold saline IV infusion.

Hypoglycemia

Causes: Acute EtOH intoxication, Salicylate toxicity (glycogen depletion).

Treatment: IV $50\%\text{ Dextrose in Water (D50W)} + \mathbf{Thiamine\text{ (Vit B1)}}$ to prevent Wernicke's Encephalopathy.

5. Decontamination, Elimination & Antidote Therapy

1. Decontamination Techniques

  • Skin Contamination: Discard all clothes, wash/bathe thoroughly with water and soap. Personnel MUST wear protective apparel (gloves, eye shields). Crucial for organophosphates.
  • Eye Contamination: Direct eye irrigation with normal saline or tap water for at least 30 minutes.
  • Emesis Induction: Rarely used. Kinchay extract ($15\text{ mL}$) or Ipecac syrup ($15\text{--}30\text{ mL}$).
    Absolute Contraindications to Emesis: Decreased sensorium, absent gag reflex, late pregnancy, ingestion of caustics (double mucosa damage) or hydrocarbons (causes fatal ARDS/pneumonitis if aspirated), convulsants, arrhythmogenic drugs, cardiac disease, aneurysms.
  • Gastric Lavage: Indicated ONLY within 1 hour of ingestion in an awake patient or after securing airway with ET tube. Contraindicated in caustics.

2. Activated Charcoal & Adsorption

  • Dosing: Charcoal to poison ratio must be 10:1.
  • Multiple-Dose Activated Charcoal (MDAC): Enhances elimination of drugs undergoing enterohepatic recirculation (e.g., Amphetamines, Paracetamol, Organochlorines).
  • CONTRAINDICATIONS: Caustics (acids/alkalis), petroleum distillates.
  • INEFFECTIVE IN (Does NOT bind):
    Alcohols (Methanol, Ethanol, Isopropyl, Ethylene Glycol)
    Cyanide
    Iron
    Lithium

Specific Antidote Mechanisms

Cyanide Antidote Regimen

Cyanide binds to cytochrome oxidase, halting mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC).

  1. Amyl Nitrate (Inhalation) / Sodium Nitrite (IV): Induces methemoglobinemia, which avidly binds cyanide to form cyanmethemoglobin, freeing cytochrome oxidase.
  2. Sodium Thiosulfate (IV): Donates sulfur groups to convert cyanide to non-toxic thiocyanate, excreted in urine.
  3. Hydroxocobalamin IV: Binds cyanide directly to form non-toxic Cyanocobalamin (Vitamin B12), excreted renally.

Sources: Inappropriately prepared cassava / labong (cyanoglycosides), jewelry cleaners (cyanide salts used to purify gold).

Toxic Alcohols: Methanol vs. Ethylene Glycol

Pathophysiology: Alcohol Dehydrogenase (ADH) converts Methanol $\rightarrow$ Formaldehyde $\rightarrow$ Formic Acid (causes optic neuritis and blindness). ADH converts Ethylene Glycol $\rightarrow$ Glycolic Acid / Oxalic Acid (causes renal failure).

Antidote Strategy: Administer Ethanol or Fomepizole to competitively inhibit ADH enzyme.

Cofactors:
• Methanol: Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)
• Ethylene Glycol: Pyridoxine (B6) & Thiamine (B1)

Other Key Specific Antidotes

  • Paracetamol: $N$-acetylcysteine (NAC) — donates cysteine to regenerate hepatic glutathione and neutralize toxic NAPQI.
  • Beta-Blocker Toxicity: Glucagon — increases cAMP inotropy/chronotropy independent of beta-2 receptors.
  • Digitalis/Digoxin: Digoxin-specific Fab antibodies (Digibind).
  • Watusi (Firecrackers): Egg whites (8–12 egg whites) locally in stomach.
  • Iodine Ingestion: Starch solution.
  • Nicotine / Strychnine / Quinine: Potassium Permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$).

6. Caustic Acid & Alkali Ingestions

Must Know

Caustic Acids

Agents: Sulfuric acid (lead batteries), Muriatic acid / Hydrochloric acid (bathroom cleaners), Nitric acid (jewelers), Glacial acetic acid.

Mechanism of Injury: Produces COAGULATION NECROSIS. Forms a thick protective necrotic mucosal eschar that limits deeper layer tissue penetration.

Primary Target: Stomach and pylorus (esophagus relatively spared due to rapid transit time).

Late Sequelae: Gastric outlet obstruction, stenosis/stricture of esophagus, stomach, and pylorus.

Common Pitfall

Caustic Alkalis

Agents: Sodium hydroxide (NaOH / Liquid Sosa), Sodium carbonate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$), Potassium hydroxide (KOH), Concentrated Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$ in jewelry cleaners).

Mechanism of Injury: Produces LIQUEFACTION NECROSIS. Dissolves mucosal protein/lipid membranes, leading to rapid, deep transmural liquefaction penetration into mediastinum and peritoneum for days!

Primary Target: Esophagus (severe liquefactive esophagitis).

Late Sequelae: Transmural perforation, stricture formation, severe risk of Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma.

Minimum Lethal Dose: $5\text{ g } / \text{ 70 kg}$.

⛔ STRICT CONTRAINDICATIONS IN ALL CAUSTIC INGESTIONS:
  1. NEVER insert Nasogastric Tube (NGT) if $>30\text{ minutes}$ post-ingestion (risk of esophageal perforation). If patient arrives with NGT already in place, DO NOT remove it!
  2. NEVER induce vomiting / emesis (re-exposes mucosa to caustic agent, doubling injury).
  3. NEVER administer neutralizing agents (e.g., antacids or vinegar)—the exothermic chemical reaction generates extreme heat and massive carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), causing stomach explosion/perforation!

Staging & Management of Caustic Alkali Burns

Burn Depth Stage Pathologic Involvement Clinical Management Protocol
1st Degree Burn Mucosal hypermia & superficial edema only Pain control, oral antacids. Excellent prognosis.
2nd Degree Burn Transmucosal ulcerations, erosions, exudates Pain control, IV Hydrocortisone (to prevent stricture formation), NPO status.
3rd Degree Burn Deep transmural necrosis into mediastinum, pleura, or peritoneum Pain control, broad-spectrum IV Antibiotics, Hydrocortisone, Emergency Endoscopy / Surgery.
Glottic Compromise Severe laryngeal / glottic edema Emergency Tracheostomy (Endotracheal intubation contraindicated due to tissue necrosis).
