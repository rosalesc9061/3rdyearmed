Adams & Victor Chapter 14: Deafness, Dizziness, Disorders of Equilibrium & Syncope
🔍

High-Yield Executive Summary

This comprehensive, self-contained interactive reviewer covers Deafness, Dizziness, Disorders of Equilibrium, and Syncope, adhering rigorously to Adams and Victor’s Principles of Neurology (Chapter 14) and tailored for 3rd-year Philippine medical students preparing for departmental long exams, clerkship shelf exams, and the Philippine Physician Licensure Examination (PLE).

⭐ Core Examination Rule
As a general rule, the association of vertigo and deafness signifies a disease of the end organs for hearing and vestibular function, or of the 8th cranial nerve (CN VIII). The precise anatomical locus is determined by tests of labyrinthine and auditory function, coupled with cranial nerve examination and imaging.

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate Conductive, Sensorineural (Cochlear vs. Retrocochlear), and Central Hearing Loss using clinical tuning fork tests (Weber, Rinne, Schwabach) and advanced audiometric procedures (Békésy, Speech Discrimination, BAEP, Acoustic-Stapedial Reflex).
  • Analyze the mechanisms, causes, and diagnostic workup of Tonal (Subjective) vs. Non-tonal/Pulsatile (Objective) Tinnitus, including vascular bruits, palatal/middle ear myoclonus, and superior canal dehiscence (Lloyd Minor syndrome).
  • Master the anatomical organization of the Cochlear and Vestibular apparatus, ascending pathways (Cochlear nuclei → Superior Olivary Complex → Lateral Lemniscus → Inferior Colliculus → Medial Geniculate Body → Area 41), and central vestibular projections (MLF, vestibulospinal tracts, vestibulocerebellum).
  • Categorize dizziness into four distinct clinical types: True Vertigo, Near-Syncope, Disequilibrium, and Ill-defined Lightheadedness/Psychiatric Dizziness.
  • Diagnose and manage major peripheral vestibular disorders: Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV - canalolithiasis/cupulolithiasis), Ménière Disease (endolymphatic hydrops, Tumarkin drop attacks), Vestibular Neuritis, Bilateral Vestibulopathy, Cogan Syndrome, and Acoustic Schwannoma.
  • Distinguish Peripheral from Central Nystagmus and Vertigo, recognizing stroke syndromes (Wallenberg, AICA infarction), Multiple Sclerosis, Basilar Migraine, and Syncope mechanisms.

Anatomy & Physiology of Hearing

The acoustic division of CN VIII has its cell bodies in the spiral ganglion of the cochlea. Bipolar neurons send peripheral processes to the Organ of Corti and central processes via the cochlear nerve to the brainstem.

Figure 14-1A/B Recreation: Auditory sound transmission path from external auditory canal through middle ear ossicles to the cochlea.

Ascending Auditory Pathway

Auditory nerve impulses pass sequentially through 6 neural stations before reaching the primary cortex:

  1. Spiral Ganglion: Bipolar primary afferent neurons innervating hair cells.
  2. Dorsal & Ventral Cochlear Nuclei: Located at the junction of the pontine tegmentum and medulla. Fibers bifurcate here.
  3. Superior Olivary Complex & Trapezoid Body: Receives crossing (80%) and uncrossed (20%) fibers. Subserves sound localization and the origin of efferent olivocochlear fibers (Rasmussen bundle).
  4. Lateral Lemniscus: Main ascending tract carrying impulses through the pontine brainstem.
  5. Inferior Colliculus: Tectal auditory reflex center (oculopostural orientation to sound).
  6. Medial Geniculate Body (Thalamus): Special sensory thalamic relay station.
  7. Primary Auditory Cortex: Transverse gyri of Heschl (Brodmann Area 41) in the superior temporal gyrus. Subcortical radiations pass through the ventrolateral posterior putamen.
💡 Clinical Pearl: Unilateral Cortical Lesions Do NOT Cause Deafness
Because ascending acoustic pathways cross bilaterally at multiple levels (trapezoid body, lateral lemniscus, commissures of Probst and inferior colliculus), a unilateral lesion of the brainstem or auditory cortex does NOT cause unilateral deafness. Unilateral deafness strictly signifies a peripheral lesion (external ear, middle ear, cochlea, or CN VIII).

Classification of Deafness

Type Anatomical Site Etiologies Key Characteristics
Conductive Deafness External or Middle Ear Cerumen impaction, otitis media, otosclerosis, tympanic membrane perforation, eustachian tube obstruction. Equal loss of all frequencies or predominant low-frequency loss; bone conduction > air conduction; sound louder in affected ear on Weber test.
Sensorineural: Cochlear Organ of Corti (Inner ear) Ménière disease, presbycusis, noise trauma, ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, cisplatin, loop diuretics), mumps. High-frequency loss; Loudness Recruitment PRESENT; Diplacusis present; Békésy Type II audiogram.
Sensorineural: Retrocochlear CN VIII (Cochlear Nerve) Acoustic Schwannoma, meningioma, carcinomatous/lymphomatous meningitis, demyelination (MS). Marked loss of Speech Discrimination (<30%); Recruitment ABSENT; Békésy Type III/IV audiogram; tone decay.
Central Deafness Cochlear Nuclei to Auditory Cortex Bilateral temporal lobe strokes, massive pontine tegmental lesions, inherited autosomal dominant tone deafness. Rare; cortical deafness requires bilateral geniculocortical interruption; preserved pure tones but lost word comprehension in pure word deafness.

Tuning Fork Examination (512 Hz)

Test Procedure Normal Result Conductive Deafness Sensorineural Deafness
Weber Test 512 Hz fork on forehead midline or central incisor. Sound heard equally in both ears. Lateralizes to AFFECTED ear (ambient noise is muted on affected side). Lateralizes to NORMAL ear (normal ear perceives bone vibration better).
Rinne Test Fork on mastoid process until unheard, then held 2.5 cm from auditory meatus. Air Conduction (AC) > Bone Conduction (BC) by 2:1 ratio (Positive Rinne). BC > AC (Abnormal / Negative Rinne). AC > BC (Positive Rinne), but quantitatively reduced duration in both.
Schwabach Test Compares patient's bone conduction duration with examiner's normal BC. Patient and examiner hear sound for equal duration. Patient hears bone conduction longer than examiner. Patient hears bone conduction shorter duration than examiner.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Bedside Hearing Tests
Ticking watches or hair rubbing emit primarily high-frequency sounds. They are useful screening surrogates for high-tone sensorineural loss (e.g., presbycusis), but will completely miss low-frequency conductive hearing loss (e.g., early otosclerosis).

Special Audiologic & Diagnostic Testing

  • Békésy Audiometry: Continuous vs. Interrupted tone tracings.
    • Type I: Normal or Conductive loss.
    • Type II: Cochlear lesion (Ménière disease; recruitment present).
    • Types III & IV: Retrocochlear lesion (CN VIII Schwannoma; severe tone decay).
  • Speech Discrimination Score: 50 phonetically balanced monosyllabic words at suprathreshold levels. A score <30% is diagnostic of retrocochlear (CN VIII) lesions.
  • Brainstem Auditory Evoked Potentials (BAEP / BAER): Evaluates 5 neuroelectric waves generated along the auditory pathway:
    • Wave I: CN VIII acoustic nerve.
    • Wave II: Cochlear nuclei (pons/medulla).
    • Wave III: Superior olivary complex.
    • Wave IV: Lateral lemniscus.
    • Wave V: Inferior colliculus.
    Board Clue In Brain Death, all waves EXCEPT Wave I are abolished.
  • Acoustic-Stapedial Reflex: High-intensity sound (>70–90 dB) triggers reflex contraction of stapedius muscles bilaterally (CN VIII afferent → CN VII efferent). Muted in conductive ossicular fixation and CN VIII/VII lesions.

Hereditary Deafness & Genetic Syndromes

Nonsyndromic hereditary deafness accounts for 80% of genetic cases. The single most important genetic discovery is the GJB2 gene mutation (Connexin-26) on chromosome 13 (DFNB1 locus), which accounts for 50% of recessive familial deafness and 37% of sporadic congenital deafness. Connexin-26 forms gap junctions necessary for recycling K+ ions from hair cells back into endolymph.

Syndrome Inheritance / Gene Key Clinical Features Board Examination Clue
Waardenburg AD (PAX3, SOX10) Sensorineural deafness, dystopic canthi, heterochromia iridis, white forelock (poliosis). Pigmentary anomalies + lateral displacement of inner canthi.
Branchio-Oto-Renal (BOR) AD (EYA1) Mixed hearing loss, brachial cleft cysts/fistulae, external ear malformations, renal dysplasia. Neck cysts + ear malformations + renal failure.
Stickler Syndrome AD (COL2A1) Progressive SNHL, cleft palate, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, severe myopia, retinal detachment. Cleft palate + joint laxity + retinal detachment.
Usher Syndrome AR (USH1/2/3) Congenital severe SNHL + progressive Retinitis Pigmentosa + vestibular dysfunction. Deafness + night blindness/tunnel vision in 1st/2nd decade.
Pendred Syndrome AR (SLC26A4) Sensorineural hearing loss + Euthyroid Goiter + Mondini dysplasia of bony labyrinth. Deafness + thyroid enlargement in childhood.
Jervell and Lange-Nielsen AR (KCNQ1, KCNE1) Profound congenital SNHL + Prolonged QT Interval on ECG + syncope / sudden cardiac death. Deaf child with syncopal spells during exercise/stress.
Alport Syndrome X-linked (85%) (COL4A5) Progressive SNHL + Hematuria + Glomerulonephritis / End-stage renal disease + Anterior lenticonus. Deafness + hereditary nephritis + ocular lens deformity.
Refsum Disease AR (PHYH) SNHL + Retinitis pigmentosa + Polyneuropathy + Cerebellar ataxia + High CSF protein (Phytanic acid accumulation). Elevated serum phytanic acid; treated with diet restriction.

Inner Ear Aplasias

  • Michel Defect: Complete absence of the otic capsule and CN VIII.
  • Mondini Defect: Incomplete development of bony and membranous labyrinths (cochlea has only 1.5 turns instead of 2.5).
  • Scheibe Defect: Membranous cochleosaccular dysplasia (most common aplasia).

Tinnitus Aurium & Perception Disorders

Tinnitus is defined as any sensation of sound for which there is no source outside the individual. Affects >37 million Americans. It is classified into Tonal (Subjective) and Nontonal/Pulsatile (Objective/Vibratory) forms.

🧠 Exam Tip: Pitch Rules of Tinnitus
  • Conductive Hearing Loss Tinnitus: Low pitch (median 490 Hz, range 90–1450 Hz).
  • Sensorineural Loss Tinnitus: High pitch (median 3900 Hz, range 545–7500 Hz; "chirping" or bell-like).
  • CRITICAL EXCEPTION — Ménière Disease: Low-pitched whooshing, roaring, or buzzing sound (median 320 Hz), despite being a sensorineural disorder!

Pulsatile and Nontonal Tinnitus Causes

Nontonal tinnitus is mechanical in origin, conducted to the inner ear via soft tissues or vascular structures:

Category Specific Cause Mechanism & Features Diagnostic Clue
Vascular Bruit Glomus Jugulare / Tympanicum Tumor, AV Malformation, Intracranial Aneurysm. Turbulent blood flow in vessels adjacent to temporal bone; synchronous with pulse. Bruit heard over mastoid by examiner; reddish mass behind tympanic membrane.
Venous Turbulence Pseudotumor Cerebri (Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension). Pressure gradient between cranial and cervical venous sinuses causing venous flow turbulence. Obliterated by gentle jugular vein compression on symptomatic side.
Arterial Stenosis Carotid Dissection, Fibromuscular Dysplasia, Atherosclerotic Stenosis. High-velocity jet through narrowed carotid or high flow contralateral to carotid occlusion. Unilateral neck/head pain + Horner syndrome + pulsatile bruit.
Middle Ear Myoclonus Palatal Myoclonus, Tensor Tympani / Stapedius muscle spasms. Rhythmic clonic contractions of Eustachian or middle ear musculature. Rhythmic clicking noise; treated with Botulinum toxin or muscle section.
Third Window Defect Superior Canal Dehiscence (Lloyd Minor Syndrome). Thinning or absence of bony roof overlying superior semicircular canal. Autophony (hearing own heartbeats/voice loudly) + Tullio phenomenon (vertigo induced by sound).

Auditory Illusions and Hallucinosis

  • Pontine Auditory Hallucinosis: Complex auditory illusions (organ-like musical tones, buzzing swarm of bees, orchestra tuning up) caused by pontine tegmental lesions. Associated with impaired hearing and intact BAEPs. Patients maintain full insight into the illusory nature.
  • Peduncular/Deafferentation Auditory Hallucinosis: Musical hallucinations occurring in elderly individuals with long-standing neurosensory deafness (analogous to Charles Bonnet syndrome in visual loss). SPECT shows right auditory cortex activation.
  • Paracusis: Abnormal auditory repetition where a sound, voice, or tune is repeated for several seconds (similar to visual palinopsia).
  • Earworm (Involuntary Musical Imagery): Chronic, repetitive internal looping of song fragments; self-limiting or obsessive complaint.

Anatomy & Physiology of Equilibrium

Maintenance of stable posture and gaze requires integrated input from 3 afferent systems: Visual, Vestibular, and Proprioceptive (neck and joint receptors).

Figure 14-1D Recreation: Functional division of the vestibular sensory epithelium into Cristae (angular transducers) and Maculae (linear/gravity force transducers).

Central Vestibular Projections & Pathways

  • Scarpa Ganglion: Bipolar cells in internal auditory meatus providing primary vestibular fibers.
  • 4 Vestibular Nuclei: Superior (Bechterew), Lateral (Deiters), Medial (Schwalbe), Inferior (Spinal/Descending).
  • Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR): Projections via Ascending Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF) to CN III, IV, and VI nuclei to stabilize gaze during head movement.
  • Vestibulospinal Pathways:
    • Lateral Vestibulospinal Tract (Uncrossed): Acts on limb motor neurons to maintain extensor tone and upright stance.
    • Medial Vestibulospinal Tract (Crossed & Uncrossed): Descends in MLF to cervical spinal cord; coordinates neck/head posture.
  • Vestibulocerebellum: Direct primary fibers enter cerebellum via juxtarestiform body to end in flocculonodular lobe and vermis. Fastigial nucleus projects back bilaterally to vestibular nuclei.

Differential Categories of "Dizziness"

Category Subjective Description Pathophysiologic Origin Associated Signs
1. True Vertigo "Environment spinning," rotation, tilting, up-and-down motion, pulsion (drawn to floor). Asymmetrical input from vestibular end organs, CN VIII, or brainstem vestibular nuclei. Nystagmus, ataxia, nausea, vomiting, past pointing, spatial disorientation.
2. Near-Syncope "Faintness," lightheadedness, dimming vision, blacking out, impending loss of consciousness. Transient global cerebral hypoperfusion (orthostasis, arrhythmia, vasovagal). Pallor, diaphoresis, tunnel vision, relief upon lying flat.
3. Disequilibrium "Unsteadiness in the legs," loss of balance while walking; head feels normal. Motor/sensory deficits: peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, Parkinsonism, posterior column loss. Gait ataxia, positive Romberg, abnormal extrapyramidal or motor signs.
4. Giddiness / Psychiatric "Walking on air," "distant feeling," floating, head detachment, inability to focus. Anxiety disorders, panic attacks, phobic postural vertigo, hyperventilation. Normal neuro-otologic exam; provoked by crowded places ("supermarket syndrome").

Bedside Tests of Labyrinthine Function

  • Rapid Head Impulse Test (Halmagyi-Cremer): Patient fixates on examiner's nose while examiner quickly rotates patient's head 10° horizontally. A catch-up saccade indicates loss of VOR on the side toward which the head was turned (pathognomonic for peripheral vestibular loss).
  • Unterberger-Fukuda Marching Test: Patient marches in place with eyes closed and arms extended for 50 steps. Rotation >45° indicates asymmetrical labyrinthine function (rotation toward affected side).
  • Caloric Testing (COWS / CUWD): Head elevated 30° (horizontal canal vertical). Irrigated with cold (30°C) or warm (44°C) water:
    • COWS (Cold Opposite, Warm Same): Refers to the direction of the FAST phase of nystagmus. Cold water induces slow deviation to irrigated side + fast phase to OPPOSITE side. Warm water induces fast phase to SAME side.
    • Bilateral Cold Irrigation: Tonic downward gaze + fast phase UPWARD.
    • Bilateral Warm Irrigation: Tonic upward gaze + fast phase DOWNWARD ("CUWD").

Peripheral Vestibular Disorders

1. Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV)

The most common cause of vertigo. Caused by canalolithiasis (free-floating otolithic debris) or cupulolithiasis in the posterior semicircular canal (90% of cases) or lateral canal (10%).

Figure 14-5/6 Recreation: Diagnostic Dix-Hallpike Test and Therapeutic Epley Canalith Repositioning Sequence for Right Posterior Canal BPPV.
⭐ Diagnostic Criteria of BPPV (Dix-Hallpike Test)
  1. Latency: Nystagmus and vertigo begin 2–10 seconds after reaching head-hanging position.
  2. Brevity: Paroxysm subsides within 30–40 seconds.
  3. Torsional Geotropic Nystagmus: Fast phase beats toward dependent (affected) ear with upward vertical component.
  4. Fatigability: Response diminishes marked upon immediate repetition of maneuver.
  5. Reversal: Direction of nystagmus reverses upon sitting upright.

2. Ménière Disease (Endolymphatic Hydrops)

  • Pathology: Distention of the endolymphatic system leading to micro-ruptures of membranous labyrinth, releasing K+-rich endolymph into perilymph and paralyzing vestibular nerve fibers.
  • Clinical Triad:
    1. Episodic Rotational Vertigo (lasting 20 mins to several hours).
    2. Fluctuating Low-Frequency Sensorineural Hearing Loss.
    3. Low-Pitched Roaring Tinnitus & Aural Fullness (in affected ear).
  • Otolithic Catastrophe of Tumarkin: Abrupt, violent drop attacks caused by sudden deformation of otolithic membranes (utricle/saccule). Patient is thrown to ground without loss of consciousness.
  • Treatment: Low-salt diet, diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide/triamterene), vestibular suppressants (meclizine, promethazine). Intratympanic Gentamicin or surgical endolymphatic sac shunt for recalcitrant cases.

3. Vestibular Neuritis (Neuronitis)

  • Pathology: Acute viral or post-viral inflammatory degeneration of the superior vestibular nerve trunk.
  • Clinical Presentation: Sudden, severe rotational vertigo, continuous for days, with nausea, vomiting, marked gait instability, and spontaneous horizontal-torsional nystagmus beating AWAY from affected side.
  • CRITICAL DIFFERENCE: Auditory function is completely NORMAL (NO hearing loss or tinnitus).
  • Management: Short course of methylprednisolone (100 mg tapered over 3 weeks) significantly accelerates recovery. Vestibular rehabilitation exercises.

4. Bilateral Vestibulopathy & Ototoxicity

  • Etiology: Aminoglycoside antibiotics (Gentamicin, Amikacin, Tobramycin) destroying vestibular hair cells.
  • Clinical Hallmark: NO TRUE VERTIGO! Instead, patients present with severe gait ataxia (worse in dark) and Oscillopsia (illusory jumping of stationary objects during walking due to lost VOR).
  • Diagnostic Test: Bilateral absent responses on caloric and rotational chair testing.

5. Acoustic / Vestibular Schwannoma (CN VIII)

  • Anatomical Site: Internal auditory canal & cerebellopontine angle (CPA). Bilateral in Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF-2).
  • Clinical Sequence: Progressive high-frequency SNHL → Tinnitus → Mild imbalance (vertigo is rare due to central compensation) → CN VII weakness → CN V corneal sensory loss → Cerebellar ataxia.
  • Audiology Profile: Speech discrimination score <30%, ABSENT recruitment, Békésy Type III/IV, delayed Wave V on BAEP.

Central Vertigo & Syncope

Central Vertigo Etiologies

Condition Anatomical Site Associated Neurologic Signs Key Differentiating Features
Wallenberg Syndrome (Lateral Medullary Infarct) PICA or Vertebral Artery occlusion (Lateral Medulla). Ipsilateral Horner syndrome, ipsilateral facial numbness (CN V), contralateral body pain/temp loss, dysphagia/hoarseness (CN IX/X), ipsilateral ataxia. Severe vertigo + lateral propulsion (falling toward lesion side) + crossed sensory loss.
AICA Territory Infarction Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery occlusion. Ipsilateral SNHL, ipsilateral facial weakness (CN VII), ipsilateral ataxia, screaming tinnitus, gaze palsy. Combines peripheral-like hearing loss with central brainstem/cerebellar signs.
Multiple Sclerosis Demyelinating plaque in vestibular nuclei entry zone or MLF. Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia (INO), optic neuritis, upper motor neuron signs, lhermitte sign. Persistent vertigo in adolescent or young adult without hearing loss.
Basilar Migraine (Migrainous Vertigo) Brainstem vascular dysfunction / cortical spreading depression. Visual aura (photopsia/blindness), vertigo, dysarthria, ataxia, tinnitus followed by intense occipital headache. Normal audiometric and caloric tests between attacks; responds to triptans/migraine prophylaxis.

Faintness and Syncope

Syncope is defined as a transient, self-limiting loss of consciousness (TLOC) caused by global cerebral hypoperfusion, characterized by rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous, complete recovery.

Pathophysiologic sequence of Transient Loss of Consciousness due to Global Cerebral Hypoperfusion.

Classification of Syncope

Mechanism Subtypes Pathophysiology Clinical Clues
1. Reflex (Neurally Mediated) Vasovagal (Common Faint), Situational (Micturition, Tussive, Deglutition), Carotid Sinus Syncope. Inappropriate cardio-inhibition (bradycardia) and/or vasodepression (venous pooling) triggered by autonomic reflexes. Clear prodrome: diaphoresis, pallor, nausea, lightheadedness, tunnel vision; triggered by fear, pain, prolonged standing, coughing.
2. Orthostatic Hypotension Neurogenic (Parkinson's, MSA, Pure Autonomic Failure, Diabetic Neuropathy) vs. Non-neurogenic (hypovolemia, drugs). Impaired sympathetic vasoconstrictive reflexes leading to blood pooling in venous bed upon standing. Drop in SBP ≥20 mmHg or DBP ≥10 mmHg within 3 mins of standing; NO reflex tachyadic response in neurogenic forms.
3. Cardiac Syncope Arrhythmia (Sick Sinus, AV Block, VT/VF, Long QT, Brugada) or Structural (Aortic Stenosis, HCM, Atrial Myxoma, PE). Abrupt drop in cardiac output; high sudden death mortality! Occurs while SUPINE or during active exercise; NO prodrome; sudden onset collapse; palpitations prior to event.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Syncope vs. Seizure Differential
Brief myoclonic jerks (<15 seconds) can occur during syncopal cerebral anoxia ("convulsive syncope") and are frequently misdiagnosed as epilepsy.
Key Distinction: Syncopal patients regain full alertness in <1 minute without postictal confusion, whereas Seizure patients display postictal lethargy/confusion, lateral tongue biting, head turn, and prolonged motor activity (>1–2 mins).

⚡ Rapid Clues: "If You See X → Think Y"

  • BAEP Wave I preserved, Waves II-V absent: Brain Death.

  • Tinnitus synchronous with pulse, stops on jugular press: Pseudotumor Cerebri.

🎯 High-Yield Buzzwords
  • Organ of Corti: ~15,000 hair cells (3,500 inner IHCs synapse with 90% afferents).
  • Rasmussen Bundle: Efferent olivocochlear tract from superior olivary complex.
  • Otosclerosis: Stapes fixation at oval window; AD variable penetrance; bilateral conductive loss.
  • Presbycusis: Age-related neuronal degeneration of spiral ganglion; high-tone SNHL.
  • COWS Caloric Mnemonic: Cold Opposite, Warm Same (refers to FAST phase of nystagmus).
  • Halmagyi Head Impulse Test: Catch-up saccade indicates peripheral VOR loss on head turn side.
  • Epley Maneuver: Canalith repositioning for BPPV.
  • Cogan Syndrome: Interstitial keratitis + vertiginous attacks + SNHL + Systemic Vasculitis / Aortic insufficiency.
  • Convulsive Syncope: Brief myoclonic jerks (<15s) due to cerebral anoxia; NO postictal state.
  • Orthostatic Hypotension: SBP drop ≥20 mmHg or DBP drop ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes standing.
