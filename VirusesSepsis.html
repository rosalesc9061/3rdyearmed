Overview: Viruses and Sepsis

Learning Objectives

  • Gain basic knowledge on the more common viral infections encountered in the clinics [cite: 1].
  • Gain basic knowledge on the definition and scope of sepsis and its management in children [cite: 1].
💡

Clinical Pearl: Important Things to Consider in Viral Exanthems

  • Exposure History: Sometimes the only clue (e.g., sibling with measles). However, absence of exposure does not rule out diagnosis [cite: 1].
  • Season: In the Philippines, viral exanthems usually occur during colder months until "ber" months, but can go into summer. Dengue is year-round but peaks in the rainy season [cite: 1].
  • Age: Roseola Infantum is typically seen in infants (9-12 months) and cannot be diagnosed in adolescents [cite: 1].
  • Fever-Rash Relation: Crucial for diagnosis. Does the rash appear while fever is high (Measles), or when fever disappears (Roseola)? [cite: 1].
  • Enanthem/Adenopathy: Koplik's spots = Measles. Forchheimer spots + Cervical adenopathy = Rubella. Aseptic meningitis = Mumps [cite: 1].
  • Rash progression: Note where it starts (e.g., cephalocaudal) and characteristics (desquamation) [cite: 1].

Measles (Rubeola) & Rubella

Measles / Rubeola ("Tigdas")

EtiologyParamyxovirus

Highly infectious, single-stranded, lipid-enveloped RNA virus (Paramyxovirus family, Morbillivirus genus) [cite: 1].

Transmission & Communicability

  • One of the three "true airborne" diseases (along with Varicella and TB) [cite: 1].
  • Airborne droplet nuclei can travel prolonged distances and remain contagious for ≤2 hours in the air or infected surfaces [cite: 1].
  • Communicability: 3 days before up to 4-6 days after the onset of rash [cite: 1].
  • R0: 12-18 (highly contagious); requires 95% vaccine coverage for herd immunity [cite: 1].

4 Phases of Infection [cite: 1]

  1. Incubation: Rash appears 7-14 days post-exposure. Virus migrates to regional lymph nodes.
  2. Prodrome: High fever (39-40°C), 3 C's (Cough, Coryza, Conjunctivitis without purulent discharge). Koplik spots (pathognomonic enanthem on buccal mucosa opposite 2nd molar, 1-4 days prior to rash).
  3. Exanthem: 7-14 days post-exposure. Begins face/upper neck, spreads cephalocaudally. Maculopapular rash becoming confluent/map-like.
  4. Recovery: Rash fades in order of appearance, becomes hyperpigmented, and undergoes brawny desquamation (distinguishing feature - "nagbalat").
⭐

Pathognomonic Signs

Warthin-Finkeldey giant cells (multinucleated cells) and Koplik's spots [cite: 1].

Complications [cite: 1]

  • Acute Otitis Media: Most common.
  • Pneumonia: Most fatal; accounts for ~60% of deaths in children. Suspect if fever persists after 4th day of rash or if rash reaches soles of feet.
  • Acute encephalitis: Most common cause of death in adults.
  • Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE): Most chronic. Fatal behavioral/neurologic deterioration occurring 7-10 years after measles infection.
  • Blindness (associated with Vit A deficiency).

Treatment & Prevention

  • Supportive care (isolation past communicability period, nutrition) [cite: 1].
  • Vitamin A: 2 doses (50k IU for <6mo; 100k IU for 6-11mo; 200k IU for >12mo) [cite: 1].
  • Vaccine (Filipino Infants): 1st dose at 9 months (as early as 6 mos if endemic/outbreak); 2nd dose (MMR) at 15 mos; 3rd dose (MMR) at 4-6 years [cite: 1].

Rubella (German Measles / 3-Day Measles)

EtiologyTogaviridae

Enveloped positive-stranded RNA virus (family Togaviridae, genus Rubivirus). Mild in children, severe in adults. Major significance: transplacental infection causing CRS [cite: 1].

Transmission

Respiratory (droplet within 3 ft) and transplacental routes. Communicable 5 days before to 6 days after rash onset [cite: 1].

Clinical Features [cite: 1]

  • Incubation: 14-21 days.
  • Prodrome: Little to none. Low-grade fever, sore throat, red eyes with/without pain.
  • Pathognomonic Sign: Retroauricular, post-cervical, and/or post-occipital lymphadenopathy (evident 24h before rash).
  • Enanthem: Forchheimer spots (petechial hemorrhages on soft palate).
  • Exanthem: 3-day rash. Pruritic, maculopapular, discrete (does NOT coalesce extensively like measles). Fades from face as it extends down. Does NOT desquamate.
🧠

Exam Tip: Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS)

Classic Triad (3 B's): Bulag (Ocular - Cataracts 43%), Bingi (Sensorineural hearing loss 67%), Butas (Congenital Heart Disease - PDA/pulmonary artery stenosis 50%) [cite: 1]. Highest risk if infected in first 8 weeks (first trimester) [cite: 1].

Complications

  • Thrombocytopenia (2 weeks post-rash) [cite: 1].
  • Arthralgia/Arthritis: 1 week after rash, up to 70% in adolescent/adult females [cite: 1].
  • Encephalitis (most serious) [cite: 1].

Roseola & Varicella

Roseola (Exanthem Subitum / Sixth Disease)

EtiologyHHV-6

Primary cause: Human Herpes Virus 6 (HHV-6). Classic presentation in a 9 to 12-month-old infant [cite: 1].

Clinical Features [cite: 1]

  • Fever: Acute 3 days high-grade fever (up to 40°C). Often associated with febrile seizures.
  • Child is non-toxic looking, playful, good appetite despite fever.
  • Rapid Defervescence: After 3 days, fever drops rapidly and a morbilliform rash appears. ("Tigdas hangin: after the fever blows away, the rash appears").
  • Rash: Maculopapular, faint pink, non-pruritic on trunk, spreads to face/extremities. Does NOT desquamate.
  • Pathognomonic: Nagayama spots (ulcers at uvulopalatoglossal junction).
⚠️

Diagnostic Pitfall

If the rash appears while the fever is still high, it is NOT Roseola. In Roseola, the rash only appears once the fever lyses [cite: 1].

Varicella (Chickenpox)

EtiologyVaricella-Zoster Virus (VZV)

Double-stranded DNA virus (Herpesviridae). Highly contagious, true airborne disease (also contact with vesicles) [cite: 1].

Clinical Presentation [cite: 1]

  • Prodrome: Virtually absent in children. First manifestation is the exanthem.
  • Rash Progression: Macules → Maculopapular → Vesicular (tear-drop shaped) → Crusting → Scab.
  • Intensely pruritic. Danger of secondary bacterial infection due to scratching.
  • Successive crops: Lesions present in several stages of development simultaneously.
  • Starts scalp/face, concentrates on trunk.
⭐

Period of Communicability

1-2 days before rash onset until ALL the lesions have formed crusts [cite: 1].

Complications & Management [cite: 1]

  • Secondary bacterial skin infection: Staph or Strep (most common cause of hospitalization).
  • Pneumonia: Viral or bacterial (common mode of exit/high mortality for adults >45).
  • Treatment: Acyclovir not routine for healthy kids; indicated for >13 yrs, immunocompromised, or chronic steroid/salicylate use.
  • PEP: Vaccine within 3-5 days of exposure. VZIG within 96 hours for immunocompromised.

Erythema Infectiosum (Fifth Disease)

EtiologyParvovirus B19

Generally affects school-age children (5-15 years). Transmitted via respiratory droplets [cite: 1].

Stages of Erythema Infectiosum Rash [cite: 1]

💡

Clinical Pearl: Period of Communicability

The patient is communicable during the initial flu-like illness (incubation 7 days). After a 7-day asymptomatic period, the rash appears. Once the rash appears, the patient is NO LONGER infectious [cite: 1].

Mumps, COVID-19, and Influenza

Mumps

EtiologyParamyxoviridae (Rubulavirus)

Incubation: 12-25 days. Shifts to older boys/men in post-vaccination era [cite: 1].

  • Characteristic Symptom: Parotitis (30-40%). Acute tender swelling. Starts postauricular → submandibular → preauricular. Displaces the ear outward/forward [cite: 1].
  • Complications:
    • Aseptic Meningitis (~50-60%, most common) [cite: 1].
    • Orchitis (20-66% in post-pubertal males; calcifications = sterility) [cite: 1].
    • Oophoritis (5%) [cite: 1].

COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2)

Children often asymptomatic or have mild flu-like symptoms. Unique features: loss of smell/taste, ARDS [cite: 1].

  • Transmission: Highest viral load day of symptom onset and up to 3 days after [cite: 1].
  • Diagnosis: RT-PCR (Best 3-5 days after exposure) [cite: 1].
  • Management: Supportive (O2 for hypoxia). Remdesivir gained EUA for hospitalized severe patients [cite: 1].
  • Imaging: Bilateral subpleural ground-glass opacities (Chest CT) [cite: 1].

Influenza

EtiologyOrthomyxoviridae (RNA)

3 types: A, B, C. Subtype A determined by Hemagglutinin and Neuraminidase [cite: 1].

Dengue & Chikungunya

Dengue Virus

EtiologyFlaviviridaeVector: Aedes aegypti

4 Serotypes. DEN-1 and DEN-2 most common in PH. Each serotype gives lifetime immunity to itself, but only 6mo cross-immunity to others [cite: 1].

⭐

Pathogenesis of Severe Dengue (Antibody-Dependent Enhancement)

Pre-existing non-neutralizing antibodies from a prior infection form complexes with the new serotype. These heterologous infectious complexes enter monocytes, where the virus replicates uncontrollably. Infected monocytes release vasoactive mediators causing vascular permeability (plasma leakage) and hemorrhage [cite: 1].

3 Phases of Dengue [cite: 1]

  1. Febrile (2-7 days): High fever, mild bleeding (tourniquet test+).
  2. Critical (Defervescence 3-7 days): Fever lyses. Patient can improve (no warning signs) OR deteriorate with warning signs within 24-48h leading to shock (DSS) or severe bleeding.
  3. Recovery (48-72 hrs later): Fluid reabsorption. "Isles of white in the sea of red" rash. WBC rises before platelets.

Classifications (WHO 2009) [cite: 1]

ClassificationCriteria
Probable Dengue Fever + 2 criteria (Nausea/vomiting, Rash, Aches, Tourniquet+, Leukopenia, Warning sign)
Warning Signs Clinical fluid accumulation, Mucosal bleed, Lethargy/restless, Liver >2cm, ↑ HCT with rapid ↓ Platelets
Severe Dengue Severe plasma leakage (Shock/Respiratory distress), Severe bleeding, Severe organ involvement (AST/ALT >1000, impaired CNS)
⚠️

Diagnostic Labs Timing

NS1 Ag: Requested Days 1-5 (virus present).
IgG/IgM: Requested > 5 days (antibodies develop late).
Hematocrit: Most important test for detecting plasma leakage (hemoconcentration) [cite: 1].

Chikungunya

EtiologyTogaviridae (Alphavirus)

Same mosquito vector as Dengue (Aedes) [cite: 1].

Clinical Hallmark [cite: 1]

  • Severe Polyarthralgia: Joint pain is debilitating, worse in the morning, migratory, affects ankles/wrists. May persist up to 1 year.
  • Saddle back pattern of pain.
  • Maculopapular rash, high fever.

Treatment: Paracetamol. Aspirin/Steroids avoided in acute stage. Must manage as Dengue until Dengue is ruled out [cite: 1].

Hand, Foot, & Mouth Disease and Mpox

Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD)

EtiologyEnteroviruses

Usually affects children <5 years old [cite: 1].

  • Coxsackievirus A16: Most common; mild self-limiting [cite: 1].
  • EV-71: Causes severe neurological complications (meningitis, encephalitis, myoclonic jerks) [cite: 1].

Clinical Features [cite: 1]

Discrete erythematous macules → papules → vesiculate → ulcerate centrally.

Oral lesions: Back of throat, fauces, soft palate. Causes refusal to feed.

Peripheral: Papulovesicular rash on palms, soles, knees, elbows, buttocks.

🧠

Exam Tip: Herpangina vs HFMD

If lesions are ONLY in the mouth/throat with fever, it's Herpangina. If you see oral lesions, ALWAYS inspect hands and feet for vesicles to diagnose HFMD [cite: 1].

Monkeypox (Mpox)

EtiologyOrthopoxvirus

Zoonotic. In children, usually acquired via close contact (lesions, contaminated items, animals). NOT airborne [cite: 1].

  • Rash Progression: Macules → Papules → Vesicles → Pustules → Crusting.
  • Key Diff: Mpox lesions are characteristically PAINFUL, whereas Varicella lesions are intensely PRURITIC [cite: 1].
  • Contagious until scabs have completely fallen off.
  • Treatment: Supportive. Tecovirimat for severe cases (not available in PH). JYNNEOS vaccine for PEP [cite: 1].

Pediatric Sepsis

Sepsis in pediatrics is a medical emergency with definitions still in evolution. It requires empiric diagnosis based on age-specific pathogens [cite: 1].

SIRS (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome) Criteria

Presence of ≥2 of the following criteria (one MUST be abnormal temperature or WBC) [cite: 1]:

  • Abnormal core temperature (<36°C or >38.5°C)
  • Abnormal HR (age-specific: >2 SD above normal, or <10th percentile if <1yr)
  • Raised RR (>2 SD above normal or need for vent)
  • Abnormal WBC (> or < normal for age, or >10% bands)

Definitions [cite: 1]

Infection Suspected or proven infection.
Sepsis SIRS in the presence of infection.
Severe Sepsis Sepsis + Cardiovascular dysfunction, OR ARDS, OR ≥2 non-CV organ dysfunctions.
Septic Shock Sepsis + Cardiovascular organ dysfunction (hypotension, vasoactive meds, impaired perfusion).

Organ Dysfunction Criteria (Highlights) [cite: 1]

  • Cardiovascular: BP <5th percentile, need for vasoactive drug (dopamine >5ug/kg/min), unexplained acidosis (base deficit >5), Lactate >2x upper limit, oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/hr, cap refill >5s. (Despite IV fluid bolus ≥40 mL/kg).
  • Neurologic: GCS ≤11 or drop >3 points.
  • Hematologic: Platelets <80,000 or 50% drop. INR > 2.
  • Renal: Creatinine 2x upper limit of normal.
💡

Diagnostic Labs in Sepsis

Blood Culture: Do ASAP, but DO NOT delay empiric antibiotics [cite: 1].

Serum Glucose: Very important. Children in sepsis often have hypoglycemia (depleted glycogen stores), sparking debate on using D5 vs isotonic fluids [cite: 1].

Lactate: Increased levels suggest septic shock (inadequate O2 delivery) [cite: 1].

CBC: Thrombocytopenia (<80k) suggests sepsis/coagulopathy [cite: 1].

Last-Minute Cram Sheet

"If you see X → Think Y" [cite: 1]

  • Fever drops, rash appears → Roseola (HHV-6)
  • Cough, Coryza, Conjunctivitis + Map-like rash → Measles (Rubeola)
  • Brawny desquamation → Measles
  • Forchheimer spots + enlarged neck nodes → Rubella
  • Slapped cheeks + Lacy rash → Erythema Infectiosum (Parvo B19)
  • Intensely pruritic, multi-stage vesicles → Varicella (Chickenpox)
  • Painful pustules, zoonotic exposure → Mpox
  • Saddleback fever + severe joint pain → Chikungunya
  • Tourniquet test + ↑ Hematocrit → Dengue
  • Fever lyses, patient deteriorates → Dengue Critical Phase
  • Parotitis + Orchitis → Mumps
  • Oral lesions + refusal to feed + foot vesicles → HFMD

Sepsis Rapid Review [cite: 1]

  • SIRS Criteria: Need ≥2 (temp, HR, RR, WBC). Temp or WBC MUST be one.
  • Severe Sepsis: Sepsis + CV dysfunction / ARDS / 2 organ dysfunctions.
  • Septic Shock: Sepsis + Cardiovascular dysfunction (needs vasoactives despite fluid).
  • Hypoglycemia: Common in sepsis due to depleted glycogen stores.
  • Culture: Get before antibiotics, but NEVER delay antibiotics for cultures.

Vaccine & Treatments [cite: 1]

  • Vit A in Measles: 50k (<6m), 100k (6-11m), 200k (>12m). Prevents blindness.
  • MMR: Live-attenuated. Given at 9m (Measles only), 15m, 4-6y in PH.
  • Acyclovir for Varicella: Only for >13y, immunocompromised, chronic steroids.
  • Remdesivir: EUA for hospitalized severe COVID-19.
  • Fluid Mgmt: Cornerstone of Dengue treatment.
