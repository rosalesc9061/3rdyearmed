Core Topic 1: Disorders of Absorption & Malabsorption

Absorption is divided into three distinct anatomical and physiological phases: Luminal Digestion, Small Intestinal Mucosal Digestion & Absorption, and Post-Mucosal Processing[cite: 1]. Any defect along these pathways produces characteristic malabsorption syndromes[cite: 1].

1. Luminal Bile Acid Deficiency & Enterohepatic Circulation ▼

Bile acids are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol, conjugated, and secreted into bile to emulsify fats and form micelles necessary for fat and fat-soluble vitamin absorption[cite: 2]. Defects in enterohepatic circulation severely deplete the intraduodenal bile acid pool[cite: 2]:

Physiologic Process Pathophysiologic Defect Clinical Disease Example
Synthesis Decreased parenchymal hepatic function Cirrhosis[cite: 2]
Biliary Secretion Altered canalicular excretion Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC)[cite: 2]
Conjugation Maintenance Bacterial deconjugation from luminal stasis Jejunal Diverticulosis / SIBO[cite: 2]
Reabsorption Abnormal terminal ileal mucosal function Crohn's Disease or Ileal Resection[cite: 2]
⭐ Must Know: Ileal Resection Cut-Off

Resection of ≥100 cm of the small intestine (specifically terminal ileum) results in massive bile acid losses that completely exceed the liver's maximal synthetic capacity[cite: 2]. Unabsorbed bile acids spill into the colon, inducing secretory diarrhea, while luminal fat micelle formation fails, causing severe steatorrhea[cite: 2, 4].

Treatment: Bile acid sequestrants (e.g., Cholestyramine, Colestipol, Colesevelam) bind unabsorbed bile acids in the lumen to prevent colonic mucosal irritation and watery diarrhea[cite: 2].

2. Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO) ▼

Etiology & Pathophysiology: Proliferation of colonic-type bacteria within the proximal small intestine resulting from bowel stasis[cite: 2]. Causes include scleroderma, chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction (CIPO), surgical blind loops (Billroth II, Roux-en-Y), small bowel strictures/fibrosis in Crohn's, and intestinal diverticulosis[cite: 2].

Bacterial titers normally increase progressively along the horizontal axis from the duodenum down to the ileum[cite: 2]. SIBO bacteria premature deconjugate bile acids, digest carbohydrates, and consume Vitamin B12[cite: 2].

Clinical Manifestations: Diarrhea with steatorrhea, abdominal distention, maldigestion, and megaloblastic anemia secondary to Vitamin B12 deficiency[cite: 2].

⚠️ Common Pitfall: SIBO Diagnostic Standard

Duodenal aspiration with quantitative bacterial culture (>105 CFU/mL) is the true GOLD STANDARD diagnostic, but it is invasive and technically difficult[cite: 2]. Breath hydrogen testing with lactulose is commonly performed clinically but suffers from high false-positive rates[cite: 2].

Treatment: Surgical correction of structural blind loops/strictures, combined with courses of non-absorbable broad-spectrum antibiotics or recurrent rotating antibiotics to prevent resistance[cite: 2].

3. Mucosal Disorders: Lactose Intolerance & Celiac Disease ▼

A. Lactose Intolerance

Deficiency of enterocyte brush border lactase enzyme[cite: 2]. Undigested lactose cannot be absorbed and exerts an osmotic effect, drawing water into the lumen while colonic bacteria ferment it into gas[cite: 2, 8].

Presentation: Osmotic diarrhea, crampy abdominal pain, flatulence, and bloating after dairy ingestion[cite: 2].

Diagnostic Procedure Protocol / Parameter Diagnostic Positive Result
Lactose Exclusion Diet Complete resolution of GI symptoms upon strict withdrawal of dairy[cite: 3]. Symptom resolution confirms clinical suspicion[cite: 3].
Lactose Tolerance Test Oral intake of 50g lactose; timed serum glucose measured every 90 minutes[cite: 3]. Serum glucose rise <20 mg/dL above baseline + symptoms of bloating/diarrhea[cite: 3].
Breath Hydrogen Test Standardized oral lactose load followed by end-expiratory breath hydrogen monitoring[cite: 3]. Breath hydrogen rise >20 ppm over baseline[cite: 3].

B. Celiac Disease (Gluten-Sensitive Enteropathy)

An immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by ingestion of gluten (found in wheat, rye, barley) in genetically susceptible individuals[cite: 3]. Characterized by autoantibodies to tissue transglutaminase (tTG)[cite: 3].

Histology: Primarily affects the proximal small intestine (duodenum/jejunum), demonstrating classic tri-fold architecture: villous atrophy (blunting), crypt hyperplasia, and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs)[cite: 3].

💡 High-Yield Disease Associations

Celiac disease is strongly associated with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM), Autoimmune Thyroiditis, Dermatitis Herpetiformis (pruritic papulovesicular skin lesions), Down syndrome, and Turner syndrome[cite: 3].

Diagnostics: IgA Tissue Transglutaminase antibody (tTG-IgA) is the screening test of choice[cite: 3]. Confirmation requires endoscopic small intestinal mucosal biopsy[cite: 3].

Treatment: Strict lifelong Gluten-Free Diet (GFD)[cite: 3]. Avoid wheat, rye, barley, oats, processed goods, and standard breads/pastas[cite: 3].

4. Short Bowel Syndrome & Protein-Losing Enteropathy (PLE) ▼

A. Short Bowel Syndrome (SBS)

Occurs when small bowel length is significantly reduced (<200 cm of functional remaining small intestine) due to extensive surgical resection[cite: 3].

Severe SBS: Remaining small intestine <100 cm without colon results in complete intestinal failure, requiring lifelong Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)[cite: 3].

Pharmacotherapy: Teduglutide (a GLP-2 analog) is specifically approved to enhance mucosal adaptation and reduce parenteral nutrition requirements[cite: 3].

Monitoring: Patients are at high risk for osteoporosis due to severe malabsorption of dietary Calcium and Vitamin D; monitor closely with baseline and serial DEXA scans and serum calcium[cite: 3].

B. Protein-Losing Enteropathy (PLE)

Characterized by excess protein loss into the GI tract, presenting with profound hypoproteinemia and generalized peripheral edema in the complete absence of impaired hepatic protein synthesis (cirrhosis) or renal protein loss (nephrotic syndrome)[cite: 3, 4].

Mucosal Mechanism Pathophysiologic Process Prototypic Disease Examples
Mucosal Ulceration Direct inflammatory protein exudation across broken mucosa Ulcerative Colitis, peptic ulceration[cite: 3]
Non-Ulcerated Permeability Defect Altered epithelial tight-junction permeability without frank ulceration Celiac disease, Ménétrier's disease, hypertrophic gastropathy[cite: 3]
Lymphatic Dysfunction / High Pressure Lymphatic obstruction or elevated central venous pressure causing protein exudation Intestinal lymphangiectasia, Fontan physiology, Constrictive Pericarditis, CHF[cite: 3]
🧠 Diagnostic Clue: Serologic Profile of PLE

PLE reduces BOTH serum albumin AND globulin levels simultaneously[cite: 3, 4]. If ONLY serum albumin is decreased, you MUST evaluate for liver failure or renal protein wastage[cite: 4]. Diagnostic confirmation: Elevated 24-hour stool alpha-1 antitrypsin clearance[cite: 3, 4].

5. Diagnostic Workup & Small Bowel Mucosal Biopsies ▼

Stool Osmotic Gap & Diarrhea Differentiation

Stool Osmotic Gap is calculated as: 290 - 2 × (Stool Na+ + Stool K+)[cite: 4].

  • Osmotic Diarrhea (>50–100 mOsm/kg): Caused by unabsorbed, non-absorbable solutes (e.g., lactose) pulling fluid into lumen. Improves or resolves with fasting[cite: 4].
  • Secretory Diarrhea (<50 mOsm/kg): Caused by active mucosal electrolyte secretion (e.g., bacterial toxins, unabsorbed fatty acids/bile acids stimulating colonic mucosa). Persists even during fasting[cite: 4].

Core Topic 2: Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

IBD comprises chronic, idiopathic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract, predominantly divided into Ulcerative Colitis (UC) and Crohn's Disease (CD)[cite: 4]. Pathophysiology involves immune dysregulation triggered by environmental factors in genetically susceptible hosts with disrupted gut microbiota[cite: 4, 5].

⭐ Epidemiologic Risk Factors (UC vs. CD)
  • Age of Onset: Bimodal distribution (peaks at 2nd–4th decades and 7th–9th decades)[cite: 5].
  • Smoking: Protective in Ulcerative Colitis; Worsens/triggers Crohn's Disease[cite: 5].
  • Appendectomy: Protective in UC (13–26% risk reduction); No protective effect in CD[cite: 5].
  • Oral Contraceptive Pills (OCPs): Increased risk in CD (Hazard Ratio 2.82); No increased risk in UC[cite: 5].
  • Twin Concordance: Monozygotic twins show 38–58% concordance in CD vs. 6–18% in UC[cite: 5].
⚠️ Diagnostic Pitfall: Ileocecal Mass (TB vs. CD)

In endemic areas (e.g., Philippines), an ileocecal mass with granulomatous inflammation presents a major diagnostic dilemma[cite: 6]:

  • Intestinal Tuberculosis: Demonstrates caseating granulomas, (+) GeneXpert MTB/RIF, and (+) QuantiFERON-TB Gold[cite: 6].
  • Crohn's Disease: Demonstrates non-caseating granulomas, (-) GeneXpert, and (-) QuantiFERON[cite: 5, 6].

Staging & Extent Classification (Montreal & Vienna)

Montreal Classification of Ulcerative Colitis

Extent:

  • E1 (Ulcerative Proctitis): Limited strictly to rectum (30% at presentation)[cite: 5, 6].
  • E2 (Left-Sided UC): Involvement up to splenic flexure (40%)[cite: 5, 6].
  • E3 (Pancolitis): Extends proximal to splenic flexure (30%)[cite: 5, 6].

Severity: S0 (remission), S1 (Mild: ≤4 stools/day, no systemic toxicity, normal ESR), S2 (Moderate: ≥4 stools/day, minimal toxicity), S3 (Severe: ≥6 bloody stools/day, HR ≥90 bpm, Temp ≥37.5°C, Hb <10.5 g/dL, ESR ≥30 mm/h)[cite: 6].

Montreal / Vienna Classification of Crohn's Disease

Age at Diagnosis: A1 (<16 yrs), A2 (17–40 yrs), A3 (>40 yrs)[cite: 6].

Location: L1 (Ileal), L2 (Colonic), L3 (Ileocolonic), L4 (Isolated Upper GI modifier)[cite: 6].

Behavior: B1 (Non-stricturing, non-penetrating), B2 (Stricturing), B3 (Penetrating), p (Perianal modifier)[cite: 6].

Medical & Surgical Management of IBD

Management utilizes two therapeutic strategies: Step-Up Approach (starting with low-cost/mild agents and progressing) vs. Top-Down Approach (early administration of biologics/immunosuppressives to rapidly arrest mucosal damage). Most gastroenterologists favor Top-Down in moderate-to-severe disease[cite: 7].

Disease State Ulcerative Colitis (UC) Management Crohn's Disease (CD) Management
Mild Disease 5-ASA (Mesalamine/Sulfasalazine oral & rectal), topical corticosteroids[cite: 6, 7, 16]. Ileocecal Budesonide (controlled-release), 5-ASA (limited efficacy), antibiotics for perianal disease[cite: 6, 7, 16].
Moderate Disease Oral systemic corticosteroids for induction; Azathioprine or 6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP) for maintenance[cite: 6, 7, 16]. Systemic corticosteroids for induction; Azathioprine, 6-MP, or Methotrexate (MTX) for maintenance[cite: 6, 7, 16].
Severe / Refractory IV Corticosteroids; if non-responsive → IV Infliximab or IV Cyclosporine[cite: 6, 7, 16]. IV Corticosteroids; Biologics (Infliximab, Adalimumab, Vedolizumab, Ustekinumab)[cite: 6, 7, 16].
Fulminant / Toxic Megacolon Bowel rest, IV fluids, high-dose IV steroids; urgent Colectomy if no response in 24–72 hours[cite: 6, 7, 16]. Emergency surgery for perforation, abscess, or unresolving mechanical obstruction[cite: 6, 7, 16].
Targeted Biologics & Novel Agents Infliximab, Adalimumab, Golimumab, Vedolizumab, Ustekinumab, Tofacitinib (JAK inhibitor)[cite: 6, 16]. Infliximab, Adalimumab, Certolizumab, Vedolizumab, Ustekinumab, Upadacitinib (JAK1), Risankizumab (IL-23)[cite: 6, 16].
💡 Surgical Curability Paradigm

Ulcerative Colitis: Total Proctocolectomy with Ileal Pouch-Anal Anastomosis (IPAA) is CURATIVE, eliminating all colonic disease and cancer risk[cite: 5, 6, 16].
Crohn's Disease: Surgery is NOT CURATIVE. Disease frequently recurs at surgical anastomosis sites; surgery is strictly reserved for complications (obstruction, abscess, refractory fistulae)[cite: 5, 6, 16].

Core Topic 3: Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

IBS is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by abdominal pain and altered bowel habits in the total absence of detectable structural, inflammatory, or metabolic abnormalities[cite: 7].

⭐ Core Diagnostic Principle

Unlike IBD, IBS exhibits NO tissue damage, mucosal inflammation, ulceration, or blood in the stool[cite: 5, 7]. It affects 10–20% of the population, with a 2–3:1 female predominance, typically presenting before age 45[cite: 7].

Multifactorial Pathophysiology

Symptoms arise from complex dysregulation of the gut-brain axis involving: altered GI motility, visceral hypersensitivity (lowered pain threshold for intestinal distention), gut microbiome dysbiosis, altered HPA-axis autonomic signaling, bile acid malabsorption, and SERT gene polymorphisms[cite: 7].

Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria for IBS

Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria

Recurrent abdominal pain, on average, at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, associated with ≥2 of the following criteria[cite: 7]:

  1. Related to defecation (relieved or exacerbated)[cite: 7].
  2. Associated with a change in the frequency of stool[cite: 7].
  3. Associated with a change in the form (appearance) of stool[cite: 7].

Note: Criteria must be fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months prior to diagnosis[cite: 7, 8]. Diagnosis CANNOT be made during a first consultation if symptoms have lasted only 1 month[cite: 8]!

⚠️ Alarm Symptoms ("Red Flags") Requiring Urgent Workup

The presence of ANY alarm symptom excludes uncomplicated IBS and mandates prompt colonoscopy and laboratory evaluation[cite: 8]:

  • Unintentional weight loss[cite: 8]
  • Rectal bleeding / Hematochezia[cite: 8]
  • Unexplained Anemia[cite: 8]
  • Nocturnal diarrhea (awakens patient)[cite: 8]
  • Onset of symptoms after age 50[cite: 8]
  • Fever or systemic signs[cite: 8]
  • Persistent diarrhea while fasting[cite: 4, 8]
  • Family history of CRC or IBD[cite: 8, 13]
  • Steatorrhea / Palpable mass[cite: 2, 8]

FODMAPs & Dietary / Pharmacologic Management

Low FODMAP Diet: Restricts Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols[cite: 8]. Unabsorbed FODMAPs exert an osmotic fluid shift and undergo rapid colonic bacterial fermentation, generating gas and intestinal distention[cite: 8]. Restrict for 2–6 weeks, then gradually reintroduce to identify specific triggers[cite: 8].

Subtype / Target Primary Clinical Features First-Line & Targeted Pharmacotherapy
IBS-C (Constipation) Hard/infrequent stools, straining, incomplete evacuation[cite: 7] Soluble fiber bulking agents, Osmotic laxatives (PEG), Prucalopride (5-HT4 receptor agonist prokinetic), Lubiprostone/Linaclotide secretagogues[cite: 8].
IBS-D (Diarrhea) Frequent loose stools, fecal urgency, mucus passage[cite: 7] Loperamide, Bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine), Antispasmodics (dicyclomine), Rifaximin (non-absorbable antibiotic for dysbiosis)[cite: 8].
Refractory Pain / Neuropathic Persistent crampy visceral pain[cite: 7] Low-dose Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs - e.g., Amitriptyline) or SSRIs[cite: 8].

Core Topic 4: Diverticular Disorders

Diverticular disorders range from congenital true diverticula (Meckel's) to acquired colonic pseudodiverticula, presenting with complications of hemorrhage or acute inflammation[cite: 8, 9].

Meckel's Diverticulum

The most common congenital anomaly of the GI tract (2–3% of population), caused by incomplete obliteration of the vitelline duct (omphalomesenteric duct)[cite: 8].

💡 Classic "Rule of 2s"
  • 2% prevalence in general population[cite: 8]
  • Located within 2 feet (100 cm) of the ileocecal valve[cite: 8]
  • Measures 2 inches (1–10 cm) in length[cite: 8]
  • Contains 2 types of ectopic tissue: Gastric (most common) & Pancreatic[cite: 8]
  • Presents clinically before 2 years of age[cite: 8]

Clinical Presentation: In pediatrics, presents as painless, massive lower GI bleeding (hematochezia) secondary to ileal ulceration caused by acid secretion from ectopic gastric mucosa[cite: 8]. In adults, presents as intestinal obstruction, intussusception, or diverticulitis[cite: 8].

Diagnosis: Technetium-99m pertechnetate scintigraphy ("Meckel Scan") binds selectively to ectopic gastric mucosa[cite: 8]. Angiography is indicated if bleeding is active[cite: 8].

Treatment: Surgical resection of affected bowel segment[cite: 8].

Colonic Diverticular Disease

Herniations through the colonic wall[cite: 8]. A True Diverticulum involves all layers of the bowel wall[cite: 8]. A False Diverticulum (Pseudodiverticulum) is a protrusion of mucosa and submucosa through the muscularis propria at weak points where nutrient arteries (vasa recta) penetrate[cite: 8].

Epidemiology: Affects >50% of people >60 years old[cite: 8]. Western populations: predominantly left-sided (sigmoid)[cite: 8, 9]. Asians: 70% are right-sided[cite: 8].

⭐ Diverticular Bleeding vs. Diverticulitis

Diverticular Bleeding: Most common cause of massive lower GI bleeding in patients >60 yrs[cite: 8, 9]. PAINLESS hematochezia[cite: 8, 9]. 80% stop spontaneously[cite: 9].
Acute Diverticulitis: Microperforation and pericolic inflammation[cite: 9]. PAINFUL LLQ abdominal pain (RLQ in Asians), fever, leukocytosis[cite: 8, 9].

Hinchey Staging & Operative Management of Acute Diverticulitis

⚠️ Diagnostic Contraindication

Colonoscopy is STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED during acute diverticulitis due to high risk of bowel perforation from air insufflation[cite: 9]. Abdominal CT scan with contrast is the diagnostic imaging modality of choice (reveals colonic wall >4mm, pericolic fat stranding, fluid/abscess)[cite: 9].

Hinchey Stage Pathologic Presentation / Description Recommended Management / Surgical Procedure
Stage Ia Pericolic phlegmon or localized inflammation[cite: 9] Outpatient/inpatient medical therapy with broad-spectrum oral/IV antibiotics[cite: 9].
Stage Ib Pericolic abscess[cite: 9] Percutaneous CT-guided abscess drainage followed by elective resection[cite: 9].
Stage II Distant pelvic, retroperitoneal, or abdominal abscess[cite: 9] Percutaneous CT-guided drainage + delayed laparoscopic/open resection ± ostomy[cite: 9].
Stage III Generalized purulent peritonitis (non-communicating perforation)[cite: 9] Laparoscopic washout & drainage OR Hartmann's Procedure (resection + end colostomy)[cite: 9].
Stage IV Generalized fecal peritonitis (communicating perforation with continuous fecal leak)[cite: 9] Emergent Hartmann's Procedure (resection of diseased segment, distal closure, proximal colostomy)[cite: 9].

Core Topic 5: Common Diseases of the Anorectum

1. Rectal Prolapse (Procidentia) & Sitzmark Study ▼

Definition: Circumferential, full-thickness protrusion of the rectal wall through the anal orifice[cite: 9]. Most common in females >60 years (6:1 F:M ratio)[cite: 9]. Associated with pelvic floor laxity, redundant sigmoid colon, anismus, and colonic inertia[cite: 9, 10].

💡 Sitzmark Transit Study (Radiopaque Marker Study)

Used to evaluate constipation and colonic motility prior to prolapse surgery[cite: 10]:

  1. Patient swallows 1 capsule containing 24 radiopaque markers on Day 0[cite: 10].
  2. Plain abdominal X-ray taken on Day 5[cite: 10].
  3. Normal: >80% of markers expelled by Day 5[cite: 10].
  4. Delayed Transit (>20% retained):
    • Markers scattered throughout colon: Colonic Inertia (Slow-transit constipation)[cite: 10].
    • Markers accumulated in rectosigmoid: Outlet Obstruction / Defecatory Disorder (Anismus)[cite: 10].

Treatment: Surgical correction is mainstay (Transabdominal repair has lower recurrence; Transperineal repair for elderly/fragile patients)[cite: 10].

2. Fecal Incontinence ▼

Involuntary passage of fecal material for at least 1 month[cite: 10]. Most common in parous females >65 years due to obstetric injury (pudendal nerve stretching or anal sphincter tearing)[cite: 10].

Diagnostics: Rectal exam, Anorectal Manometry (documents sphincter pressures), and Endoanal Ultrasound (visualizes structural sphincter tears)[cite: 10].

Management: Bulking agents (fiber, loperamide), Biofeedback physical therapy, and Overlapping Sphincteroplasty (GOLD STANDARD surgical repair)[cite: 10].

3. Hemorrhoidal Disease ▼

Symptomatic prolapse or engorgement of normal hemorrhoidal vascular cushions (located at Left Lateral, Right Anterior, Right Posterior complexes)[cite: 10].

  • External Hemorrhoids: Below dentate line, covered by squamous epithelium; painful when thrombosed[cite: 10].
  • Internal Hemorrhoids: Above dentate line, covered by mucosa/transitional epithelium; painless bleeding and prolapse[cite: 10].
Internal Stage Clinical Classification & Presentation Recommended Therapeutic Strategy
Stage I Vascular enlargement with painless bleeding; NO prolapse[cite: 10] Fiber supplementation, hydrocortisone suppositories, sclerotherapy, infrared coagulation[cite: 10].
Stage II Prolapse with defecation but reduces spontaneously[cite: 10] Fiber, hydrocortisone, sclerotherapy, rubber band ligation (RBL)[cite: 10, 11].
Stage III Prolapse requiring manual reduction[cite: 10] Fiber, Rubber Band Ligation (RBL), or Operative Hemorrhoidectomy[cite: 10, 11].
Stage IV Irreducible prolapse; chronic discomfort/bleeding[cite: 10] Fiber, hydrocortisone suppositories, Operative Hemorrhoidectomy[cite: 10, 11].
4. Anorectal Abscess, Fistula-in-Ano & Anal Fissure ▼

A. Anorectal Abscess

Infection originating from obstructed anal crypt glands[cite: 11]. Male-to-female ratio 3:1[cite: 11]. Locations: Perianal (40–50%, MOST COMMON), Ischiorectal (20–50%), Intersphincteric (2–5%), Supralevator (2.5%)[cite: 11].

⭐ Treatment Standard

Prompt Incision and Drainage (I&D) is the GOLD STANDARD[cite: 11]. Antibiotics have a limited role except in immunocompromised patients, diabetics, or those with prosthetic valves/joints[cite: 11].

B. Fistula-in-Ano

Chronic inflammatory tract connecting an internal opening in the anal canal to an external skin opening, usually secondary to drained perianal abscesses[cite: 11]. Classifications: Intersphincteric (70%, MOST COMMON), Trans-sphincteric, Suprasphincteric (5%), Extrasphincteric (5%)[cite: 11]. Mainstay treatment is Fistulotomy[cite: 11].

C. Anal Fissure

Linear mucosal tear in the distal anal canal, causing severe sharp pain during defecation and bright red bleeding[cite: 11].

⚠️ High-Yield Location Pitfall

Classic trauma fissures occur in the Posterior Midline (>90%) or Anterior Midline[cite: 11].
An OFF-MIDLINE / LATERAL ANAL FISSURE is abnormal and MANDATES evaluation for underlying systemic disease: Tuberculosis, Syphilis, Crohn's Disease, HIV/AIDS, or Malignancy[cite: 11, 15]!

Core Topic 6: Mesenteric Ischemia, Malignancy & Obstruction

1. Acute vs. Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia (AMI vs. CMI) ▼

Perfusion failure to the splanchnic bed resulting in bowel hypoxia and necrosis. Overall mortality remains high (50–80%)[cite: 11].

⭐ Cardinal Presentation of Acute Mesenteric Ischemia

The hallmark of AMI is sudden, severe abdominal pain DISPROPORTIONATE to physical exam findings[cite: 11]. Early on, the abdomen remains completely soft and non-tender despite excruciating pain[cite: 11]. Peritoneal signs (rigidity, guarding) develop only after frank bowel infarction occurs[cite: 11, 15]. Urgent CT Angiography (CTA) is the diagnostic test of choice[cite: 11].

Feature Acute Mesenteric Ischemia (AMI) Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia (CMI)
Onset & Duration Sudden, catastrophic (hours)[cite: 11] Gradual, recurrent (months to years)[cite: 11]
Pain Character Severe, constant, disproportionate to exam[cite: 11] Post-prandial dull crampy pain ("intestinal angina")[cite: 11]
Etiology & Etiopathogenesis SMA Embolism (50%), Thrombosis, NOMI, MVT[cite: 11] Atherosclerotic narrowing of ≥2 splanchnic vessels[cite: 11]
Behavioral Clues Cardioembolic risk (AFib, post-MI)[cite: 11, 15] Severe weight loss, Sitophobia (fear of eating)[cite: 11]
Definitive Management Emergency Laparotomy & revascularization[cite: 11, 15] Elective revascularization (stenting or bypass)[cite: 11]

Nonocclusive Mesenteric Ischemia (NOMI) vs. Mesenteric Venous Thrombosis (MVT)

  • NOMI: Functional splanchnic vasospasm and hypoperfusion in critically ill patients (shock, sepsis, high-dose vasopressors, CHF) without vascular occlusion[cite: 11, 12]. Management: Restore perfusion, stop vasopressors, intra-arterial papaverine vasodilators[cite: 12, 18]. Anticoagulation is NOT indicated[cite: 12].
  • MVT: Venous occlusion of SMV/portal vein in hypercoagulable states, cirrhosis, pancreatitis, or OCP use[cite: 11, 12]. Management: Immediate systemic Anticoagulation (LMWH → Warfarin/DOAC) to restore venous outflow[cite: 12, 18].
2. Colorectal Cancer (CRC) Screening & Surveillance ▼

CRC arises via the Adenoma-Carcinoma Sequence: Normal mucosa → Adenomatous polyp → Severe dysplasia (submucosa) → Adenocarcinoma (invades muscularis propria) → Invasive Cancer (invades serosa)[cite: 12, 13]. Barium enema classically reveals an "apple-core" annular constricting lesion[cite: 12].

💡 Screening Guidelines & Family History Calculation
  • Average Risk Patients: Start screening at age 45 (conditional) or age 50–75 (strong recommendation)[cite: 13]. Stop at age 75 if life expectancy <5 yrs[cite: 13].
  • First-Degree Relative with CRC or Advanced Polyp: Start screening at age 40 OR 10 years earlier than the age of the youngest affected relative at diagnosis (whichever is younger)[cite: 13]!
    • Example A: Relative diagnosed at age 55 → Start screening at age 40[cite: 13].
    • Example B: Relative diagnosed at age 45 → Start screening at age 35[cite: 13]!

Post-Polypectomy Surveillance Intervals

Risk Category Colonoscopic Findings at Screening Recommended Surveillance Interval
Low Risk 1–2 small tubular adenomas (<1 cm)[cite: 15] Repeat Colonoscopy in 5 years[cite: 14, 15]
Intermediate Risk 3–4 adenomas OR at least 1 adenoma ≥1 cm[cite: 15] Repeat Colonoscopy in 3 years[cite: 15]
High Risk ≥5 adenomas OR ≥3 adenomas with at least 1 ≥1 cm[cite: 15] Repeat Colonoscopy in 1 year[cite: 15]
3. Paralytic Ileus vs. Mechanical Intestinal Obstruction ▼

Ileus: Functional loss of peristalsis without physical lumen occlusion[cite: 13]. Common in post-op abdominal surgery, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, sepsis, or opiate use[cite: 13]. Treated conservatively (wait 3–5 days, correct electrolytes, NGT decompression)[cite: 14].

Etiology of Acute Mechanical Intestinal Obstruction

Etiology / Cause Overall Incidence (%) High-Yield Clinical Pearls
Postoperative Adhesions >50% (MOST COMMON)[cite: 13, 14] Can occur following surgeries performed years or even decades prior[cite: 13, 14].
Neoplasms ~20%[cite: 14] Primary colonic adenocarcinoma or metastatic carcinomatosis[cite: 13, 14].
Hernias ~10%[cite: 14] Ventral, inguinal, or internal hernias; high risk of strangulation[cite: 13, 14].
IBD / Strictures ~5%[cite: 14] Fibrotic strictures secondary to Crohn's disease or diverticulitis[cite: 13, 14].
Volvulus / Intussusception <15%[cite: 14] Lead point in adult intussusception is almost always a tumor/polyp[cite: 13].
🧠 Complete vs. Partial Obstruction Management

Complete Mechanical Obstruction: MANDATES IMMEDIATE SURGICAL INTERVENTION to prevent bowel strangulation and gangrene[cite: 14].
Partial Mechanical Obstruction: Trial of conservative medical management (NGT decompression, IV fluids, serial exams)[cite: 14]. Avoid prolonged fasting to support mucosal healing[cite: 14].
