Module Summary

This comprehensive interactive reviewer covers core surgical concepts of the esophagus, stomach, upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB), and associated benign and malignant conditions. It is specifically structured for 3rd-year medical students taking Surgery II Long Examinations and preparing for the Physician Licensure Examination (PLE).

💡 High-Yield Board Exam Objectives
  • Master esophageal anatomy, vascular supply, non-contiguous (skip) lymphatic spread, and 4 major anatomical constrictions.
  • Distinguish alkali (liquefaction) vs. acidic (coagulation) caustic injuries, Zargar endoscopic grading, diagnostic golden window (24–48h), and management phases.
  • Differentiate Types I–IV Hiatal Hernias and surgical indications for Nissen Fundoplication.
  • Compare esophageal diverticula: Zenker's, Parabronchial, and Epiphrenic.
  • Differentiate SCCA vs. Adenocarcinoma of the esophagus and master surgical resections (Ivor Lewis, McKeown, Left Thoracoabdominal, Transhiatal).
  • Understand stomach anatomy, vagal supply (LaRp rule), PUD pathophysiology, Modified Johnson Classification, Forrest Classification, and surgical vagotomies/resections.
  • Approach UGIB systematically and manage non-variceal causes (Mallory-Weiss, Dieulafoy's, AVMs, Erosive Esophagitis).
  • Stage and manage Gastric Adenocarcinoma (D2 Lymphadenectomy, Billroth I vs. Roux-en-Y) and Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GIST).

Esophagus Clinical Anatomy, Hernia & Diverticula

1. Anatomical Divisions & Measurements

The esophagus is a muscular, tubular structure spanning from the cricoid cartilage to the stomach. Its length varies by sex and habitus:

  • Females: ~35–36 cm from incisors
  • Males: ~39–40 cm from incisors
Section Anatomical Boundaries Key Features
Cervical Esophagus Cricopharyngeus muscle to thoracic inlet Proximal third; striated muscle
Upper Thoracic Esophagus Thoracic inlet to carina Subdivision of middle third
Mid Thoracic Esophagus Carina to inferior pulmonary vein Subdivision of middle third
Lower Thoracic Esophagus Inferior pulmonary vein to GEJ Subdivision of middle third
Abdominal Esophagus GEJ into abdominal cavity Distal third; smooth muscle

⭐ MUST KNOW: Clinical Significance of Esophageal Narrowings
  • Common sites where swallowed foreign bodies become lodged.
  • Primary sites for stricture development post-instrumentation or caustic ingestion.
  • Predilection sites for esophageal carcinoma development due to acid stasis and bacterial pooling.
  • Anatomical hurdles during blind instrumentation, NGT insertion, or endoscopy.

2. Vascular Supply & Lymphatic Drainage

Section Arterial Supply Venous Drainage
Cervical Esophageal branch of Inferior Thyroid Artery (from Subclavian) Inferior Thyroid Veins
Thoracic Aortic esophageal arteries & Bronchial Artery branches Azygous and Hemiazygous Veins
Abdominal Ascending branch of Left Gastric Artery & Inf Phrenic Arteries Left Gastric (Coronary) Vein (Portal System)
💡 Clinical Pearl: Unique Lymphatic Pattern & Skip Metastasis

Unlike rest of the GI tract where lymphatics reside mainly in the submucosa, the esophagus has rich lymphatic capillaries extending up into the lamina propria. Lymphatic drainage is bidirectional (cephalad and caudad) and interconnected. Tumors can metastasize longitudinally in the submucosa, producing skip metastases 8–10 cm away from the primary tumor bed without involving intermediate nodes. This necessitates wide surgical resection margins and extensive lymphadenectomy.

3. Hiatal Hernia

Protrusion of abdominal viscera through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm.

Type Name Anatomical Configuration Incidence Clinical Features & Mgmt
Type I Sliding Hernia GEJ migrates above the diaphragm 85% Associated with GERD. Medical tx (PPIs); surgery if refractory.
Type II Rolling / Paraesophageal GEJ in normal position; fundus herniates alongside 4.5% Risk of volvulus, strangulation, perforation. Elective surgery advised.
Type III Combined Sliding & Rolling Both GEJ and fundus herniate above diaphragm 0.4% GERD symptoms + risk of strangulation. Surgical repair.
Type IV Complex / Organ-containing Presence of other organs (colon, spleen, small bowel) in chest 0.1% High risk of obstruction/strangulation. Surgical repair.
🧠 Surgical Procedure of Choice: Nissen Fundoplication

360-degree gastric wrap around the distal esophagus. Objectives: (1) Restore intra-abdominal esophageal segment length, (2) Repair widened diaphragmatic hiatus (crural closure +/- mesh), (3) Recreate acute esophagogastric Angle of His to act as an anti-reflux valve.

Caustic Injuries of the Esophagus & Stomach

1. Etiology & Pathophysiology: Alkali vs. Acidic Agents

Feature Alkali Agents (pH > 7) Acidic Agents (pH < 7)
Epidemiology Most common in Western countries Most common in Asian countries
Examples Lye (NaOH, KOH), drain/oven cleaners, disc batteries Toilet bowl cleaners (sulfuric, HCl), anti-rust, pool chemicals
Taste / Sensation Odorless & tasteless (ingested in large volumes) Immediate burning pain (rapidly expelled/vomited)
Necrosis Mechanism LIQUEFACTION Necrosis
• Saponification of fats & protein solubilization
• Deep penetration into muscular layers
• Severe damage occurs within minutes		 COAGULATION Necrosis
• Forms a protective superficial coagulum/eschar
• Limits depth of penetration into esophageal wall
• Eschar sloughs in 3–4 days
Primary Target Organ Oropharynx, Hypopharynx, Esophagus Stomach (esophageal squamous mucosa resistant)
• Pyloric & antral spasm leads to gastric pooling
Long-Term Complications Esophageal Strictures (develops in 2–4 weeks) Gastric Outlet Obstruction (GOO)

2. Endoscopic Staging: Zargar Classification

⭐ Endoscopy Golden Rule

Upper GI Endoscopy (EGD) is the gold standard. Perform strictly within 24 to 48 hours post-ingestion. STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED between Days 5–15 because esophageal tissues reach maximum friability and lowest tensile strength, leading to catastrophic instrumentation perforation!

Grade Endoscopic Findings Clinical Implication
Grade 0Normal mucosal appearanceNo injury; safe discharge
Grade 1Edema and hyperemia of mucosaBenign course; early oral feeds
Grade 2AFriability, blisters, exudates, hemorrhages, superficial ulcerationsLow stricture risk
Grade 2BGrade 2A + deep discrete or circumferential ulcerationsHigh risk of stricture formation
Grade 3ASmall scattered areas of multiple ulceration & brownish-black/gray necrosisHigh stricture & perforation risk
Grade 3BExtensive mucosal necrosisVery high mortality; urgent surgical review

3. 3-Phase Management Algorithm

FIGURE 2: Phases of Caustic Ingestion Management
⚠️ COMMON PITFALLS & CONTRAINDICATIONS
  • NEVER insert an NGT blindly: Risk of puncturing necrotic, friable esophageal tissues. NGT must only be placed under direct endoscopic guidance.
  • NEVER induce vomiting or give emetics: Re-exposes esophageal and oropharyngeal mucosa to the corrosive agent ("double injury").
  • NEVER give neutralizing agents (alkali for acid, or acid for alkali): Neutralization reaction generates extreme exothermic heat, resulting in massive thermal injuries and tissue destruction.
  • NEVER perform endoscopy between Days 5–15 post-ingestion.

Malignant Diseases of the Esophagus

1. Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) vs. Adenocarcinoma

Feature Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) Adenocarcinoma
Major Risk Factors Alcohol and Tobacco, achalasia, caustic strictures GERD, Barrett's Esophagus, High BMI / Obesity
Predominant Location Upper and Middle thirds of esophagus Distal third and GEJ
Geographic Pattern More common in Eastern countries / Asia More common in Western countries
Median Age & Sex 50s; Males > Females 60s; Males > Females
Lymph Node Spread Earlier lymph node spread Early regional lymph node spread
Prognosis Poorer prognosis overall Poor prognosis

2. Metaplasia-Dysplasia-Carcinoma Sequence (Barrett's Esophagus)

Chronic acid reflux transforms normal Stratified Squamous Epithelium into Metaplastic Columnar Epithelium (Barrett's Esophagus).

Normal Squamous Mucosa ➔ Reflux Esophagitis ➔ Barrett's (No Dysplasia) ➔ Low-Grade Dysplasia ➔ High-Grade Dysplasia ➔ Invasive Adenocarcinoma

3. AJCC Clinical Staging (cTNM) Summary

  • Tis: High-Grade Dysplasia (Carcinoma in situ)
  • T1a: Tumor invades lamina propria or muscularis mucosae
  • T1b: Tumor invades submucosa (EMR no longer indicated due to high LN metastasis risk)
  • T2: Tumor invades muscularis propria
  • T3: Tumor invades adventitia / paraesophageal tissues
  • T4a: Invades pleura, pericardium, diaphragm (resectable)
  • T4b: Invades adjacent structures like aorta, trachea, vertebral body (unresectable)
  • N Staging: N0 (0), N1 (1–2 nodes), N2 (3–6 nodes), N3 (7+ nodes)

4. Surgical Management Modalities

Surgical Approach Incisions / Technique Indications & Features
Endoscopic Mucosal Resection (EMR) Transoral endoscopic mucosal lift & snare excision Limited disease: T1a N0 M0, <3 cm size, non-circumferential
Ivor Lewis Esophagogastrectomy 2 Incisions: Right Thoracotomy + Laparotomy Mid-to-distal esophageal and GEJ tumors. Anastomosis in chest.
McKeown Esophagogastrectomy 3 Incisions: Cervical + Thoracic + Abdominal Upper/mid-thoracic tumors or SCC. Anastomosis in the neck.
Left Thoracoabdominal Approach Single continuous thoracoabdominal incision Distal esophageal and GEJ tumors
Transhiatal Esophagectomy 2 Incisions: Abdominal + Left Cervical (NO thoracotomy) Distal esophageal tumors in patients with high pulmonary risk. Disadvantage: Blind mediastinal dissection.

Stomach Anatomy & Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

1. Clinical Anatomy, Vasculature & Innervation

The stomach is divided into 5 regions: Cardia, Fundus, Body, Antrum, and Pylorus.

🧠 High-Yield Mnemonic: Vagal Innervation

LaRp: Left vagus nerve supplies the Anterior gastric wall; Right vagus nerve supplies the Posterior gastric wall.

5. Surgical Options for Refractory PUD

  • Antrectomy + Truncal Vagotomy: Lowest recurrence rate (<1%), but highest morbidity/mortality. Resects gastrin-producing antrum and cuts acid-stimulating vagus nerves.
  • Highly Selective Vagotomy (Parietal Cell Vagotomy): Cuts only branches supplying acid-secreting body/fundus. Preserves motor branches to antrum/pylorus (Nerve of Latarjet). Lowest side effects, but highest recurrence rate (~10-15%).
  • Heineke-Mikulicz Pyloroplasty: Drainage procedure done with truncal vagotomy. Transverse incision across pylorus closed vertically to prevent post-vagotomy gastroparesis.

Approach to Upper GI Bleeding (UGIB)

1. Initial Management Protocol

  1. Resuscitation & Stabilization: Airway protection, 2 large-bore IV lines, IV crystalloids, blood transfusion.
  2. Pharmacotherapy: IV High-dose PPI (e.g., Esomeprazole 80 mg bolus then 8 mg/hr infusion). If portal hypertension/cirrhosis suspected: IV Octreotide + broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  3. Urgent EGD: Diagnostic & therapeutic within 24 hours.

2. Etiology & Specific Management of Non-Variceal Bleeding

Condition Pathophysiology & Endoscopic Appearance Treatment Modalities
Mallory-Weiss Tear Longitudinal mucosal laceration at GEJ caused by severe retching/vomiting. Submucosal vessel erosion. Self-limiting (80-90%). If ongoing bleeding: PPIs, endoscopic clips, or epinephrine injection.
Dieulafoy's Lesion Large, aberrantly dilated submucosal artery that erodes mucosal surface in the absence of ulceration. "Miliary aneurysm". Endoscopic clipping, thermocoagulation, or Angiographic Embolization.
Gastrointestinal AVMs Arteriovenous malformations; accounts for <5% of UGIB. Flat red mucosal lesions or polypoid lesions. Endoscopic therapy (APC/clipping/epinephrine).
Erosive / Reflux Esophagitis Mucosal destruction due to acid reflux. Graded using Los Angeles (LA) Classification (Grades A–D). Grades A/B: Medical (PPI). Grades C/D: Aggressive medical or endoscopic therapy if bleeding.

Gastric Malignancy & GIST

1. Gastric Adenocarcinoma

5th most common malignancy worldwide, 3rd most deadly. 95% of gastric cancers are adenocarcinomas.

⭐ Diagnostic Laparoscopy Mandate

Before initiating curative resection or neoadjuvant therapy for localized gastric cancer, a Diagnostic Laparoscopy must be performed to rule out occult peritoneal carcinomatosis, which renders the disease Stage IV (unresectable).

2. Surgical Standards: D2 Lymphadenectomy & Reconstruction

  • Curative Resection Margins: Requires at least 5 cm proximal and distal clear margins.
  • D1 Lymphadenectomy: Harvests Perigastric Nodes (Stations 1–6).
  • D2 Lymphadenectomy: Mandatory global standard. Harvests Stations 1–6 PLUS Central/Vascular Nodes (Stations 7–12 along celiac axis, hepatic, splenic arteries). Must retrieve at least 15 lymph nodes.
Reconstruction Method Description Clinical Implication
Billroth I (Gastroduodenostomy) Anastomosis of remaining gastric remnant directly to duodenum Option post-distal gastrectomy; higher rate of bile reflux
Roux-en-Y Gastrojejunostomy Y-configuration jejunal limb anastomosed to gastric remnant/esophagus Preferred reconstruction method; eliminates alkaline bile reflux esophagitis

3. Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST)

Mesenchymal neoplasms arising from the Interstitial Cells of Cajal (ICC) (pacemaker cells of GI tract). 2/3 occur in the stomach.

Risk Category Tumor Size Mitotic Count (per 50 HPF)
Very Low Risk< 2 cm< 5 mitoses
Low Risk2 to 5 cm< 5 mitoses
Intermediate Risk< 5 cm OR 5–10 cm6–10 mitoses OR < 5 mitoses
High Risk> 5 cm OR > 10 cm> 5 mitoses OR Any mitotic rate
💡 GIST Surgical & Medical Management

Surgical: Complete wedge resection with clean negative margins. Lymphadenectomy is NOT required because GIST metastasizes via hematogenous route (primarily to liver/peritoneum), not lymphatics.
Targeted Therapy: Imatinib mesylate (Gleevec) (c-KIT tyrosine kinase inhibitor) indicated preoperatively for downstaging or postoperatively for 3+ years in high-risk patients.

Ultra-Condensed 10-Minute Cram Sheet

🎯 "IF YOU SEE X → THINK Y"

  • Swallowed disc battery: Emergency alkali burn ➔ Immediate endoscopic removal.
  • TB patient with mid-esophageal pouch: Parabronchial Diverticulum (Traction, True).
  • Regurgitation of undigested food + Halitosis: Zenker's Diverticulum (Killian triangle).
  • Post-retching hematemesis: Mallory-Weiss Tear (Longitudinal GEJ mucosal laceration).
  • Massive UGIB without ulceration/erosion: Dieulafoy's Lesion (Aberrant submucosal artery).
  • Gastric biopsy showing Cajal cells: GIST (Treat with Imatinib).
