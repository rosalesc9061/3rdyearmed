Soil-transmitted helminthiasis

Children, burden, and consequences

Core burden

Hookworm, ascariasis, and trichuriasis are the three common STHs emphasized. Multiple intestinal infections in the Philippines are common (51–64%). Schoolchildren are a high-risk reservoir: poor hand hygiene, dirty nails, raw or poorly washed food, outdoor soil exposure, rapid growth and high nutritional demand. Growth, learning, fluency and memory can suffer; early disease may be overlooked.

Patterns of harm

Hookworm causes intestinal bleeding and iron deficiency; Ascaris competes for micronutrients, impairs growth and can obstruct the bowel; Trichuris causes diarrhea or dysentery and rectal prolapse. All three are linked in the lecture to cognitive impairment.

Ascariasis

Intestinal adult worm and lung larval migration

Transmission and disease

Ascaris lumbricoides is a nematode; adults inhabit the small-intestinal lumen. Hand-to-mouth ingestion of contaminated raw produce spreads ova, which the lecture says can persist at 5–10°C for up to two years. Passing a spaghetti-like worm per rectum is common; vague abdominal pain occurs, usually without diarrhea/dysentery. Mild infection can be asymptomatic; heavy infection causes malnutrition, GI symptoms and obstruction.

Migration and complications

The larval migratory phase causes Löffler syndrome: transient pneumonitis, fever, wheeze, eosinophilia and sometimes hemoptysis. Eosinophilia reflects hypersensitivity to larvae. Adult migration may be triggered by fever, illness, anesthesia or some anthelminthics; worms may exit nose or mouth or enter the biliary tract, appendix, and peritoneum. The lecture's complication series: intestinal obstruction 63%, bile duct obstruction 23%, perforation/peritonitis 3.2%, volvulus 2.7%, hepatic abscess 2.1%, appendicitis 2.1%, pancreatitis 1%, cerebral encephalitis 1%, intussusception 0.5%. Of obstruction cases, 85% occurred at 1–5 years; this is a series-specific proportion.

Environmental contamination

In endemic settings ova may contaminate hands and nail dirt, produce, dust, household objects, transport, money, bathrooms, schools and outdoor play areas. Soil play with poor hygiene increases exposure.

Trichuriasis

Whipworm and colonic burden

Clinical spectrum

Most cases are asymptomatic. Heavy infections, especially in younger children, cause vague abdominal pain, chronic profuse or nocturnal diarrhea with or without mucus and blood, dysentery (lecture: >200 worms), anemia in massive infection, stunting, weight loss and failure to thrive. There is no pulmonary migratory phase in this lecture's comparison.

Rectal prolapse

The worst complication highlighted: worms adhering to rectal mucosa with a heavy burden contribute to prolapse. The lecture describes a 'cheese on a cinnamon roll' appearance and notes manual reduction may be possible.

Hookworm infection

Skin entry and blood loss

Epidemiology

Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale are common human hookworms; N. americanus predominates in the Philippines. Warm moist agricultural areas and barefoot exposure increase risk.

Organ manifestations

Skin penetration causes erythema, macules/papules and ground itch on soles. Larval passage through lungs causes verminous pneumonia. Adults attach to upper intestinal mucosa: epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, altered bowel habit and sometimes bloody dysentery. Blood and nutrient loss can cause weight loss and microcytic hypochromic anemia (labeled rare in the lecture symptom table). Correct anemia as supportive care.

Prevention

Use shoes/boots, sanitary disposal of feces and control exposure. The hookworm-specific lecture note says mass chemotherapy if incidence >50%.

STH prevention and control

WHO and Philippine DOH schedules

WHO 2017 preventive chemotherapy

Aim: reduce worm burden. Target young children 12–23 months, preschool 24–59 months, school-age children, and nonpregnant adolescent girls (10–19) and women of reproductive age (15–49) where prevalence of any STH is ≥20%. Single albendazole 400 mg (200 mg under 24 months) or mebendazole 500 mg annually; twice yearly if baseline prevalence >50%.

DOH program

Chemotherapy through schools and health centers is the core package. Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) and behavior change sustain impact. Philippine lecture recommends six-monthly treatment owing to high prevalence. DOH table: 1–24 months albendazole 200 mg or mebendazole 500 mg every six months; ≥24 months albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 500 mg every six months; adolescent females albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 500 mg annually. The 400-mg albendazole tablet can be halved for a 200-mg dose; adolescent females consulting health centers are offered anthelminthics. The lecture's 1-month lower bound differs from WHO's 12-month target; verify age eligibility with current local protocol before clinical use.

Enterobiasis

Perianal eggs and household reinfection

Clinical pattern

Enterobius vermicularis often causes no symptoms. Nocturnal gravid female migration deposits eggs in perianal folds, causing typical night pruritus, scratching, excoriation and bacterial superinfection; thumb sucking leads to autoinoculation. Heavy burden can disturb sleep, appetite, mood, weight and GI function. Female genital migration may cause vaginitis, endometritis and granulomas; rare appendix, peritoneal or bladder involvement.

Spread and diagnosis

A familial disease spreading through shared bedding, oral hand-to-mouth contamination, inhalation of egg-laden dust and anal re-entry. The lecture calls it the only intestinal nematode not controlled by fecal disposal because eggs are deposited outside the stool. Gold standard is cellulose/cellophane (Graham Scotch) tape or anal swab for eggs, collected morning before washing or defecation.

Management and prevention

Albendazole, mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate are listed; repeat treatment after two weeks may be necessary because reinfection is common. Treat entire household; short nails, handwashing, showering, careful laundering or boiling bedding and sleep separation reduce spread.

Lymphatic filariasis

Nocturnal microfilariae and lymphatic disease

Burden and early disease

Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi. Lecture notes endemicity in 44 provinces across 12 regions, 30 million targeted for mass treatment and major long-term disability; these are lecture-era figures. Most infection is asymptomatic yet subclinical lymphatic dilation may occur. Children may have lymphadenopathy; ultrasound may visualize adults in inguinal, crural or axillary vessels before puberty.

Progression

Death of adult worms provokes retrograde acute lymphangitis with mild fever/headache. Postpubertal males can develop funiculitis, epididymitis, orchitis or palpable granulomatous nodule in scrotal lymphatics. Chronic obstruction and inflammation cause lymphedema, mostly legs but also arms, breast or genitalia; recurrent bacterial infection hastens elephantiasis. Hydrocele may occur in about 30% of infected males per lecture.

Diagnosis and control

Standard active-infection diagnosis: microfilariae in blood smear drawn 10 pm–2 am due to nocturnal periodicity. Serologic antifilarial IgG1/IgG4 assays are alternatives. Selective positive-case treatment is DEC 6 mg/kg/day in three divided doses for 12 days after meals. In established areas, annual mass therapy for ages ≥2 years is DEC 6 mg/kg single dose plus albendazole 400 mg. Lymphedema care and hydrocele surgery address disability.

Schistosomiasis

Cercarial entry, egg granulomas and fibrosis

Early and late disease

Freshwater cercariae penetrate skin causing swimmer's itch. Egg antigen release can trigger Katayama fever with fever, urticaria, malaise and diarrhea. Trapped eggs cause macrophage/T-cell granulomas; fibroblasts stimulated by egg products and inflammatory cytokines deposit collagen, producing late fibrosis. Lecture mentions coastal endemic areas such as Leyte.

Diagnosis

Gold standard: microscopic eggs in stool or urine. S. haematobium has a terminal spine, S. mansoni a lateral spine, and S. japonicum is small, nearly spherical with a tiny spine. Circumoval Precipitin Test (COPT) is described as the Philippine method of choice for definitive immunologic diagnosis, but antibodies cannot distinguish current from past infection or quantify burden. Antigen blood/urine tests at RITM are described as unavailable commercially. Molecular probes can detect S. mansoni DNA; genome work has potential vaccine applications.

Management and control

Swimmer's itch and Katayama fever are treated symptomatically. Praziquantel is the drug of choice, sourced through a DOH schistosoma unit per lecture: S. mansoni, S. haematobium, S. intercalatum 40 mg/kg/day orally in 2 divided doses for one day; S. japonicum and S. mekongi 60 mg/kg/day orally in 3 divided doses for one day. Avoid freshwater exposure; education, snail control by molluscicides and irrigation engineering reduce transmission.

Disaster-related diseases

Crowding, water, wounds and surveillance

Risk pathways

After natural disaster expect waterborne, airborne/droplet, vectorborne and contaminated-wound disease. Crowding in evacuation centers favors diarrhea, respiratory and contact spread. Unsafe stagnant water favors diarrhea, dysentery and leptospirosis. Interrupted immunization can increase vaccine-preventable disease.

Infection prevention

Regular handwashing is the universal low-cost measure, alongside clean living spaces and personal hygiene. Screen and triage ill persons, isolate or cohort similar illness, refer for assessment, and post visual alerts; crowding limits feasibility.

Immediate public-health response

Where baseline measles coverage under 15 years is <90%, implement mass measles immunization with vitamin A rapidly; priority 6 months–5 years, expanding to 15 years if feasible. Primary care prioritizes diarrhea and ARI in under-fives, malaria treatment within 24 hours of fever onset in endemic areas (artemisinin combination for P. falciparum), ORS, proper wound care with tetanus toxoid ± immunoglobulin as indicated, and antimicrobial access. Train surveillance workers, report priority diseases and stock sampling, transport and outbreak supplies (e.g., cholera kits).

Childhood leptospirosis

Flood exposure, early recognition and PIDSP prophylaxis

Agent and exposure

Leptospira interrogans is a thin helical spirochete. Rat reservoirs Rattus norvegicus and R. rattus are principal sources in the Philippines; dogs, cats, livestock and goats also occur. Exposure includes contaminated floodwater, rat-infested premises or food, farm runoff and infected animal urine. Entry via broken skin or intact mucosa; prolonged soaking can permit entry without visible breaks. Bloodstream spread causes vasculitis with hepatic and renal injury. Lecture reports 92% seropositivity in 106 Metro Manila/Laguna rats and Manilae, Hebdomadis and Losbanos serovars (a survey, not population prevalence).

Suspect and assess severity

Fever, abdominal pain, myalgia/joint pain and headache plus calf tenderness, jaundice or hepatomegaly support suspicion. Pulmonary findings, shock, altered mental state and bleeding predict severe illness. The teaching case: 12-year-old after flood wading, fever, calf pain, vomiting, oliguria, conjunctival suffusion, thrombocytopenia and raised creatinine; IV penicillin and supportive care led to recovery. Jaundice should prompt renal assessment.

Testing

MAT is the gold standard but not a routine test in this guideline: antibodies peak weeks 2–3, prior titers persist 1–5 years. Rapid IgM tests may help from day 5 within the first week; PCR/serology can confirm, but do not delay treatment for results. Practical complication predictors: low platelet count, high creatinine and potassium abnormalities. Lecture lists platelets ≤50,000/µL, lactate >2.5 mM, amylase >250 UI/L and assorted renal/hematologic tests; creatinine '>200 Mm' and leptospiremia '1000/MI' have apparent unit transcription problems, so confirm units before using a threshold.

Treatment

Uncomplicated: doxycycline 2–4 mg/kg/day (max 200 mg/day) ×7 days; second-line amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day every 8 h ×7 days or azithromycin 10 mg/kg/day (lecture does not specify azithromycin duration). Alternatives listed are cefotaxime 100–150 mg/kg in 3–4 divided doses, ceftriaxone 80–100 mg/kg/day once daily (max 2 g). Severe illness/oliguria or severe abdominal pain warrants admission and early IV penicillin per lecture; ceftriaxone is an IV alternative for penicillin allergy. Support organ dysfunction.

Prevention and August 2026 PIDSP advisory

Avoid floodwater; if unavoidable use boots, goggles, overalls and gloves, protect wounds, safe drinking water and washed/cooked produce, prevent rodent contamination; no human vaccine is listed. The lecture's advisory calls for prophylaxis within 24–72 hours after exposure. One-time wading: doxycycline 4 mg/kg single dose (max 200 mg); alternative azithromycin 10 mg/kg single dose (max 500 mg). Open wounds or ingested water: doxycycline for 5 days or azithromycin for 3 days; oral penicillin and amoxicillin regimens are also listed. Repeated exposure: once weekly until end of risk. Short doxycycline use below age 8 may be considered in this specific guideline; a single small cited study is the lecture's tooth-staining rationale. Pre-exposure doxycycline begins two weeks before risk and continues through it; food helps GI upset; separate dairy, iron or antacids one hour before and two hours after.
