📖 Comprehensive Executive Summary

Lecture 02 • Dr. John M. Gomez

This master interactive study suite synthesizes the complete lecture curriculum on Duodenum, Small Intestine, and Appendix for 3rd-year medical students preparing for Surgery II examinations and the Physician Licensure Examination (PLE). The small intestine represents the body's primary nutrient absorption organ, its largest reservoir of endocrine cells, and its largest immune system component (GALT).

🎯 Core Module Blueprint & Learning Objectives

1. Basic Sciences Foundation

  • Master gross anatomical boundaries, mesentery differences between jejunum and ileum.
  • Detail 4 histological layers, crypt stem cell lineages (Paneth, Enterocytes, Goblet, Enteroendocrine).
  • Understand embryological midgut rotation and vitelline duct remnants.
  • Analyze fluid/electrolyte fluxes (8-9L daily traversal) and nutrient breakdown pathways.

2. Intestinal Motility & Obstruction

  • Distinguish mechanical Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO) from Postoperative Ileus and CIPO.
  • Interpret radiological findings (CT transition points, target sign, step-ladder fluid levels).
  • Identify indications for conservative vs emergency surgical interventions.

3. Mesenteric Ischemia & Vascular Disorders

  • Compare Acute Mesenteric Ischemia (SMA Embolus vs Thrombosis vs NOMI vs SMV Thrombosis).
  • Identify classic clinical triads ("pain out of proportion to exam", postprandial angina, food fear).
  • Execute rapid diagnostic workups and surgical management strategies.

4. Appendiceal Disease & Neoplasms

  • Calculate Alvarado Score (MANTRELS) and follow evidence-based treatment algorithms.
  • Recognize physical exam signs (Rovsing, Dunphy, Obturator, Psoas, Rectal bogginess).
  • Differentiate carcinoids, adenocarcinomas, mucoeceles, and Pseudomyxoma Peritonei.

🔬 Gross Anatomy, Histology & Embryology

Core Science
1. Gross Anatomy: Duodenum, Jejunum & Ileum ▼

The small intestine is a tubular structure measuring 4 to 6 meters in length, extending from the pylorus to the ileocecal valve.

Anatomical Feature Jejunum (Proximal 40%) Ileum (Distal 60%)
Lumen Diameter & Wall Larger diameter, thicker muscular wall Smaller diameter, thinner wall
Plicae Circulares (Valvulae Conniventes) Large, tall, closely spaced folds Sparse, low, absent in distal ileum
Mesenteric Fat Less fat; clear "windows" near bowel wall Abundant fat ("creeping fat" extends onto serosa)
Vascular Arcades & Vasa Recta Simple arcades (1-2 tiers), longer vasa recta Complex arcades (3-5 tiers), shorter vasa recta
Lymphoid Tissue Solitary lymph follicles Peyer's Patches (macroscopic aggregates)
⭐ Must Know - Blood Supply & Lymphatics

• Arterial Supply: Celiac Axis (Duodenum proximal to Papilla of Vater) and Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) (distal duodenum to splenic flexure).

• Venous Drainage: Superior Mesenteric Vein (SMV) → joins Splenic Vein to form Portal Vein.

• Lymphatic Drainage: Lacteals → Mesenteric Lymph Nodes → Cisterna Chyli → Thoracic Duct → Left Subclavian Vein.

2. Histology & Crypt Stem Cell Lineages ▼

The small intestinal wall consists of 4 distinct concentric layers: Mucosa, Submucosa, Muscularis Propria, and Serosa.

Figure 1: Schematic cross-section of the 4 layers of the small intestinal wall.

All epithelial cells originate from stem cells located at the base of the Crypts of Lieberkühn (250-300 cells/crypt) and differentiate into 4 pathways:

  • Enterocytes: Predominant absorptive cell; microvilli increase surface area 40-fold.
  • Goblet Cells: Mucus-secreting cells providing mucosal lubrication and barrier defense.
  • Paneth Cells: Located at the crypt base; secrete digestive enzymes, growth factors, and antimicrobial peptides (defensins, lysozyme) to shape the microbiome.
  • Enteroendocrine Cells: Contain hormone-filled secretory granules regulating GI function.
💡 Clinical Pearl - High Turnover & Injury

The rapid epithelial renewal rate provides remarkable mucosal resiliency, but makes the gut uniquely vulnerable to acute cytotoxic damage from radiation therapy and chemotherapy.

3. Embryology & Vitelline Duct Anomalies ▼

The embryonic gut tube derives from Endoderm:

  • Foregut: Proximal Duodenum (up to Papilla of Vater).
  • Midgut: Distal Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum, Cecum, Appendix, Ascending Colon, proximal 2/3 Transverse Colon.
  • Hindgut: Distal 1/3 Transverse Colon to Upper Anal Canal.

Timeline:

  • 5th Week: Rapid elongation causes physiological extra-coelomic herniation into umbilical cord.
  • 6th Week: Vitelline duct (omphalomesenteric duct) narrows.
  • 9th Week: Villi and crypt formation begins.
  • 10th-11th Week: Return of midgut to abdominal cavity with 270° counterclockwise rotation around SMA.
  • 12th Week: Organogenesis complete; vitelline duct normally obliterates completely.
⚠️ Common Pitfall - Meckel's Diverticulum

Failure of complete vitelline duct obliteration produces a spectrum of anomalies, most commonly Meckel's Diverticulum (true diverticulum containing all wall layers).

🧠 Exam Tip - Motility Pacemaker

The fundamental electrical slow waves of small intestinal peristalsis are generated by the Interstitial Cells of Cajal (ICC), which function as the intrinsic pacemaker cells located within the muscularis propria.

🚧 Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO), Ileus & CIPO

High Yield Exam Topic
1. Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO): Pathophysiology & Etiologies ▼

Mechanical obstruction to the flow of intestinal contents. Can be simple, closed-loop, or strangulated.

⭐ Must Know - SBO Etiology Ranking

• Postoperative Intra-abdominal Adhesions (#1 Overall cause in Western world, >60-70%): Prior surgery is the single biggest risk factor.

• Hernias (#1 cause worldwide in unoperated abdomens): Inguinal, femoral, ventral, incisional, internal.

• Neoplasms (Extrinsic or Intrinsic): Metastases (ovarian, colon), primary carcinoid/adenocarcinoma.

• Other Causes: Intussusception, Crohn's strictures, Gallstone ileus, Volvulus, Radiation strictures.

Pathophysiologic Cascade:

Bowel distention proximal to obstruction → fluid/gas accumulation → elevated intraluminal pressure → venous congestion → bowel wall edema → arterial compromise → ischemia → necrosis and perforation!

2. Diagnostic Workup & CT Findings ▼

Clinical Triad: Crampy abdominal pain, obstipation/constipation, bilious vomiting, and abdominal distention.

Imaging Modalities:

  • Abdominal X-rays (Supine + Upright): Dilated small bowel loops (>3 cm), air-fluid levels in a "step-ladder" pattern, absence of colonic gas.
  • Contrast CT Scan of Abdomen/Pelvis (Gold Standard): Identifies exact site of transition point (dilated proximal bowel → collapsed distal bowel), etiology, and signs of strangulation.
CT Scan Finding Pathological Meaning Clinical Urgency
Transition Point Abrupt change from dilated proximal bowel to collapsed distal bowel Diagnostic of mechanical SBO
Small Bowel Feces Sign Particulate material mixed with gas in distal dilated small bowel Indicates subacute or low-grade mechanical obstruction
Closed-Loop Obstruction U- or C-shaped bowel loop with two points of obstruction; mesenteric radial convergence High risk of strangulation! Emergency surgery
Pneumatosis Intestinalis & Portal Venous Gas Gas inside bowel wall and intrahepatic portal veins Bowel Infarction / Gangrene! STAT Laparotomy
3. Management Algorithm: Conservative vs Emergency Surgery ▼

Non-Operative Management (Partial / Uncomplicated SBO)

  • NPO (Nothing per os)
  • Nasogastric (NG) Tube Decompression
  • Isotonic IV Fluid Resuscitation + Electrolyte correction
  • Serial physical examinations every 4-6 hours
  • Water-soluble contrast study (Gastrografin challenge): If contrast reaches colon within 24 hours → 90%+ resolution rate!

Immediate Operative Indications (Exploratory Laparotomy)

  • Signs of Strangulation / Ischemia: Peritonitis (fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, localized rebound/guarding).
  • Complete SBO: No passage of gas/stool and no distal colonic air on CT.
  • Closed-loop obstruction or volvulus.
  • Failure of conservative therapy after 48–72 hours.
  • Incarcerated irreducible hernia.
4. Postoperative Ileus vs CIPO Comparison ▼
Feature Postoperative Ileus (Physiologic / Prolonged) Chronic Intestinal Pseudo-Obstruction (CIPO)
Definition Transient impairment of bowel motility following surgical stress/manipulation. Rare, chronic severe motility disorder mimicking mechanical SBO without structural lesion.
Normal Motility Recovery Sequence • Small Intestine: Recovers within 24 hours
• Stomach: Recovers in 24-48 hours
• Colon: Recovers in 3 to 5 days		 Recurrent episodes over months to years; primary visceral myopathy or neuropathy.
Best Clinical Indicator of Recovery Flatus or bowel movement (Bowel sounds are NOT a reliable indicator!). Intermittent pseudo-obstruction flare-ups.
Management Correction of electrolytes (K+, Mg2+), minimization of opioids, early ambulation, gum chewing, prokinetics. Prokinetics (Prucalopride, Metoclopramide), nutritional support, decompression, avoid surgery!

🫀 Acute & Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia

Surgical Emergency
1. Acute Mesenteric Ischemia (AMI): 4 Subtypes ▼

Acute mesenteric ischemia carries a mortality rate of 60% - 80% if diagnosis is delayed beyond infarction!

Subtype Frequency Etiology / Pathophysiology Key Risk Factors & Diagnostic Clues
1. Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) Embolus ~50% (Most Common) Embolus dislodged from heart lodges in SMA, typically 6-8 cm distal to origin (spares proximal jejunum & duodenum) Atrial fibrillation, recent MI, mural thrombus. Sudden excruciating onset! Spares proximal jejunum.
2. SMA Thrombosis ~25% Acute thrombosis superimposed on chronic pre-existing atherosclerotic stenosis at SMA origin Generalized atherosclerosis, history of postprandial abdominal angina and weight loss. Infarcts proximal jejunum too!
3. Non-Occlusive Mesenteric Ischemia (NOMI) ~20% Splanchnic vasoconstriction secondary to low cardiac output or hypovolemia without organic vessel occlusion ICU patients, shock, vasopressor therapy (norepinephrine), digitalis toxicity, heart failure.
4. Superior Mesenteric Vein (SMV) Thrombosis ~5-10% Thrombotic occlusion of SMV causing massive venous engorgement, bowel wall edema, and secondary arterial inflow failure Hypercoagulable states (Factor V Leiden, OCP use, malignancy), portal hypertension, pancreatitis. Circumferential wall thickening!
💡 Clinical Pearl - Classic Presentation Paradox

Early AMI presents with severe, intractable abdominal pain OUT OF PROPORTION to physical exam findings (abdomen remains soft, non-tender initially because mucosal ischemia precedes full-thickness transmural necrosis). Soft abdomen + severe pain + Atrial Fibrillation = SMA Embolus until proven otherwise!

2. Diagnostic & Surgical Management Pathways ▼
  • CTA Abdomen/Pelvis (Mesenteric Angiography CT): Diagnostic modality of choice. Shows vessel cut-off, lack of wall enhancement, bowel loop dilatation, pneumatosis intestinalis.
  • Laboratory Markers: Leukocytosis (>20,000), severe L-lactic acidosis (late marker of transmural necrosis), elevated amylase/D-dimer.

Surgical Intervention Steps (Exploratory Laparotomy):

  1. Revascularization First: Restore arterial flow via SMA embolectomy/bypass BEFORE resecting bowel, as borderline tissue may recover!
  2. Resection of Non-Viable Bowel: Segmental resection of frankly necrotic bowel.
  3. Assessment of Bowel Viability: Inspection of color, peristalsis, arterial pulsation, intravenous fluorescein with Wood's lamp, or Doppler ultrasound.
  4. Second-Look Laparotomy: Planned re-exploration in 24 to 48 hours to reassess marginal bowel before definitive anastomosis.
3. Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia (CMI) ▼

Develops insidiously secondary to severe atherosclerotic narrowing of splanchnic arteries. Requires severe stenosis of at least 2 of the 3 major visceral arteries (Celiac Axis, SMA, IMA) due to extensive collateral network.

⭐ Must Know - Classic Clinical Triad of CMI

1. Postprandial Abdominal Angina: Severe dull/crampy epigastric pain occurring 15-45 minutes after eating (due to unfulfilled visceral metabolic demand).

2. "Food Fear" (Sitophobia): Patient consciously avoids eating to prevent severe postprandial pain.

3. Significant Weight Loss: Secondary to sitophobia and progressive malabsorption.

Treatment: Mesenteric revascularization (Endovascular angioplasty/stenting OR open surgical bypass).

🔥 Inflammatory, Diverticular & Miscellaneous Conditions

Diverse Pathology
1. Crohn's Disease & Intestinal Fistulas ▼

Crohn's Disease: Transmural inflammatory process that can affect any part of the GI tract (terminal ileum most common site, ~50%). Features transmural lymphoid aggregates, non-caseating granulomas, skip lesions, cobblestone mucosa, creeping fat, and deep fissures/fistulas.

Intestinal Fistulas:

Abnormal communications between two epithelialized surfaces. 80-90% are iatrogenic post-surgical complications!

⚠️ Common Pitfall - Factors Preventing Spontaneous Fistula Closure

Remember the classic "FRIEND" Mnemonic:

• Foreign body within fistula tract

• Radiation enteritis

• Infection / Inflammation / Abscess at origin

• Epithelialization of the fistula tract

• Neoplasm at fistula site

• Distal intestinal obstruction

Fistula Surgical Timing: Operative intervention is delayed for 2 to 3 months (at least 5-8 weeks) of conservative TPN/sepsis control, as 90% of fistulas that close spontaneously do so within 5-6 weeks.

2. Radiation Enteritis & Small Bowel Perforation ▼

Radiation Enteritis: Damage from pelvic/abdominal radiotherapy (dosage >4,500 - 5,000 cGy). Acute injury features villus blunting; chronic injury features progressive occlusive endarteritis / vasculitis leading to ischemia, strictures, abscesses, and fistulas. Anastomotic leak rates post-surgery are extremely high!

Small Bowel Perforation & Stapfer Classification:

Most common cause of duodenal perforation currently is iatrogenic trauma during ERCP with endoscopic sphincterotomy categorized by the Stapfer Classification:

Type Anatomical Site Mechanism & Primary Management
Type I Duodenal lateral or medial wall Direct scope perforation. Free intraperitoneal air. Requires immediate surgery!
Type II (Most Common) Periampullary retroperitoneal wall Sphincterotomy injury. Retroperitoneal air. Usually managed CONSERVATIVELY (NPO, IV antibiotics, drainage).
Type III Bile duct or pancreatic duct Wire / basket instrumentation. Managed non-operatively or endoscopically with stent.
Type IV Retroperitoneal air alone Hyperinsufflation. Spontaneous resolution without treatment.
3. Meckel's Diverticulum: Epidemiology & Management ▼

The most common congenital anomaly of the GI tract (present in ~2% of the population).

⭐ Must Know - Meckel's "Rule of 2s"

• 2% prevalence in general population

• 2 feet (60 cm) proximal to the ileocecal valve

• 2 inches (5 cm) in length

• 2:1 Male-to-Female ratio

• 2 types of common ectopic tissue: Heterotopic Gastric mucosa (~50%) and Pancreatic tissue

• Presents before 2 years of age (most symptomatic cases)

Patient Demographic Most Common Symptomatic Presentation Pathophysiological Mechanism
Pediatric Population (<18 yrs) Painless Lower GI Bleeding (Melena / Bright Red Stool) Ectopic gastric mucosa secretes HCl → acid ulceration of adjacent normal ileal mucosa! Diagnostic scan: Technetium-99m Pertechnetate Scan ("Meckel's Scan").
Adult Population Intestinal Obstruction or Diverticulitis Volvulus around persistent fibrous omphalomesenteric band, intussusception lead point, or inflammation mimicking appendicitis.

Hernia Association: Littre's Hernia = Presence of a Meckel's diverticulum within a hernia sac (inguinal or femoral).

4. Chylous Ascites, Intussusception, Pneumatosis & Short Bowel Syndrome ▼
  • Chylous Ascites: Accumulation of milky triglyceride-rich lymph in peritoneal cavity. Treatment: TPN, Octreotide, and High-Protein Low-Fat diet with Medium-Chain Triglycerides (MCTs) (MCTs absorb directly into portal vein without entering lymphatic chyle!).
  • Intussusception: Telescoping of proximal bowel into distal bowel. Pediatric = idiopathic/lymphoid hyperplasia (treat with air/contrast enema); Adult = 90% have an underlying organic lead point (frequently neoplasm) → requires surgical resection without initial reduction!
  • Short Bowel Syndrome (SBS): Malabsorptive state resulting from massive small bowel resection (remaining small bowel <200 cm). Requires TPN, enteral adaptation, GLP-2 analog (Teduglutide), and surgical procedures like STEP (Serial Transverse Enteroplasty) or LILT.

🎗️ Small Bowel Neoplasms

Rare Primary Tumors

Small intestinal neoplasms account for less than 3% of all GI tract tumors, despite the small bowel representing 75% of GI tract length and 90% of mucosal surface area.

1. Benign vs Malignant Small Bowel Tumors ▼
Neoplasm Class Tumor Subtype Anatomical Distribution & Characteristics
Benign Tumors (#1 Adenomas) • Adenomatous Polyps
• Leiomyomas
• Lipomas
• Hamartomas		 Adenomas are most common; highest frequency in Duodenum. Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome features hamartomatous polyps + mucocutaneous pigmentation.
Malignant Tumors 1. Neuroendocrine Tumors (NETs / Carcinoids) Most common overall primary malignant SI tumor! Most common in Distal Ileum. Secrete Serotonin, Histamine, Substance P. Carcinoid Syndrome (flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm, right heart valvular disease) occurs only with liver metastases!
Malignant Tumors 2. Adenocarcinoma Most common in Duodenum (40%) and proximal jejunum. Strongly associated with Crohn's, Celiac sprue, FAP, and HNPCC. Present with obstruction or occult bleeding.
Malignant Tumors 3. Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GIST) Arise from Interstitial Cells of Cajal (ICC). Express c-KIT (CD117) mutation. High bleeding risk due to central ulceration. Treatment: Resection + Imatinib (Gleevec).
Malignant Tumors 4. Lymphoma Most common site = Ileum (due to abundance of Peyer's patches). Non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma most frequent. High risk of perforation during chemotherapy!
2. Surgical Management Strategies for Duodenal & Jejunoileal Neoplasms ▼
  • Duodenal Adenomas / Polyps: Endoscopic polypectomy if <1 cm; Transduodenal resective polypectomy if >2 cm; Whipple Procedure (Pancreaticoduodenectomy) for invasive duodenal adenocarcinoma.
  • Jejunoileal Malignancy: Wide segmental intestinal resection including regional mesenteric lymph node drainage with clear negative margins.

🩺 Acute Appendicitis & Appendiceal Neoplasms

Most Common Surgical Emergency
1. Anatomy, Etiology & Pathophysiologic Cascade ▼

Anatomy: Arises from the posteromedial cecal border ~2 cm below ileocecal valve, at the convergence of the 3 taeniae coli (taenia libera leads directly to base). Position is variable: Retrocecal (~65%, most common), Pelvic (~30%), Subcecal, Preileal, Postileal.

Etiology of Luminal Obstruction:

  • Pediatric Population: Lymphoid Hyperplasia secondary to viral/bacterial infections (#1 cause).
  • Adult Population: Fecalith (Appendicolith) (#1 cause), stricture, foreign body, parasites (Enterobius), or neoplasm.

Pathophysiologic Timeline:

2. Classical Physical Examination Signs & Diagnostic Criteria ▼
Physical Exam Sign Clinical Maneuver Pathological Mechanism / Meaning
McBurney's Point Tenderness Palpation 1/3 distance from Anterior Superior Iliac Spine (ASIS) to umbilicus Direct local inflammation of parietal peritoneum. Maximum tenderness site.
Rovsing's Sign Palpation/deep pressure in Left Lower Quadrant (LLQ) causes pain in RLQ Retrograde displacement of colonic gas distends inflamed cecum/appendix.
Dunphy's Sign Increased abdominal pain elicited by coughing Peritoneal irritation (frequently positive in retrocecal appendicitis).
Iliopsoas (Psoas) Sign Passive extension of right hip (or active hip flexion against resistance) Irritation of retroperitoneal psoas muscle by an inflamed retrocecal appendix.
Obturator Sign Passive internal rotation of flexed right hip Irritation of internal obturator muscle by an inflamed pelvic appendix.
Rectal / Pelvic Bogginess Tenderness/fullness on right side during Digital Rectal Examination (DRE) Indicates pelvic appendix or pelvic abscess collection.

Alvarado Score 1 - 4 (Low Risk, ~30%)

Unlikely appendicitis. Safe to discharge home with strict warning instructions and outpatient re-evaluation in 24 hours.

Alvarado Score 5 - 6 (Intermediate Risk, ~66%)

Suspected appendicitis. Admit for observation, serial abdominal exams, and diagnostic imaging (CT scan for non-pregnant adults; USG for pregnant females/children).

Alvarado Score 7 - 10 (High Risk, ~93%)

High probability of acute appendicitis. Proceed directly to surgical consultation for Appendectomy (or urgent exploratory intervention).

4. Diagnostic Imaging & Appendiceal Neoplasms ▼

Radiological Diagnostic Criteria:

  • Abdominal CT Scan (Gold Standard, Sensitivity/Specificity >95%): Non-compressible appendix with diameter > 6 mm, outer wall thickness > 2 mm, periappendiceal fat stranding, appendicolith, appendiceal wall enhancement.
  • Graded Compression Ultrasound: Target sign, non-compressible blind-ending tubular structure > 6 mm in diameter. Preferred initial modality in pregnant women and pediatric patients to avoid ionizing radiation.

Appendiceal Neoplasms:

Neoplasm Type Clinical Features Surgical Treatment Strategy
Appendiceal Carcinoid (NET) Most common appendiceal tumor (~50%). Usually an incidental finding at tip. • Simple Appendectomy: Tumor < 1.0 cm or 1.0-2.0 cm at tip without mesoappendiceal invasion.
• Right Hemicolectomy: Tumor > 2.0 cm, involved mesoappendix, base involvement, or goblet cell carcinoid.
Mucinous Cystadenoma / Mucocele Distended mucus-filled appendix. Care must be taken not to rupture intraoperatively! Appendectomy with clear base margin. Spillage risks Pseudomyxoma Peritonei!
Pseudomyxoma Peritonei (PMP) "Jelly belly" — diffuse mucinous ascites and peritoneal implants secondary to ruptured mucinous neoplasm. Cytoreductive Surgery (CRS) + HIPEC (Hyperthermic Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy with Mitomycin C) + Peritonectomy.
Appendiceal Adenocarcinoma Rare malignant tumor. Presents like acute appendicitis in elderly patients. Right Hemicolectomy (regardless of size).
⚠️ Surgical Complication - Stump Appendicitis

Leaving an appendiceal remnant longer than 0.5 cm (5 mm) during appendectomy predisposes the patient to recurrent acute inflammation in the residual tissue, known as Stump Appendicitis.

⚡ Ultra-Condensed Cram Sheet (10-Minute Last-Minute Review)

High-Yield Buzzwords

🎯 "If You See X → Think Y" Clinical Reasoning

  • Severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical exam + Atrial Fibrillation → SMA Embolus
  • Postprandial abdominal angina + sitophobia (food fear) + weight loss → Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia

  • Air in the biliary tree (Pneumobilia) + SBO + radiopaque stone in RLQ → Gallstone Ileus (Rigler's Triad)
  • Milky peritoneal fluid rich in triglycerides → Chylous Ascites (Treat with TPN + MCTs + Octreotide)
  • Target sign on USG / CT with appendiceal diameter >6 mm → Acute Appendicitis
  • Recurrent appendicitis after prior appendectomy with stump >0.5 cm → Stump Appendicitis
  • Mucinous ascites + ruptured appendiceal mucocele → Pseudomyxoma Peritonei (Treat with CRS + HIPEC)
  • GIST tumor + c-KIT / CD117 positive → Originates from Interstitial Cells of Cajal (Treat with Imatinib)
  • Carcinoid syndrome (flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm) → Metastatic Neuroendocrine Tumor to Liver

🧠 Essential Mnemonics & Key Scores

  • Alvarado Score ("MANTRELS"): Migration (1), Anorexia (1), Nausea (1), Tenderness RLQ (2), Rebound (1), Elevated Temp (1), Leukocytosis (2), Shift to left (1). Maximum = 10.
  • FRIEND Mnemonic (Inhibits Fistula Closure): Foreign body, Radiation, Infection/Inflammation, Epithelialization, Neoplasm, Distal obstruction.
  • Meckel's Rule of 2s: 2% population, 2 feet from IC valve, 2 inches long, 2:1 M:F ratio, 2 ectopic tissues (gastric/pancreatic), presents before 2 years.
  • Stapfer Classification (ERCP Perforation): Type I (Duodenal wall - surgery), Type II (Periampullary - managed conservatively, most common), Type III (Ductal), Type IV (Air only).
  • Small Intestine Motility Recovery Timeline: Small Intestine (24 hrs), Stomach (24-48 hrs), Colon (3-5 days). Flatus/bowel movement = best recovery sign!
  • SBO Primary Etiologies: Postop Adhesions (#1 in operated abdomens), Hernias (#1 in unoperated abdomens worldwide).
