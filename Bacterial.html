I. Approach to a Child with Fever & SBI Risk

CLINICAL EVALUATION & GUIDELINES

🌡️ Fever Definitions & Temperature Sites

Fever is a systemic response of body temperature to foreign antigens or stress. Core/rectal temperature $\ge 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($100.4^\circ\text{F}$) is standard. Accepted site thresholds (PPS Handbook):

  • Rectal / Core: $\ge 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($100.4^\circ\text{F}$)
  • Oral: $\ge 37.6^\circ\text{C}$
  • Axillary: $\ge 37.4^\circ\text{C}$
  • Infrared Tympanic: $\ge 37.6^\circ\text{C}$ (Best for > 3 months of age)
⚠️ Hyperpyrexia Warning
Hyperpyrexia: Defined as a rectal temperature > 40.0°C (104.0°F). Demands immediate investigation for severe invasive bacterial infection or heat stroke.

📈 Patterns of Clinical Fever

Fever Pattern Temperature Behavior Classic Examples / Etiologies
Intermittent Returns to normal baseline on most days Sepsis (wide fluctuations)
Remittent Varies by > 0.5°C/day; never touches normal baseline Typhoid fever, Sepsis, Tuberculosis, Rheumatic fever
Sustained Persistent elevation; varies by $\le 0.5^\circ\text{C}$/day Typhus, Viral pneumonia
Relapsing Febrile episodes separated by afebrile intervals Tertian (every 48h: P. vivax), Quartan (every 72h: P. malariae)
Undulating Slow increase, persistent high, abrupt drop, short afebrile Brucellosis, Hodgkin's disease
Biphasic 2 distinct periods of fever ("camelback" pattern) Dengue fever, Leptospirosis (first afebrile then slight spike)
Periodic Recurring pattern every 3-4 weeks PFAPA syndrome (Pharyngitis, Aphthous stomatitis, Adenitis)
Factitious Self-induced or self-reported Malingering (not a true organic fever)

🔍 Fever Categories & Serious Bacterial Infection (SBI) Risk

Fever with Localizing Signs

Acute illness where a specific organ focus is identified upon history & physical exam (e.g., cough → respiratory tract; vomiting/diarrhea → GI).

Fever without Localizing Signs

Fever of recent onset (< 1 week), rectal temp $\ge 38^\circ\text{C}$ as sole feature without obvious focus. Requires urgent risk evaluation & empiric therapy.

Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO)

Fever > 38.0°C persisting for > 3 weeks with no cause identified after at least 8 days of intensive evaluation.

⭐ Age Distribution of Blood Culture-Proven Sepsis
  • Neonates (< 28 days): Most common infection is Primary Bloodstream Infection (Sepsis) followed by CLABSI. Key pathogens: E. coli (#1), Group B Streptococcus, Coagulase-negative Staph.
  • Young Infants (< 1 year): Most common infection is Urinary Tract Infection (UTI). Pathogen: E. coli. Routine urinalysis is required for fever without focus!
  • Older Children (≥ 1 year): Respiratory infections predominate (Pneumonia, Otitis Media). Pathogen: Streptococcus pneumoniae.
  • Children with Comorbidities (e.g. Malignancies): CLABSI is the most common cause due to central venous lines.

📋 Risk Factors for UTI in Children 2–24 Months of Age

Female Risk Factors

  • White race
  • Age < 1 year
  • Temperature $\ge 39.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($102.2^\circ\text{F}$)
  • Fever duration $\ge 2$ days
  • No obvious source of infection

Male Risk Factors

  • Uncircumcised boys at higher risk
  • Non-black race
  • Temperature $\ge 39.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($102.2^\circ\text{F}$)
  • Fever duration > 1 day
  • No obvious source of infection

II. Streptococcal Infections

S. PNEUMONIAE, S. PYOGENES (GAS), GBS

🧫 Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcus)

Gram-positive, lancet-shaped, encapsulated diplococcus operating singly or in short chains. Over 91 serotypes identified based on polysaccharide capsular antigens.

Epidemiology & Serotypes

  • Reservoir: Human nasopharynx (>90% carriage in children 6 mos – 5 yrs).
  • Transmission: Direct contact and respiratory droplets.
  • PH Top Serotypes: 6, 18, and 14.
  • US Top Serotypes: 4, 6B, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F, 23F.
  • #1 cause of bacteremia, bacterial pneumonia, otitis media, and bacterial meningitis in children.

Risk Factors for Invasive Disease (IPD)

  • Children < 2 years: Poor antibody production against T-cell independent antigens.
  • Asplenia / Sickle Cell: 30 to 100-fold increased risk due to deficient opsonization and absence of splenic clearance.
  • Immunocompromised: HIV, nephrotic syndrome, chemotherapy.
  • Viral Precursor: Influenza/viral infection damages ciliary activity and alveolar macrophages.
💡 Clinical Manifestations & CXR Findings
Memory Aid: BOMP (Bacteremia, Otitis Media, Meningitis, Pneumonia).
On Chest X-Ray, S. pneumoniae classically presents as a dense lobar consolidation known as "Round Pneumonia". Contrast this with S. aureus pneumonia, which causes pneumatoceles (thin-walled air-filled cavitary lesions).

🔴 Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus / GAS)

Virulence depends primarily on the M protein (antiphagocytic). Pyrogenic exotoxins (speA) cause scarlet fever.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Acute Pharyngitis (Strep Throat): IP 2-5 days. Tonsillar exudates, palatal petechiae. NOT associated with cough, coryza, diarrhea, or mouth ulcers (viral clues!).
  • Scarlet Fever: Sandpaper rash (diffuse, fine papular), Pastia's lines (accentuated skin creases in elbow/axilla/groin), Strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, and desquamation of palms/soles in 6-9 days.
  • Impetigo (Superficial Pyoderma): Non-bullous impetigo presents with honey-colored crusts.
  • Erysipelas: Infection of deeper skin layers with a sharply defined, slightly elevated border.
  • Invasive Disease: Streptococcal Toxic Shock Syndrome & Necrotizing Fasciitis.

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Gold Standard: Throat swab culture.
  • Serology: ASO & Anti-DNase B titers (requires $\ge 4$-fold rise between acute and convalescent phase). Do not use for acute pharyngitis diagnosis!
  • Drug of Choice: Oral Penicillin V x 10 days OR Single Benzathine Penicillin G IM (600,000 IU for $\le 60$ lb; 1.2M IU for > 60 lb).
  • Penicillin Allergy: Oral Cephalosporins, Clindamycin, or Macrolides (Erythromycin).

👶 Group B Streptococcus (S. agalactiae)

Major cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis. Serotypes Ia, III, V.

  • Risk Factors: Heavy maternal colonization, Prolonged Rupture of Membranes (PROM), intrapartum fever, maternal bacteriuria.
  • Empiric Therapy: Ampicillin + Aminoglycoside (Gentamicin).
  • Duration: 10 days for bacteremia; 2–3 weeks for meningitis.

💚 Viridans Streptococci

Alpha-hemolytic streptococci that are part of the normal oral flora. Most common cause of subacute bacterial endocarditis in patients with underlying structural heart disease.

III. Staphylococcal Infections

S. AUREUS, CONS, TOXIN SYNDROMES

🟡 Staphylococcus aureus Pathogenesis & Spectrum

Coagulase-positive gram-positive cocci in clusters. 20–40% of normal individuals carry S. aureus in the anterior nares. Transmitted via autoinoculation or direct contact. Major risk factor is disruption of intact skin.

Local Suppurative Infections

  • Impetigo: Honey-colored crusts.
  • Folliculitis / Furuncle / Carbuncle
  • Abscess: Fluctuant pus collection with inflammatory capsule. Hallmark of staph infection!

Disseminated Infections

  • Lungs: Pneumonia with pneumatoceles, empyema.
  • CNS: Brain abscess.
  • CVS: Infective endocarditis (vegetations).
  • Musculoskeletal: Osteomyelitis, septic arthritis.

Toxin-Mediated Diseases

  • SSSS (Exfoliatin): Stratum granulosum cleavage, Nikolsky sign (+).
  • TSS (TSST-1): Sudden fever, rash, hypotension, multi-organ failure.
  • Food Poisoning: Preformed enterotoxin, rapid onset (hours), self-limiting.

🧬 Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci (CoNS)

S. epidermidis

Normal skin flora that forms an exopolysaccharide slime biofilm layer. Enhances adhesion to medical devices (catheters, shunts, prosthetic valves). Resists phagocytosis and antibiotic penetration.

S. saprophyticus & S. haemolyticus

S. saprophyticus: Common cause of UTI in sexually active young females.
S. haemolyticus: Nosocomial UTI in patients with indwelling urinary catheters.

IV & V. Haemophilus influenzae & Neisseria Infections

HIB, N. MENINGITIDIS, N. GONORRHOEAE

🛡️ Haemophilus influenzae

Small, fastidious Gram-negative coccobacilli with 6 capsular polysaccharide types (a–f). Type b (Hib) caused >90% of invasive disease prior to widespread vaccination.

Clinical Manifestations & Radiograph Findings

  • Supraglottitis / Acute Epiglottitis: Medical emergency! Presents with high fever, stridor, drooling, tripod positioning. Lateral neck radiograph shows classic "Thumb Sign".
  • Cellulitis: Preseptal and cheek cellulitis presenting with purple/bluish discoloration without prior trauma.
  • Orbital Cellulitis: Ethmoid/sphenoid sinus complication. Distinguished by proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and painful eye movement.
  • Meningitis: High risk of neurological sequelae (hearing loss!). Administer Dexamethasone (0.6 mg/kg/day q6h) to reduce hearing impairment.
  • Otitis Media & Sinusitis: Caused predominantly by Nontypeable H. influenzae. DOC: High-dose Amoxicillin (80–90 mg/kg/day).

Treatment & Chemoprophylaxis

  • Parenteral Invasive Disease: Ampicillin (susceptible) or Ceftriaxone / Cefotaxime (Ampicillin-resistant).
  • Oral Less Severe Disease: Amoxicillin or Amox-Clav / 2nd-3rd gen Cephalosporins.
  • Chemoprophylaxis: Rifampin 20 mg/kg/day PO once daily for 4 days (max 600 mg/day). Given to close household contacts if unimmunized children < 48 months reside in the house.

⚡ Neisseria meningitidis (Meningococcus)

Aerobic Gram-negative diplococcus possessing a polysaccharide capsule. 13 serogroups; major invasive groups are A, B, C, W, X, Y.

Serogroup Characteristics

Serogroup A: Most common cause of epidemics.
Serogroup B: Most common cause of sporadic disease.
Serogroup Y: Associated with pneumonia.

Pathophysiology & Endotoxin

LPS endotoxin causes microvascular injury, severe capillary leak, pathologic vasoconstriction/vasodilation, DIC, and myocardial failure.

Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome

Dreaded fulminant meningococcemia complication featuring purpura fulminans, DIC, shock, and bilateral adrenal hemorrhage.

🌌 Neisseria gonorrhoeae

Gram-negative intracellular diplococci. Co-infection with Chlamydia trachomatis is extremely high; always co-treat!

Clinical Syndromes

  • Urethritis / Cervicitis: Profuse purulent yellowish discharge with dysuria.
  • Ophthalmia Neonatorum: Severe conjunctivitis appearing 1–4 days after birth.
  • Disseminated Infection (DGI): Fever, tenosynovitis, polyarthralgia, petechial/pustular acral skin lesions.

Management & Prophylaxis

  • Neonatal Ophthalmia Prophylaxis: 0.5% Erythromycin eye ointment at birth.
  • Neonatal Ophthalmia Treatment: Ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg IM single dose (max 125 mg).
  • Uncomplicated Gonorrhea Rx: Ceftriaxone IM + Azithromycin PO (to cover Chlamydia).

VI & VII. Diphtheria & Tetanus

TOXIN-MEDIATED BACTERIAL DISEASES

🦠 Corynebacterium diphtheriae

Gram-positive non-motile bacillus. Pathogenicity is driven by a 62-kDa exotoxin (encoded by bacteriophage lysogeny) that inhibits protein synthesis via inactivation of Elongation Factor-2 (EF-2).

Clinical Presentation

  • Respiratory Diphtheria: IP 2-4 days. Sore throat, low fever, followed by a gray-brown leather-like pseudomembrane over tonsils/pharynx that bleeds upon manipulation.
  • "Bull Neck": Massive cervical lymphadenopathy and soft tissue edema leading to airway obstruction.
  • Cutaneous Diphtheria: Non-healing punched-out ecthymatous ulcer with a gray membrane.

Complications & Isolation Culture

  • Toxic Cardiomyopathy: Most common cause of death! Occurs in 2nd–3rd week as throat improves. Manifests with AV block, dysrhythmias, VTach.
  • Toxic Neuropathy: Soft palate paralysis, hypesthesia, cranial nerve palsies.
  • Culture Media: Cystine-Tellurite Blood Agar (CTBA) or Tinsdale Agar.
⭐ Diphtheria Management Triad
  1. Diphtheria Antitoxin (DAT): Mainstay of therapy! Single IV dose (20,000 to 100,000 units) to neutralize circulating un-bound toxin.
  2. Antimicrobial Therapy: Erythromycin (40–50 mg/kg/day PO q6h x 14 days) OR Penicillin G (14 days). Document clearance with 2 consecutive negative cultures 24 hours apart.
  3. Prophylaxis for Exposed Contacts: Single IM Benzathine Penicillin G or Erythromycin x 10 days. Vaccination during convalescence (disease does not confer lifelong immunity!).

🧱 Clostridium tetani

Gram-positive, spore-forming, obligate anaerobic rod. Produces tetanospasmin, which binds gangliosides at the neuromuscular junction and retrogradely travels to spinal cord inhibitory interneurons, blocking the release of GABA and Glycine.

Generalized Tetanus

• Trismus (Lockjaw): Spasm of masseter muscle (#1 presenting sign).
• Risus Sardonicus: Facial muscle spasm ("sneering smile").
• Opisthotonos: Severe hyperextension of spine/neck.
• Autonomic instability (labile BP, diaphoresis).

Localized & Cephalic

• Localized: Painful spasms adjacent to injury site.
• Cephalic Tetanus: Rare form involving cranial nerves; classically associated with Chronic Otitis Media (COM)!

Management Principles

• Airway protection (Tracheostomy).
• Dark, quiet room (avoid spasm triggers).
• TIG (Tetanus Immunoglobulin): 500 Units IM.
• Metronidazole: 30 mg/kg/day q6h x 7–10 days.

VIII, IX, X, XI. Pertussis & Spirochetes / Typhoid

B. PERTUSSIS, LEPTOSPIRA, S. TYPHI, T. PALLIDUM

🌬️ Bordetella pertussis (Whooping Cough)

Small, fastidious Gram-negative coccobacillus. Attaches to ciliated respiratory epithelium via filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA) and pertactin.

Stage Duration Clinical Features Diagnostic & Transmissibility Highlights
1. Catarrhal Stage 1–2 weeks Coryza, rhinorrhea, low fever, lacrimation. Mimics common cold. MOST CONTAGIOUS STAGE! Highest culture yield. Shows marked leukocytosis with absolute lymphocytosis (15,000–100,000 /$\mu L$).
2. Paroxysmal Stage 2–6 weeks Paroxysmal dry coughing fits ending in an inspiratory whoop, posttussive emesis, cyanosis, subconjunctival hemorrhages. In infants < 3 months, classic whoop may be absent; present with apnea & cyanosis instead.
3. Convalescent Stage > 2 weeks (months) Gradual reduction in cough frequency and intensity. Coughing fits may recur with subsequent viral respiratory infections.
⚠️ Infant Pertussis Antibiotic Warning
Drug of Choice: Macrolides (Azithromycin).
In neonates < 1 month of age, Azithromycin (10 mg/kg/day x 5 days) is the ONLY recommended agent. Erythromycin is avoided due to its strong association with Infantile Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (IHPS)! TMP-SMX is contraindicated in < 2 months (risk of kernicterus).

🌊 Leptospirosis (Leptospira interrogans)

Thin helix-shaped spirochete transmitted via direct contact of broken skin/mucosa with floodwaters or soil contaminated with rodent urine.

Biphasic Clinical Course

  • 1. Septicemic Phase (Days 1–7): Abrupt high remittent fever, severe headache, gastrocnemius/myalgia tenderness, and conjunctival suffusion. Spirochetes present in blood & CSF.
  • 2. Immune Phase (Days 4–30): Disappearance of spirochetes from blood; appearance of IgM antibodies. Aseptic meningitis and leptospiruria.
  • Weil Syndrome (Icteric Leptospirosis): Severe form (10% cases) featuring triad of Jaundice, Renal failure, and Hemorrhagic pneumonitis / DIC.

Diagnosis & Management

  • Gold Standard Diagnostic: Microscopic Agglutination Test (MAT).
  • Severe Illness Rx: Penicillin G IV (6–8 M U/m²/day) or Ceftriaxone IV x 7 days.
  • Mild Illness Rx: Oral Doxycycline, Amoxicillin, or Ampicillin.
  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Doxycycline 4 mg/kg single dose (Max: 200 mg) regardless of age, taken within 24–72 hours of floodwater exposure.

🌾 Typhoid Fever (Salmonella Typhi)

  • Clinical Course: Sustained/remittent step-ladder fever, abdominal pain, hepatosplenomegaly, relative bradycardia (Faget sign).
  • Rose Spots: Discrete salmon-colored macules appearing on trunk during 2nd week.
  • Diagnosis: Blood culture (+ in 1st week; gold standard early). Stool/urine (+ in 2nd–3rd week). Bone marrow culture is most sensitive. Widal test is obsolete due to low specificity.
  • Treatment: Oral Chloramphenicol/Amoxicillin (uncomplicated); IV Ceftriaxone (admitted/severe).

📜 Syphilis (Treponema pallidum)

  • Secondary Syphilis: Diffuse maculopapular rash on palms and soles, condyloma lata.
  • Serologic Testing: Non-treponemal (VDRL/RPR) wane after treatment (used for monitoring); Treponemal (FTA-Abs, TPHA) remain positive for life (confirmatory).
  • Congenital Syphilis Rx: Aqueous Crystalline Penicillin G IV (200,000–300,000 U/kg/day div q4-6h x 10 days).
  • Primary/Secondary Rx: Benzathine Penicillin G IM single dose (50,000 U/kg up to 2.4M U).
