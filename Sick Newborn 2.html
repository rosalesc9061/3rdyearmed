💩 Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC)

Definition: Most common life-threatening GIT emergency in newborns, characterized by mucosal or transmural necrosis of the intestine.

Epidemiology: Occurs in 5-10% of infants with birth weight <1500g. Overall mortality is 20-30%, reaching up to 50% in infants requiring surgical intervention.

Predilection Sites: Distal ileum and proximal colon (watershed areas of gut perfusion). NEC Totalis refers to extensive gangrene from stomach to rectum.

⭐ MUST KNOW — RISK FACTORS
3 Major Risk Factors:
  1. Prematurity: Single greatest risk factor (>90% of cases).
  2. Bacterial Colonization of the Gut: Microbial dysbiosis.
  3. Formula Feeding: Exposure to enteral substrate in context of gut immaturity.
In Term Infants: Occurs secondary to birth asphyxia, Down syndrome, Congenital Heart Disease (CHD), Rotavirus infection, Hirschsprung disease, or Gastroschisis.

Most Common Late Sequela: Intestinal Stricture formation is the most common long-term complication following medical recovery from NEC. Observe closely on follow-up!

💩 Meconium Disorders

1. Meconium Plug Syndrome

Transient functional immaturity of the colon leading to delayed passage of meconium. Considered a benign variation of Hirschsprung disease. Presents with abdominal distention and bilious emesis. AXR shows multiple dilated bowel loops without rectal gas. Usually resolves after enema.

2. Meconium Ileus

Seen in 90% of infants with Cystic Fibrosis. Thicker, hyperviscid meconium obstructs the distal ileum. AXR shows distended intestinal loops without fluid levels, displaying a characteristic granular or "bubbly" / "ground-glass" appearance. Therapeutic contrast/barium enema relieves obstruction in ~60% of cases.

3. Meconium Peritonitis

In utero intestinal perforation with leakage of meconium into the peritoneal cavity causing chemical peritonitis and peritoneal calcifications. Often heals spontaneously in utero. Surgery is indicated if persistent obstruction, persistent peritonitis/sepsis, or abdominal mass is present.

🟡 Neonatal Hyperbilirubinemia & Jaundice

Clinical jaundice is visible when serum bilirubin exceeds 2 mg/dL in adults and >5 mg/dL in newborns. Occurs in 60% of term and 80% of preterm neonates.

🧠 Kernicterus (Bilirubin Encephalopathy)

Bilirubin-induced brain dysfunction secondary to unconjugated bilirubin deposition in deep brain structures when TSB >20 mg/dL in term infants (lower in preterms).

Stained Brain Regions: Basal ganglia, Hippocampal cortex, Subthalamic nucleus, Cerebellum. Cerebral cortex is SPARED!

Clinical Progression:

  • Phase I (Days 1-2): Poor suck, stupor, hypotonia, seizures.
  • Phase II (Middle of Wk 1): Extensor muscle hypertonia, opisthotonos, retrocollis, fever.
  • Phase III (After Wk 1): Hypertonia, rigidity.
  • Chronic Form: Choreoathetosis, sensorineural hearing loss, upward gaze palsy, dental enamel dysplasia.

💡 Treatment Modalities

  • Phototherapy: Blue light (420-470 nm) converts UCB into water-soluble isomers:
    • Configurational Photoisomerization: Reversible 4Z-15Z to 4Z-5E.
    • Structural Photoisomerization: Forms Lumibilirubin (Irreversible, main excretory pathway).
    Complication: Bronze Baby Syndrome (if direct hyperbilirubinemia present).
    Contraindication: Porphyria.
  • Exchange Transfusion: Indicated if intensive phototherapy fails or TSB ≥25 mg/dL with signs of acute encephalopathy.
  • IVIG: Adjunctive therapy for isoimmune hemolytic disease (ABO/Rh) when TSB approaches exchange levels.

2. Neonatal Polycythemia

Defined as Venous Hemoglobin >20 g/dL or Venous Hematocrit >65%. Capillary blood samples overestimate hematocrit; must confirm with central venous draw.

Primary Cause: Increased fetal EPO (IUGR, IDM, Trisomy 21, Beckwith-Wiedemann, hypoxia).

Secondary Cause: Excessive fetal transfusion (Twin-to-twin, delayed cord clamping).

Treatment: Partial Volume Exchange Transfusion (target desired Hct 55-60%).

Partial Exchange Volume Formula:
$$\text{Volume Exchanged (mL)} = \frac{\text{Observed Hct} - \text{Desired Hct}}{\text{Observed Hct}} \times \text{Infant Blood Volume}$$ (Term Blood Vol = 80 mL/kg; Preterm = 100 mL/kg)

3. Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB)

Vitamin K is required for γ-carboxylation of coagulation factors II, VII, IX, X (1972) and Proteins C, S, Z.

Clinical Features of IDM: LGA / Macrosomia (>4300g), tachypnea, jitteriness, tremors, lethargy, seizures, polycythemia, hyperbilirubinemia, hypocalcemia.

Specific Congenital Anomalies: Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy (asymmetric septal hypertrophy) and Caudal Regression Syndrome (Sacral Agenesis).

Management: Symptomatic: 10% Dextrose IV bolus 2 mL/kg → IV Dextrose infusion 4-10 mg/kg/min (target >40 mg/dL). Resistant cases: Hydrocortisone, Glucagon, or Diazoxide.

3. Neonatal Hypocalcemia

Early Hypocalcemia (<72 hours): Caused by prematurity, asphyxia, IDM, maternal hyperparathyroidism.

Late Hypocalcemia (>72 hours): Caused by cow's milk formula (high phosphate), hypoparathyroidism (DiGeorge syndrome), hypomagnesemia.

Signs & Symptoms: Jitteriness, high-pitched cry, tetany, Chvostek/Trousseau signs, seizures, prolonged QTc interval on ECG.

🦠 Neonatal Sepsis

Systemic infection in the first month of life. Sepsis is the 2nd leading cause of neonatal death in the Philippines (12.4%), behind prematurity (13%).

⚠️ COMMON PITFALL
Ceftriaxone Contraindication: Ceftriaxone must be strictly avoided in neonates with sepsis and hyperbilirubinemia because it displaces bilirubin from albumin (increasing kernicterus risk) and precipitates as calcium-ceftriaxone crystals in lungs/kidneys! Use Cefotaxime instead.

🚨 SIRS Criteria in Neonates

Requires ≥2 of the following conditions:

  • Temperature Instability: <35.0°C (95°F) or >38.5°C (101.3°F).
  • Respiratory Dysfunction: Tachypnea (>2 SD above mean), Apnea, or Hypoxemia (PaO2 <70 mmHg).
  • Cardiac Dysfunction: Tachycardia (>2 SD above mean), CRT >3 seconds, or Hypotension.
  • Perfusion Abnormalities: Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/hr), lactic acidosis, or altered mental status.

🔪 The Surgical Neonate

1. Congenital Hyperplastic Pyloric Stenosis

Male-to-female ratio 5:1. Associated with blood groups O and B. Presents in 1st few weeks of life with non-bilious projectile vomiting, dehydration, and palpable olive-shaped epigastric mass.

Metabolic Disturbance: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Diagnostics: Ultrasound shows Bull's Eye / Target Sign. Barium swallow shows String Sign. Treatment: Ramstedt Pyloromyotomy.

2. Esophageal Atresia (EA) & Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF)

Associated with VACTERL anomalies in 30-40%. 5 anatomical varieties:

  • Type C (85% - Most Common): Proximal Esophageal Atresia with Distal TEF.
  • H-Type TEF: Rare; presents with coughing/choking during feeds and recurrent aspiration pneumonia.

Presentation: Excessive drooling, choking, inability to pass OG tube into stomach.

3. Duodenal Atresia

Failure of intestinal recanalization during 8-10th week GA. Strongly associated with Trisomy 21 (31%) and CHD (30%). Antenatal USG reveals polyhydramnios. Postnatal presentation: Bilious emesis within 24 hours of birth. AXR reveals pathognomonic Double Bubble Sign.

4. Hirschsprung Disease (Congenital Aganglionic Megacolon)

Failure of neural crest cell migration cranial-to-caudal. Absence of intramural ganglion cells in Meissner (submucosal) and Auerbach (myenteric) plexuses. Male:Female 4:1. 80% limited to rectosigmoid.

Clinical Signs: Failure to pass meconium within 48 hours, bilious emesis, abdominal distention relieved by digital rectal examination (explosive release of gas/stool).

Gold Standard Diagnostic: Rectal Suction Biopsy (shows absent ganglion cells and hypertrophic nerve trunks with elevated acetylcholinesterase).

🧬 Dysmorphology & Clinical Genetics

1 in 40 newborns (2.5%) has a recognizable structural birth defect. 20-30% of infant deaths are attributed to congenital anomalies.

🔍 Major vs. Minor Anomalies

Major Anomaly: Structural defect with surgical, medical, or cosmetic significance (e.g., CHD, cleft lip, neural tube defect).

Minor Anomaly: Morphological variation of little/no medical significance present in <4% of population (e.g., single palmar crease, 5th finger clinodactyly, preauricular pit, epicanthic folds).

⭐ RED FLAG
Presence of >3 minor anomalies or >1 major anomaly strongly suggests an underlying genetic syndrome!

📚 Multiple Anomaly Patterns

  • Syndrome: Pattern of multiple anomalies related by unified pathophysiology and single defined etiology (e.g., Trisomy 21).
  • Sequence: Cascading series of secondary defects arising from a single initial primary error (e.g., Pierre Robin Sequence: Micrognathia → Glossoptosis → Cleft Palate).
  • Association: Non-random occurrence of a combination of anomalies that occur together more frequently than chance, without a single known cause (e.g., VACTERL Association: Vertebral, Anal, Cardiac, TEF, Renal, Limb).

⚡ Rapid Fire Buzzword Flash Points

  • NEC Watershed Areas: Distal ileum and proximal colon.
  • NEC Medically Treated Sequela: Intestinal Stricture.
  • Phototherapy Wavelength: Blue light (420-470 nm).
  • Phototherapy Main Structural Isomer: Lumibilirubin (Irreversible).
  • Kernicterus Cortical Sparing: Cerebral cortex is SPARED; basal ganglia affected.
  • RhoGAM Timing: 28 weeks GA and within 72 hours post-delivery for Rh(-) mothers.
  • Polycythemia Diagnostic Cutoff: Venous Hct >65% or Venous Hb >20 g/dL.
  • Vitamin K Coagulation Profile: Prolonged PT, Normal PTT, Normal Platelets.
  • EOS vs LOS Cutoff: 7 days (or 72 hours in NICU).
  • EOS Primary Antibiotic Regimen: Ampicillin + Gentamicin.
  • Avoid Ceftriaxone in Neonates: Displaces bilirubin, forms calcium-ceftriaxone crystals.
  • Pyloric Stenosis Metabolic Acid-Base: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.
