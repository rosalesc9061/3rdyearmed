🎯 Core Learning Objectives

  • Formulate accurate empiric and definitive antimicrobial regimens according to DOH National Antibiotic Guidelines 2018 and lecture specifications.
  • Distinguish surgical emergencies (e.g., Necrotizing Fasciitis, Pneumoperitoneum, Left Lobe Liver Abscess, Valve Dehiscence) from medical conditions.

📌 Key SSTI Entities Breakdown
  • Impetigo: Nonbullous (GAS / S. pyogenes - honey-colored crusts) vs Bullous (S. aureus - exotoxin degrades epidermal layer).
  • Erysipelas: Superficial dermis infection caused by GAS. Characterized by well-demarcated, raised, indurated margins. Common on facial areas in pediatrics.
  • Cellulitis: Deeper dermis/subcutaneous fat involvement. Characterized by poorly demarcated erythema, warmth, swelling, and systemic symptoms (fever, malaise).
  • Folliculitis / Furuncle / Carbuncle: Hair follicle infections predominantly caused by S. aureus. Carbuncles are clusters of furuncles common in diabetics.
  • Pyomyositis: Pus accumulation within muscle tissue, predominantly caused by S. aureus. Non-purulent myositis is associated with GAS or Coxsackie B virus.
⭐ Must Know: Erysipelas vs Cellulitis Margins
Erysipelas is superficial, presenting with sharply demarcated, well-defined indurated borders.
Cellulitis involves deeper subcutaneous tissue, presenting with poorly demarcated, diffuse borders.
💡 Clinical Pearl: Purulent vs Non-Purulent
Staphylococcus aureus = Pus-forming (abscesses, furuncles, pyomyositis, purulent cellulitis).
Streptococcus pyogenes (GAS) = Non-pus-forming (spreading non-purulent cellulitis, erysipelas).
⚠️ Necrotizing Fasciitis (Myonecrosis / Gas Gangrene) Surgical Emergency

A rapidly progressive, life-threatening infection of the fascial planes and subcutaneous tissue driven by bacterial exotoxins.

Pathophysiology Chain: Mucosal/Skin Breach → Exotoxin release (PVL, Streptolysin, SPE) → Subcutaneous fat degradation → Vascular thrombosis & hypercoagulable state → Ischemia & Tissue Necrosis → Skin color progression (Erythema → Violaceous → Dark Bronze) → Gas/Bullae formation & Septic Shock.
Clinical Presentation & Diagnostic Clues
  • Hallmark Clue: Severe pain OUT OF PROPORTION to physical examination findings.
  • Discoloration: Rapid progression from red to violaceous, then dark bronze-colored patches.
  • Bullae & Crepitus: Bullae (seropurulent or hemorrhagic) and gas production (crepitus on palpation).
  • Systemic Toxicity: High-grade fever, confusion, obtundation, rapid hemodynamic collapse.
  • Laboratory Clues: Markedly elevated WBC with neutrophilia, hyponatremia, decreased Hb, and elevated Serum Creatinine (sepsis-associated AKI).
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Diagnostic Delay
Necrotizing fasciitis is a surgical diagnosis! Never delay urgent surgical exploration and debridement while waiting for imaging (MRI/CT).
🧠 Exam Tip: Why Clindamycin in Type 2 GAS?
In Type 2 (GAS) Necrotizing Fasciitis, Clindamycin is added to Penicillin G NOT just for bactericidal action, but to suppress exotoxin synthesis (PVL & SPE) at the ribosomal level!

🐾 Pathogen Clues & Bite Management (DOH Guidelines 2018)
Environmental Exposure Clues
  • Freshwater exposure + trauma: Aeromonas hydrophila (aggressive necrotizing cellulitis).
  • Saltwater / Marine / Shellfish: Vibrio vulnificus (bullous skin lesions, necrotizing vasculitis, septic shock).
  • Fisherman / Handling Fish/Poultry: Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae. Exam Key: Naturally resistant to Vancomycin, Cotrimoxazole, and Chloramphenicol!
  • Neutropenic patient (ANC < 500): Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Ecthyma gangrenosum, hot-tub folliculitis).
  • Penetrating nail injury through sneaker: Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
  • Black eschar skin ulcer: Bacillus anthracis.
  • Swimmer's itch (Samar, Leyte, Agusan): Schistosoma spp.
Bite Infection Principles
  • Cat Bites: 80% infection rate! Rapid onset (<24h). Dominant pathogen: Pasteurella multocida.
    CRITICAL RESISTANCE: P. multocida is RESISTANT to Dicloxacillin, Cephalexin, and Clindamycin!
  • Dog Bites: Only 5% infected. Treat if severe or in diabetics. Pathogens: Pasteurella canis, Capnocytophaga, anaerobes.
  • Human Bites: High risk of deep tissue/tendon space infection. Viridans streptococci (100%), Eikenella corrodens (15%).
    EIKENELLA RESISTANCE: Resistant to Clindamycin, Metronidazole, Nafcillin, Cephalexin, Cotrimoxazole! Sensitive to Beta-lactamase inhibitors & Fluoroquinolones.
  • Rat Bites: Spirillum minus, Streptobacillus moniliformis. Causes Rat Bite Fever. Note: Rabies post-exposure prophylaxis is NOT indicated for rat bites!

⭐ Must Know: Toxin vs Invasive Diarrhea
Vomiting within 1-6 hours is driven by preformed bacterial exotoxins. Antibiotics are completely useless against preformed toxins! Management is strictly volume replacement.
🧠 Exam Tip: Specific Parasitic Clues
• Giardia lamblia: Hikers drinking stream water; greasy, foul-smelling, bulky, whitish/soap-like stool with bloating. DOC: Metronidazole.
• Cryptosporidium: Immunocompromised / PLHIV with low CD4; voluminous watery diarrhea. Chlorine resistant.
🧫 Diagnostic Stool Examination & Selective Culture Media
Stool Findings & Localization
  • Non-Inflammatory (No Fecal WBCs): Proximal small bowel location. Watery stool. Pathogens: V. cholerae, ETEC, S. aureus, B. cereus, Viruses (Norovirus, Rotavirus), Giardia.
  • Inflammatory (Fecal WBCs / Neutrophils Present): Colon / Distal ileum. Mucosal destruction & invasion. Pathogens: Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni, C. difficile, E. histolytica.
  • Pseudoappendicitis: Yersinia enterocolitica causes RLQ ileocecal colitis mimicking acute appendicitis.
  • Tenesmus (Painful rectal spasms): Hallmark of Shigellosis or Amebic colitis.

DOH 2018 Empiric Antibiotic Regimens for Infectious Diarrhea
  • Mild (<3 unformed stools/day): Oral rehydration therapy alone.
  • Moderate (3-4 unformed stools/day with symptoms): Oral or parenteral hydration.
  • Severe / Inflammatory / Dysentery (Fever, tenesmus, blood, fecal WBCs):
    • Shigella / Bacillary Dysentery: Ciprofloxacin 500mg PO q12h OR Levofloxacin 500mg PO q24h x 3-5 days.
    • Campylobacter jejuni (SE Asia travel): Azithromycin 500mg PO q24h x 3 days (Preferred due to high fluoroquinolone resistance in SE Asia).
🌊 Spontaneous / Primary Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP / PBP) Monomicrobial Entity

Infection of the peritoneal fluid without any evident intra-abdominal surface rupture or viscus perforation. Occurs predominantly in patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites.

Diagnostic Criteria & Paracentesis
  • Gold Standard Diagnostic Test: Diagnostic Paracentesis.
  • Diagnostic Cutoff: Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) ≥ 250 PMNs/µL.
    Formula: ANC = Total WBC count × % PMNs
  • Microbiology: Almost strictly MONOMICROBIAL. Dominant pathogens are Gram-negative enteric bacilli: E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae.
⭐ MUST KNOW EXAM FACT: NO Anaerobic Coverage in SBP!
Unlike secondary peritonitis, SBP DOES NOT require anaerobic coverage.
Why? Peritoneal ascitic fluid possesses intrinsic bacteriostatic activity against Bacteroides fragilis, and the partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) in ascitic fluid is too high for obligate anaerobes to survive.
SBP Diagnostic Variants
Variant Entity Culture ANC Cutoff Management
Classic SBP Positive ≥ 250 PMNs/µL Treat immediately
Culture-Negative Neutrocytic Ascites Negative ≥ 250 PMNs/µL Treat immediately
Monomicrobial Non-neutrocytic Bacterascites Positive (1 organism) < 250 PMNs/µL Treat IF symptomatic; Observe if asymptomatic
Polymicrobial Bacterascites Multiple organisms Variable Suspicious for traumatic tap or Secondary Peritonitis! Work up perforation.
Empiric Treatment (DOH 2018)
  • First-line Therapy: Cefotaxime 2g IV q4-8h OR Ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h x 5 days.
    Caution: Ceftriaxone may induce biliary sludge and worsen cholestatic jaundice in severe cirrhosis. Cefotaxime is preferred.
  • If Penicillin/Cephalosporin Allergic: Ampicillin-sulbactam, Piperacillin-tazobactam, or Ertapenem.
Indications for SBP Prophylaxis
💡 High-Yield Prophylaxis Indications
1. Previous episode of SBP (Secondary prophylaxis).
2. Low protein ascites (<15 g/L).
3. Cirrhosis + Acute Upper GI / Variceal Bleeding (Norfloxacin 400mg PO bid or Ceftriaxone 1g IV daily x 7 days).
4. Advanced Liver Failure (Child-Pugh > 9, Bilirubin > 3 mg/dL) + Renal Dysfunction (Cr > 1.2, BUN > 25, Na < 130).
• Prophylactic Drugs: Norfloxacin 400mg PO daily, Ciprofloxacin, or Co-trimoxazole.
⚡ Secondary Peritonitis & Intra-Abdominal Abscesses
Secondary Peritonitis Principles
  • Caused by direct acute contamination via perforated hollow viscus, trauma, or surgical breakdown.
  • Radiologic Pathognomonic Sign: Pneumoperitoneum (Air under the diaphragm on upright chest X-ray) = Immediate Surgical Emergency!
  • Microbiology: Strictly POLYMICROBIAL. Mixed facultative Gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella) + Anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis).
  • Empiric Treatment: Surgical source control + Broad-spectrum coverage. Pip-Tazo OR 3rd Gen Cephalosporin / Fluoroquinolone PLUS Metronidazole (to add B. fragilis coverage!).
Pathophysiology of Intra-Abdominal Abscesses
Bacterial Contamination → Intense Local Inflammation → Coagulation Cascade Activation → Fibrin Localization → Tissue Ischemia & Liquefactive Necrosis → Encapsulated Fluid Collection.

Why Antibiotics Alone Fail: High bacterial load, necrotic debris, acidic pH inactivating drugs, and the thick fibrin capsule preventing antibiotic penetration.

🫀 Pathophysiology & Clinical Manifestations of Infective Endocarditis
Pathogenesis Sequence: Endothelial Injury (turbulence, VSD, RHD) → Exposure of Subendothelial Tissue Factor → Platelet & Fibrin Deposition → Non-Bacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE) → Transient Bacteremia → Bacterial Adherence & Colonization → Mature Vegetation Formation → Local Tissue Destruction & Distal Septic Embolization.
Core Pathogen Associations
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Most virulent pathogen. Sole Exception: Can infect NORMAL, UNDAMAGED heart valves! Forms large vegetations (≥10mm) with high risk of distal septic arterial embolization. Common in IV drug users (Tricuspid valve).
  • Viridans Group Streptococci: Subacute presentation on pre-damaged or rheumatic valves following dental procedures or oral manipulation.
  • Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis): Strong, high-yield association with UNDERLYING COLONIC MALIGNANCY / COLON CANCER! Colonoscopy with biopsy indicated.
  • Enterococcus faecalis: Associated with urologic manipulation, catheterization, or lower GI endoscopy in elderly patients.
  • HACEK Group: Fastidious Gram-negative bacilli from oral flora, associated with culture-negative endocarditis.
Peripheral Manifestations & Emboli
  • Janeway Lesions: Non-tender, erythematous macules on palms and soles (septic microemboli, strongly associated with S. aureus).
  • Osler Nodes: Painful, tender, raised erythematous nodules on the pads of fingers and toes (immunologic complex deposition).
  • Roth Spots: Retinal hemorrhages with pale, central microinfarcts (immunologic).
  • Splinter Hemorrhages: Subungual dark linear streaks on nail beds.
  • Septic Pulmonary Infarcts: Right-sided (tricuspid) IE in IVDU presenting with cough, hemoptysis, and chest pain.
📋 Modified Duke Criteria (2000) for Infective Endocarditis
MAJOR CRITERIA
  • 1. Positive Blood Cultures for IE:
    • Typical microorganism from 2 separate blood cultures (Viridans strep, S. gallolyticus, HACEK, S. aureus, community Enterococci); OR
    • Persistently positive blood cultures (≥2 cultures drawn >12 hours apart, or majority of ≥4 separate cultures).
  • 2. Evidence of Endocardial Involvement:
    • Positive Echocardiogram (oscillating intracardiac mass/vegetation on valve, perivalvular abscess, or new partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve); OR
    • New valvular regurgitation murmur on physical exam; OR
    • Abnormal activity around prosthetic valve site on 18F-FDG PET/CT or cardiac CT.
MINOR CRITERIA
  1. Predisposition: Predisposing heart condition (RHD, VSD, prosthetic valve) or IV drug use.
  2. Fever: Temperature ≥ 38.0°C (100.4°F).
  3. Vascular Phenomena: Major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial hemorrhage, Janeway lesions.
  4. Immunologic Phenomena: Glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, positive Rheumatoid Factor.
  5. Microbiologic Evidence: Positive blood culture not meeting major criterion or serologic evidence of active infection.
⭐ Diagnostic Cutoffs for Modified Duke Criteria
• DEFINITIVE IE: 2 Major Criteria OR 1 Major + 3 Minor Criteria OR 5 Minor Criteria.
• POSSIBLE IE: 1 Major + 1 Minor Criterion OR 3 Minor Criteria.
• REJECTED IE: Firm alternative diagnosis OR resolution with ≤4 days of antibiotics OR no pathologic evidence at surgery/autopsy with ≤4 days of antibiotics.

🚨 Indications for Emergency / Urgent Surgical Intervention in IE
  • 1. ACUTE HEART FAILURE (NUMBER 1 MOST URGENT EMEGENCY INDICATION): Severe acute aortic or mitral regurgitation leading to pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock. Immediate valve repair/replacement required.
  • 2. UNCONTROLLED INFECTION: Perivalvular extension, annular abscess, pseudoaneurysm, new AV conduction block/ECG changes, persistent bacteremia or fever ≥ 5–7 days despite appropriate antibiotics, or fungal/MDR IE.
  • 3. EMBOLISM PREVENTION: Large vegetation ≥ 10 mm (≥ 1 cm) combined with a prior embolic event or severe valve dysfunction (Urgent 3-5 days).

🎯 Key Numbers & Diagnostic Cutoffs
  • Neutropenia Cutoff: Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) < 500 /µL.
  • SBP Paracentesis Cutoff: ANC ≥ 250 PMNs/µL is diagnostic.
  • SBP Low Protein Ascites: Ascitic protein < 15 g/L (< 1.5 g/dL) = Indication for Prophylaxis!
  • Pyogenic Liver Abscess Drainage Cutoff: > 5 cm in Right Lobe (Left Lobe = Always Drain!).
  • Splenic Abscess Drainage Cutoff: < 4 cm, solitary, unilocular for percutaneous drainage; else splenectomy.
  • Psoas Abscess Drainage Cutoff: > 5 cm collection.
  • Perinephric Abscess Drainage Cutoff: > 3–5 cm collection.
  • Endocarditis Vegetation Size Cutoff: ≥ 10 mm (≥ 1 cm) = High risk for systemic embolization; indication for surgery if prior embolus.
  • Vancomycin Trough Target (SSTI / IE): 15–20 µg/mL.
