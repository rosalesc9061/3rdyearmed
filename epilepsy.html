📌 Core Definitions Snapshot

Seizure: Transient occurrence of signs/symptoms due to abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.

Convulsion: Paroxysmal involuntary muscular contractions (all convulsions are seizures, but not all seizures are convulsive!).

Epilepsy: A chronic brain state characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate recurrent spontaneous seizures.

Epileptogenesis: The sequence of cellular and structural events converting a normal neuronal network into a hyperexcitable network.

Definitions & Neuroanatomical Basis

1. Definitions & Practical Clinical Criteria for Epilepsy ▼

Seizure: An episode of neurologic dysfunction in which abnormal neuronal firing manifests clinically through changes in motor control, sensory perception, behavior, and/or autonomic function. Transient occurrence of signs/symptoms due to abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity (ILAE). High-frequency or low-frequency high-voltage discharge (Adams).

Convulsion: Seizure events specifically characterized by intense paroxysms of involuntary muscular contractions. Key distinction: All convulsions are seizures, but non-convulsive seizures (e.g., absence staring spells, focal sensory aura) are NOT convulsions.

⭐ 2014 ILAE Practical Clinical Definition of Epilepsy

Epilepsy is diagnosed when ANY of the following 3 criteria are met:

  1. At least two unprovoked (or reflex) seizures occurring >24 hours apart. (No diagnostic workup strictly needed if clinically clear).
  2. One unprovoked (or reflex) seizure AND a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk (at least 60%) over the next 10 years (e.g., single seizure + underlying brain tumor/glioma, stroke, or epileptiform EEG).
  3. Diagnosis of a specific epilepsy syndrome (e.g., Childhood Absence Epilepsy, Benign Rolandic Epilepsy).

Resolution of Epilepsy: Epilepsy is considered resolved for individuals who had an age-dependent epilepsy syndrome but are now past the applicable age, OR individuals who have remained seizure-free for the last 10 years, with no seizure medications for the last 5 years.

2. Neuroanatomical Basis & The Papez Circuit ▼

Seizure generation and spread involve specific anatomical structures of the cerebral cortex and limbic system: Hippocampus, Amygdala, Frontal cortex, Temporal cortex, and Olfactory cortex.

Anatomical Hyperexcitability Theories:

  • Dormant Basket Cell Theory: Loss of excitatory input from dentate mossy cells reduces the activation of inhibitory basket cells, rendering basket cells "dormant" and releasing hippocampal granule cells from GABAergic inhibition.
  • Mossy Fiber Theory: Recurrent mossy fiber sprouting forms excitatory feedback circuits among dentate granule cells, creating a self-reinforcing hyperexcitable network.
The Papez Circuit (Limbic Seizure Propagation Pathway)
Hippocampus ➔ Fornix ➔ Mammillary Bodies ➔ Mammillothalamic Tract ➔ Anterior Nucleus of Thalamus ➔ Cingulum / Cingulate Gyrus ➔ Entorhinal Cortex ➔ Back to Hippocampus
💡 Clinical Pearl: Papez Circuit & Comorbidities

Because the Papez circuit is fundamental to learning, memory, and emotional processing, patients with chronic temporal/limbic epilepsy frequently suffer from comorbid learning difficulties, memory deficits, depression, and affective dysfunction.

Cellular Mechanics & Pathophysiology

⚡ Cellular Excitability: Cations & Synaptic Imbalance

Seizures result from a fundamental imbalance between neuronal excitation and inhibition:

  • Na+ Channels: Increased Na+ influx $\rightarrow$ increased intracellular Na+ and water (water follows Na+) $\rightarrow$ neuronal swelling $\rightarrow$ hyper-excitable tissue.
  • Ca2+ Channels (T-type): Increased Ca2+ influx via low-threshold T-type Ca2+ channels $\rightarrow$ increased cellular excitability $\rightarrow$ repetitive oscillatory firing of thalamic and cortical neurons (drives Absence seizures).
  • K+ Channels: Impaired K+ efflux / decreased intracellular K+ $\rightarrow$ failure to repolarize membrane $\rightarrow$ persistent hyperexcitability.
  • Synaptic Neurotransmitters: Decreased inhibitory GABAergic activity vs. Increased excitatory Glutamatergic (NMDA/AMPA) activity.

🌊 Paroxysmal Depolarization Shift (PDS)

The PDS is the hallmark cellular correlation of interictal epileptiform spikes on scalp EEG:

  • Abnormal, sustained depolarization of neuronal membranes mediated by AMPA and NMDA receptor activation.
  • Followed by a hyperpolarizing post-potential mediated by GABA-A/GABA-B receptors and K+ channels.
  • Activation of T-type Ca2+ currents promotes thalamocortical synchrony, leading to focal epileptiform discharges.

Seizure Semiology & 2025 ILAE Classification

🧠 Exam Tip: Focal vs. Generalized Onset

Classification depends strictly on the earliest clinical symptoms and initial EEG onset. Focal seizures originate in one hemisphere; Generalized seizures engage bilateral thalamocortical networks from onset. Initial classification dictates choice of Anti-Seizure Medication (ASM)!

Focal Seizures

  • Originate within neuronal networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere.
  • Focal Aware (Simple Partial): Consciousness and awareness remain completely preserved. Patient can converse during event.
  • Focal Impaired Awareness (Complex Partial): Consciousness or awareness is impaired. Begins with blank stare, oral/manual automatisms, followed by post-ictal confusion.
  • Spread: May march along the homunculus, spread to adjacent lobes, or propagate via commissural pathways to involve both hemispheres (Focal to Bilateral Tonic-Clonic).
  • EEG: Focal epileptiform spikes/discharges (e.g., temporal LPDs).

Generalized Seizures

  • Engage bilateral thalamocortical circuits simultaneously at onset.
  • Generalized Tonic-Clonic (GTCS / Grand Mal): 30–120 seconds. Loss of consciousness $\rightarrow$ Tonic phase (stiffening, fall, ictal cry) $\rightarrow$ Clonic phase (rhythmic jerking, tapering) $\rightarrow$ Post-ictal confusion/lethargy. EEG: Generalized polyspikes.
  • Absence (Petit Mal): 3–20 seconds. Sudden brief staring spell in children (4–14 yrs), sudden onset & resolution, NO post-ictal confusion. Provoked by hyperventilation. EEG: Generalized 3 Hz spike-wave.
  • Atonic (Drop Attacks): Sudden loss of postural tone (seconds). High risk of head trauma $\rightarrow$ requires protective helmets!
  • Myoclonic: Rapid, shock-like, explosive jerks (upper extremities).

📋 Focal Seizure Semiology by Cortical Origin

Clinical Approach, Physical Exam & Seizure Mimics

⚠️ Diagnostic Pitfall: Seizure Threshold Modulators

Never start long-term ASMs after a single seizure without ruling out acute provoking metabolic factors or threshold-lowering drugs! Correct the underlying metabolic derangement first.

📉 Factors Lowering Seizure Threshold

  • Common: Sleep deprivation (most common in med students!), alcohol withdrawal, dehydration, systemic infection/fever, physical trauma, malnutrition, missed meals/hypoglycemia.
  • Occasional: Barbiturate withdrawal, hyperventilation, flashing lights (photic stimulation).

💊 Medications Lowering Seizure Threshold

  • Antidepressants: Bupropion, Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs).
  • Neuroleptics: Phenothiazines, Clozapine.
  • Antibiotics/Others: Penicillins, Carbapenems, Isoniazid (INH), Theophylline, Cyclosporine, Meperidine.

🔍 Physical & Neurologic Exam Findings in Seizure Patients

  • Meningeal Signs / Fever: Low-grade fever is common post-convulsion; persistent high fever indicates CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis). Nuchal rigidity demands lumbar puncture.
  • Cushing's Triad (HTN + Bradycardia): Indicates surging intracranial pressure and impending brain herniation!
  • Trauma Evaluation: Always evaluate for soft tissue and skeletal trauma. Posterior shoulder dislocation (<0.6% rare but classic for seizure!), humeral, thoracic spine, and femoral neck fractures.
  • Todd's Paralysis: Transient, focal neurological deficit (e.g., hemiparesis) following a seizure that resolves over hours. Must be differentiated from acute ischemic stroke!

Other Key Mimics:

  • Hyperventilation Syndrome: Anxious patient presenting with perioral cyanosis, paresthesias of hands, rapid breathing, and carpopedal spasm.
  • Migraine / Atypical Migraine: Visual aura, throbbing headache, photophobia. Occipital seizures present with short (seconds) geometric patterns vs. migraine aura lasting 15–30 minutes.
  • Transient Global Amnesia (TGA): Isolated amnesic syndrome lasting several hours to 1–2 days. Patient repeatedly asks the same questions. Consciousness, motor function, and language are intact. Recurrence is extremely rare.

Diagnostic Modalities & Pre-Surgical Workup

⭐ EEG Diagnostic Rules

1. EEG is the single most important diagnostic test in epilepsy.
2. A normal EEG NEVER rules out epilepsy (up to 10% of epileptic patients have persistently normal EEGs).
3. An abnormal EEG does NOT automatically equal epilepsy (must correlate with clinical semiology!).

⚡ EEG Modalities & Electrode Placement

10-20 System Nomenclature: Odd numbers = Left hemisphere; Even numbers = Right hemisphere; Z = Midline. FP = Frontopolar, F = Frontal, T = Temporal, C = Central, P = Parietal, O = Occipital.

  • Routine Scalp EEG: ~30 min recording. Uses hyperventilation and photic stimulation. Low yield for catching infrequent spikes.
  • Sleep-Deprived EEG: Patient is kept awake overnight. Transition from drowsiness to sleep strongly activates interictal epileptiform discharges.
  • Continuous Inpatient Video-EEG: Gold standard for capturing clinical events and correlating with electrographic onset. Used in ICU to rule out non-convulsive status.
  • Intracranial EEG: Subdural grids or depth electrodes (sEEG) implanted directly onto/into brain parenchyma for pre-surgical mapping.

🧠 Cranial CT vs. Epilepsy-Protocol Cranial MRI

Cranial CT: Fast, accessible. Preferred ONLY in acute emergency settings to rule out gross intracranial hemorrhage, trauma, or massive edema.

Epilepsy-Protocol MRI: 30–80% more sensitive than CT. Superior visualization of mesial temporal sclerosis, cortical dysgenesis (FCD), low-grade tumors (DNET, ganglioglioma), and vascular malformations.

Mandatory Sequences: T1 coronal (thin cuts perpendicular to hippocampus), T2 fast spin echo, FLAIR, Gradient Echo, 3D volume acquisition.

Anti-Seizure Medication (ASM) Selection & Toxicity

📈 Risk of Seizure Recurrence After 1st Unprovoked Seizure

Overall recurrence risk ranges from 21–45% in the first 2 years:

  • 1 Month: 7%
  • 6 Months: 24%
  • 1 Year: 32%
  • 2 Years: 36% (21–45%)
  • 5 Years: 46% (>5 Years: 49%)
⭐ 4 Factors Favoring Immediate ASM Start After Single Seizure
  1. Prior brain insult (prior stroke, trauma, infection).
  2. Epileptiform discharges clear on EEG.
  3. Abnormal brain imaging (gliosis, tumor, encephalomalacia).
  4. Nocturnal seizure (seizure occurring out of sleep).

🎯 Mechanisms of Action (MOA) Categories

  • Na+ Channel Blockers (Fast Inactivation): Carbamazepine, Oxcarbazepine, Phenytoin, Lamotrigine, Zonisamide.
  • Na+ Channel Enhancers (Slow Inactivation): Lacosamide.
  • SV2A Synaptic Vesicle Ligands: Levetiracetam, Brivaracetam.
  • T-type Ca2+ Channel Blockers: Ethosuximide (absence), Valproate.
  • GABA-A Receptor Enhancers: Benzodiazepines, Phenobarbital, Topiramate.
  • GABA Turnover / Reuptake Inhibitors: Valproate, Vigabatrin, Tiagabine.
  • AMPA / NMDA Blockers: Perampanel (AMPA), Felbamate, Topiramate.
  • Alpha-2-Delta Ca2+ Ligands: Gabapentin, Pregabalin.
⚠️ CRITICAL EXAM PITFALL: Narrow vs. Broad Spectrum ASMs

Narrow-Spectrum ASMs (Focal Only): Carbamazepine, Oxcarbazepine, Phenytoin, Lacosamide, Gabapentin, Pregabalin, Phenobarbital.
WARNING: Giving narrow-spectrum drugs (CBZ, OXC, PHT) to patients with generalized epilepsy syndromes (like Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy) can PARADOXICALLY EXACERBATE myoclonic and absence seizures!
Broad-Spectrum ASMs (Focal & Generalized): Lamotrigine, Levetiracetam, Valproate, Topiramate, Zonisamide, Perampanel, Clobazam.

Surgical, Neurostimulation & Dietary Therapies

1. Resective & Palliative Epilepsy Surgery ▼

Epilepsy surgery is the GOLD STANDARD treatment for drug-resistant (medically intractable) focal epilepsy. Evaluation requires answering 2 questions: Where do seizures originate? Can that region be safely resected without unacceptable neurological deficits?

Surgical Procedures Classification:

  • Resective Surgery (Curative Intent): Aim is complete seizure freedom. Includes Anterior Temporal Lobectomy + Selective Amygdalohippocampectomy (gold standard for Mesial Temporal Sclerosis), Lesionectomy, Corticectomy, Lobectomy.
  • MR-Guided Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy (LITT): Minimally invasive thermal ablation of deep focal lesions (e.g., hypothalamic hamartoma, hippocampal sclerosis) via stereotactic laser probe under real-time MRI thermal mapping.
  • Dissociative / Palliative Surgery: Aim is seizure reduction, not complete cure. Includes Corpus Callosotomy (disconnects hemispheres to prevent drop attacks in Lennox-Gastaut), Hemispherectomy (anatomical or functional for severe single-hemisphere damage), and Multiple Subpial Transections.
2. Neurostimulation Therapies ▼

⚡ Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS)

Subcutaneous pulse generator implanted in chest delivering scheduled intermittent stimulation to the left vagus nerve. Palliative adjunctive therapy. Side effects: Hoarseness, voice alteration, paresthesias, cough.

🧠 Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)

Delivers continuous or scheduled electrical pulses to specific subcortical thalamic targets: Anterior Nucleus of Thalamus (ANT) or Centromedian Nucleus (CMN) to disrupt network synchrony.

🔄 Responsive Neurostimulation (RNS)

Closed-loop system: Cranially implanted neurostimulator connected to intracranial depth/strip leads. Continuously monitors electrographic activity, detects early epileptiform patterns, and delivers immediate electrical counter-stimulation to abort seizures.

3. Dietary Therapies in Epilepsy ▼

High-fat, low-carbohydrate diets shift cerebral energy metabolism from glucose oxidation to ketone body utilization (beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate). Mechanisms: increased ATP production, altered brain pH, direct inhibition of AMPA receptors by fatty acids, and increased GABA synthesis.

Status Epilepticus & Comprehensive Clinical Case

⭐ Status Epilepticus Operational Time Points (T1 vs. T2)

Status Epilepticus (SE): Failure of seizure termination mechanisms leading to abnormally prolonged seizures.
• Time Point T1 (5 minutes for convulsive SE): Operational threshold to START emergency treatment to prevent prolonged seizure.
• Time Point T2 (30 minutes for convulsive SE): Threshold beyond which permanent neuronal injury, neuronal death, and network alteration occur.

⚡ Status Epilepticus Definitions Spectrum

  • Established Status Epilepticus: Convulsive seizure lasting $\ge 5$ minutes or $\ge 2$ discrete seizures without full recovery of consciousness between episodes.
  • Refractory Status Epilepticus (RSE): SE persisting despite administration of at least 2 appropriately selected and dosed parenteral drugs (Benzodiazepine + 2nd-line ASM).
  • Super-Refractory Status Epilepticus (SRSE): SE persisting $\ge 24$ hours despite continuous IV anesthetic therapy, or recurring upon weaning anesthetics.
  • Prolonged SRSE: SRSE persisting for $\ge 7$ days continuous anesthetic therapy.

🔄 Pathophysiological Shifts & Receptor Dynamics

  • Receptor Internalization: Progressive endocytosis and loss of functional surface GABA-A receptors + concurrent upregulation and synaptic recruitment of NMDA/AMPA excitatory receptors. Explains why Benzodiazepines lose efficacy when treatment is delayed!
  • Systemic Decompensation: Initial sympathetic activation (increased BP, HR, glucose) gives way to late failure (hypotension, hypoxemia, hypoglycemia, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, multi-organ failure).
  • Electroclinical Dissociation: Visible motor convulsions die down while continuous electrographic status continues on EEG!
AES Emergency Treatment Algorithm for Convulsive Status Epilepticus

0 – 5 Minutes (Stabilization Phase):

• Assess Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABCs); give oxygen.
• Check Capillary Blood Glucose (CBG) immediately $\rightarrow$ If hypoglycemic, give IV dextrose.
• Establish IV access, draw baseline bloods (CBC, electrolytes, toxic screen, ASM levels).

↓ If Seizure Continues $\ge 5$ Minutes:

5 – 20 Minutes (Initial Therapy Phase - Benzodiazepines):

• IV Lorazepam (0.1 mg/kg, max 4 mg) OR IV Diazepam (10 mg IV) OR IM Midazolam (10 mg IM if no IV access).
• If seizure persists after 5–10 min, repeat dose once.

↓ If Seizure Continues $\ge 20$ Minutes:

20 – 40 Minutes (Second Therapy Phase - IV Non-Sedating ASMs):

• IV Levetiracetam (60 mg/kg, max 4500 mg) OR IV Valproate (40 mg/kg, max 3000 mg) OR IV Fosphenytoin (20 mg PE/kg).

↓ If Seizure Continues $\ge 40$ Minutes (Refractory SE):

40 – 60 Minutes (Third Therapy Phase - ICU Anesthetic Infusion):

• Intubate patient, transfer to ICU.
• Start continuous IV continuous infusions of Midazolam, Propofol, or Pentobarbital with continuous Video-EEG monitoring.

🩺 Clinical Case Study: Step-by-Step Reasoning

Patient Profile & Presentation

Patient: 24-year-old male, Right-handed Filipino warehouse staff, no prior chronic medical conditions.

Chief Complaint: First witnessed generalized convulsive episode on day of admission.

History of Present Illness: 2 hours prior to consult, while working, suddenly stopped, stared blankly, and exhibited lip-smacking and left-hand fumbling (~30 seconds). This was immediately followed by generalized body stiffening and 4-extremity rhythmic jerking (~2 minutes), eye rolling, frothing, lateral tongue biting, and urinary incontinence. Post-ictally confused and somnolent for 20 minutes with total amnesia of the event.

Review of Systems: Patient admits to experiencing brief (<1 min) stereotyped episodes over the past 2 months of "spacing out" preceded by a rising epigastric sensation and déjà vu.

Social History: Works night shifts, chronic sleep deprivation. Occasional alcohol drinker.

Step-by-Step Clinical Decision Reasoning:

  1. Seizure vs. Mimic? The presence of an autonomic aura (rising epigastric sensation), experiential aura (déjà vu), stereotyped automatisms, lateral tongue biting, and prolonged post-ictal confusion confirms an epileptic seizure (rules out syncope, PNES, migraine).
  2. Seizure Semiologic Classification? Although witnesses saw a generalized convulsion, the stereotyped aura, behavioral arrest, lip-smacking, and unimanual fumbling establish a Focal Impaired Awareness Seizure evolving into Focal-to-Bilateral Tonic-Clonic Seizure originating from the Right Temporal Lobe.
  3. Initial Diagnostic Workup? General physical and neuro exams are normal. Routine bloods and initial non-contrast CT are clear. Routine 30-min scalp EEG and brain MRI are reported normal. Next best step? Obtain a 24-Hour Prolonged Video-EEG Monitoring! (Normal routine EEG does NOT rule out epilepsy).
  4. Diagnostic Result: Prolonged EEG reveals Lateralized Periodic Discharges (LPDs) over the Right Fronto-Temporal region (F8-T4).
  5. Management & ASM Selection: Patient meets 2014 ILAE criteria for Epilepsy (prior unprovoked focal seizures over 2 months + epileptiform LPDs on EEG). First-line monotherapy options are Lamotrigine or Levetiracetam. Because the patient works as a warehouse staff requiring constant vigilance (avoid sedating drugs) and does NOT have acute seizure clusters, Lamotrigine is the preferred long-term monotherapy choice (excellent mood/cognitive profile, no sedating effect, though requiring slow 6–8 week titration to avoid SJS).

🎯 "If You See X → Think Y" Rapid Clinical Triggers

  • Child with 5-second blank stare, no post-ictal confusion, triggered by blowing a pinwheel $\rightarrow$ Absence Seizure (EEG: Generalized 3 Hz spike-wave; Tx: Ethosuximide or VPA).
  • Night shift worker waking up with violent lower extremity thrashing $\rightarrow$ Frontal Lobe Hypermotor Seizure.
  • Déjà vu + rising epigastric aura + lip-smacking + manual fumbling $\rightarrow$ Temporal Lobe Focal Impaired Awareness Seizure (Mesial Temporal Sclerosis).
  • Posterior shoulder dislocation after sudden collapse $\rightarrow$ True Epileptic Seizure (GTCS or Tonic).
  • Lateral tongue laceration $\rightarrow$ High-specificity indicator of true Epileptic Seizure (Tip of tongue = Syncope/PNES).
  • Pregnant woman taking Valproate $\rightarrow$ High risk of Neural Tube Defects (Spina Bifida) and cognitive impairment.
  • Overweight/obese patient with focal epilepsy needing ASM $\rightarrow$ Topiramate or Zonisamide (promotes weight loss).
  • Patient taking Lamotrigine started on Valproate $\rightarrow$ Cut Lamotrigine dose by 50%! (Valproate inhibits metabolism $\rightarrow$ severe SJS risk).
  • Han Chinese patient planned for Carbamazepine $\rightarrow$ Screen for HLA-B*1502 allele before starting (SJS/TEN risk).
  • Seizure lasting >5 minutes $\rightarrow$ Convulsive Status Epilepticus Time Point T1 (Give IV Lorazepam 4mg or IV Diazepam 10mg or IM Midazolam 10mg immediately!).
  • Seizure continuing despite Benzodiazepine + IV Levetiracetam/Valproate $\rightarrow$ Refractory Status Epilepticus (Transfer to ICU, intubate, start Propofol/Midazolam infusion).
  • Interictal FDG-PET showing hypometabolism in temporal lobe $\rightarrow$ Temporal Lobe Epilepsy focus.
  • Ictal SPECT showing hyperperfusion $\rightarrow$ Active seizure onset zone.
