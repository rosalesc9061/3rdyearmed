Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding (LGIB)

Comprehensive Interactive Reviewer for PLE | Based on SURGERY 2-LEC: LE 3 | TRANS 3

Overview & Core Concepts

Definition: Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding (LGIB) is defined as any bleeding that originates distal to (beyond) the Ligament of Treitz.

Anatomical Landmark

The Ligament of Treitz serves as the critical anatomical divider between the duodenum and the jejunum. Any hemorrhage occurring beyond this point is categorized as a lower GI bleed.

Broad Categories of LGIB

  • Vascular / Structural: Diverticular bleeding, Angiodysplasia, Aortoenteric fistula, Radiation proctitis, Hemorrhoids.
  • Neoplastic Diseases: Colorectal Cancer (CRC), Small Intestine neoplasms/hemangiomas.
  • Inflammatory / Ischemic: Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), Autoimmune Disorders/Vasculitis, Ischemic Colitis.
  • Mechanical / Traumatic: Penetrating/Blunt Trauma, Intussusception.
  • Post-Procedural Bleeding: Most common after anastomosis of intestinal segments (e.g., post-colectomy). Bleeding is from vessels surrounding the luminal margin. Managed conservatively; may require repeat resection if persistent.
High-Yield Anatomy: The Ligament of Treitz connects the duodenojejunal flexure to the right crus of the diaphragm.

Specific Causes of LGIB

  • Epidemiology: Common in the >40 age group (elderly patients).
  • Pathophysiology: Solitary cavitations/diverticula cause the bleeding, NOT the "Swiss cheese" type of diverticular lesions. The underlying vasa recta rupture.
  • Incidence/Prognosis: 3-15% of patients with diverticula may bleed. 75% stop spontaneously. 10% have rebleeds within 1 year.
  • Anatomy: Right-sided diverticulosis has a HIGHER chance of bleeding than left-sided.
  • Clinical Presentation: Bouts of diverticulitis, Left lower quadrant pain, Severe bleeding (in some cases).
  • Treatment & Management:
    • Can resolve spontaneously.
    • Conservative: Low residue diet during flares, medications. Afterwards, high fiber diet.
    • Interventions: Colonoscopic control, Angiography + embolization.
    • Surgical Indications: Complications (bleeding, perforation, stricture).
    • Surgery type: Colonic Resection (Blind colectomy/Subtotal) if uncontrolled by scopes/angio. Left Hemicolectomy if extensive left-sided disease. Formal oncologic colectomy is not warranted.
  • Definition: Degenerative vascular lesions in the elderly.
  • Epidemiology: 80-90 year olds have greater disposition.
  • Anatomy: Lesions are often right-sided (cecum/ascending colon).
  • Prognosis: Spontaneous resolution of bleed is common, but a handful present with massive bleeding.
  • Treatment Pipeline:
    1. Colonoscopy control (epinephrine, clip placement, electrocoagulation, sclerosing agents).
    2. Angiography + embolization / intra-arterial vasopressin.
    3. Colonic resection (last line).
⚠️ Elderly Surgical Risk: Make sure the patient is prepared with adequate blood products and cardiac/pulmonary clearance prior to radical surgeries. Tailor approach because elderly patients might not have safe outcomes compared to younger patients.
  • Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST): More common in the upper GI tract (small intestine). Treat with resection if not too bulky, or colonic diversion proximal to the lesion.
  • Polyps: Endoscopic removal/control (Polypectomy) or Colectomy if too large.
  • Adenocarcinoma: Most common encountered neoplasia. Treat with resection or diversion.
💡 Surgical Management for Unresectable/Unstable Tumors: If the tumor is adherent and the patient is unstable/unfit, perform a proximal diversion (e.g., transverse colostomy for a bleeding sigmoid CA). This diverts stool, lowering bleeding risk while you nutritionally and medically build the patient up for formal resection later.
  • Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): More common in Caucasians, rare in Filipinos. Ulcerative Colitis is a more common cause of LGIB than Crohn's disease.
    • Presentation: Up to 20 bloody bowel movements per day, crampy abdominal pain, tenesmus.
    • Tx: Medical (common) or Surgical (Total colectomy; solves UC but requires lifelong surveillance).
  • Infectious Colitis: Requires history + stool culture. Pathogens: C. difficile, Cytomegalovirus. Treat medically.
  • Radiation Proctitis: Commonly seen after gynecologic malignancy treatment (e.g., cervical cancer radiation).
    • Tx: Treat conservatively (Hydrocortisone enemas, Formalin ablation). Surgery (proctectomy) is rare.
  • Occult Blood Loss: Presents as Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA), FOBT/FIT (+).
    • Tx: Treat conservatively with iron supplementation; usually resolves. If unexplained, do colonoscopy.
  • Obscure Bleeding: Defined by negative findings on BOTH upper (EGD) and lower endoscopy.
    • 80% respond to iron supplementation.
  • Obscure-Overt Bleeding (1%): Visible bleeding but scopes negative. Source is often the small intestine (e.g., small bowel ulcers, polyps, Crohn's). Requires Capsule Endoscopy or Enteroscopy.
Differential Diagnosis of Obscure GI Bleeding (Box 46-3)
Upper GI: Angiodysplasia, PUD, Aortoenteric fistula, Neoplasia, Dieulafoy, Hemobilia, Hemosuccus pancreaticus, GAVE
Small Bowel: Crohn's, Meckel's diverticulum, Radiation enteritis, NSAID erosions, Angiodysplasia
Colon: Colitis (UC, Crohn's, Ischemic, Radiation, Infective), Solitary rectal ulcer, Hemorrhoids, Angiodysplasia

Incidence: Hemorrhoids are the most common cause of ANY lower GI bleeding.

  • Conservative Management (First-line): Resolves bleeding in 50-80%. High-fiber diet, lifestyle mods, Flavonoids/Diosmin.
  • Interventional (Last-line): Rubber Band Ligation (RBL) for bleeding internal hemorrhoids, Hemorrhoidectomy.

Clinical Approach and Diagnostic Workup

Rule #1 of GI Bleeding: Immediate resuscitation precedes diagnostic localization. "It is pointless to start looking for where the bleeding is coming from if your patient is already crashing."

1. Initial Management (Unstable Patient)

  • Insert two large-bore IV catheters.
  • Rapidly prepare blood products (PRBCs) for volume replacement.
  • Correct coagulopathies.
  • Once stabilized, identify source. Differentiate UGIB from LGIB (Nasogastric aspirate).

2. Age-Based Evaluation Strategy (Stable Patients)

Patients <40 years old (Lower malignancy risk)Patients >40 years old (Higher neoplasia risk)
  1. Visual Inspection & DRE (check anal/rectal lesions)
  2. Anoscopy / Proctosigmoidoscopy
  3. Flexible Sigmoidoscopy / Office Colonoscopy
  4. Formal Colonoscopy under IV Sedation (if persistent)
  1. Immediate bedside evaluation: DRE, Anoscopy, Proctosigmoidoscopy
  2. Formal Colonoscopy: Paramount to rule out colonic neoplasia.

3. Endoscopic Evaluation (Colonoscopy)

Primary diagnostic and therapeutic tool.

  • Bowel Prep (2-3 Day Protocol):
    • Day 1-2: General liquid diet (water, clear soup, gelatin, crackers). NO BREAD.
    • Day before: CLEAR liquids only + Purging agents (Castor Oil or Dulcolax).
    • 1-2 Hours Before: Fleet Enema to evacuate distal rectum/sigmoid.
  • Technique: Left Lateral Decubitus position under IV anesthesia. Advance through sigmoid to cecum/appendiceal orifice. Attempt ileal intubation to evaluate terminal ileum. Inspect on withdrawal (360°).
  • Therapeutic Options: Epinephrine injection (submucosa), Hemoclips / Deployable clips for immediate hemostasis.
  • Acute/Emergent challenges: Poor bowel prep = blood clots requiring continuous flushing.

4. Imaging Modalities

Modality Details & Sensitivity Pros / Cons
Radionuclide Scanning (99mTc RBC) Tracer mixed with 10-15cc blood, reinfused. Extremely sensitive up to 0.1 mL/min. Pros: Highly sensitive. Cons: Presumptive (only determines quadrant, not exact segment), limited availability, costly.
CT Angiography Computed tomography + IV contrast. Pros: Better localization. Cons: NOT therapeutic, Risk of Contrast-Induced Nephropathy (kidney injury), especially in elderly.
Mesenteric Angiography Diagnostic and Therapeutic. Requires brisk bleeding (0.5 - 1.0 mL/min). Pros: Can infuse vasopressin or embolize. Cons: Complications (thrombosis, hematoma, renal failure, dye rxn). Costly.
Video Capsule Endoscopy Swallowed capsule records video of small bowel. Pros: Visualizes areas beyond Ligament of Treitz. Cons: Not gold standard, 90% success, 30-40% failure in localizing obscure bleeds.
Obscure GI Bleeding Algorithm: Minor (intermittent) -> Enteroscopy/Capsule. Major (persistent) -> If stable, Tagged RBC scan; If unstable -> Operating Room (Intraoperative Endoscopy / Serial clamping).

Clinical Comparisons

Melena vs Hematochezia

Feature Melena Hematochezia
Origin Upper GI Bleed (Proximal to Ligament of Treitz) Lower GI Bleed (Distal to Ligament of Treitz)
Appearance Jet-black, tarry stools (Compare to "dinuguan") Dark red, maroon, or fresh/bright red blood
Pathophysiology Blood has undergone oxidation and acid hematin formation Rapid transit or lower colonic source; unoxidized
Transit Time Prolonged stay required (~14 hours) Fast transit time
Clinical Pitfall ⚠️ A patient with a severe, torrentially bleeding upper GI source (massive PUD/varices) can present with hematochezia due to rapid transit. Always consider NGT aspirate to rule out massive UGIB.

Diverticulosis vs Angiodysplasia (Common LGIB in Elderly)

Feature Diverticular Bleeding Angiodysplasia (AVMs)
Pathology Solitary cavitations; rupture of vasa recta Degenerative vascular malformations
Age Group > 40 years old 80-90 years old
Common Location Right-sided diverticula bleed more (though diverticula are common on left) Predominantly Right-sided (Cecum/Ascending)
Clinical Course 75% stop spontaneously; 10% rebleed in 1 year Spontaneous resolution common, but can have massive hemorrhage

Self-Assessment (Board-Style MCQs)

Last-Minute Cram Sheet

Top-Yield Buzzwords & Associations

  • Ligament of Treitz: Divides UGIB (melena) and LGIB (hematochezia).
  • Massive UGIB: Can present as hematochezia due to rapid transit. Rule out with NGT aspirate.
  • Melena transit time: Requires ~14 hours in the GI tract. "Dinuguan" appearance.
  • Most common cause of ANY lower GI bleed: Hemorrhoids.
  • Most common cause of LGIB in adults (per review Qs): Diverticulosis (typically right-sided bleeds).
  • Bleeding Diverticula: Caused by solitary cavitations, NOT "Swiss cheese" diverticulitis. 75% stop spontaneously.
  • Angiodysplasia: Degenerative AVMs, 80-90 y/o, Right-sided colon.
  • Bleeding post-gynecologic malignancy radiation: Radiation proctitis. Treat with hydrocortisone enema or formalin.
  • Post-procedural bleeding: Bleeding from vessels at luminal margin of anastomosis.
  • 20 bloody BMs/day + tenesmus: Ulcerative Colitis.
  • Obscure GI Bleed: Negative EGD & Colonoscopy. Next step: Trial of Iron (80% respond). If overt/persistent, suspect small bowel (Capsule endoscopy).

Diagnostic Modalities Cheat Sheet

  • Radionuclide Scan (Tagged RBC): MOST SENSITIVE. Detects 0.1 mL/min. Presumptive (quadrant only).
  • Mesenteric Angiography: Diagnostic + THERAPEUTIC (vasopressin/embolize). Needs brisk bleed (0.5 - 1.0 mL/min).
  • CT Angiography: Localizes well, NOT therapeutic. Beware kidney injury (contrast).
  • Colonoscopy Prep: No bread 2-3 days prior. Castor oil/Dulcolax day before. Fleet enema 1-2 hrs before. Patient in Left Lateral Decubitus.

"If You See X → Think Y"

If you see: Hemodynamic instability + Hematochezia

→ Think: Massive UGIB or Massive Diverticular/Arterial bleed. Action: Resuscitate (2 large bore IVs) before scopes!

If you see: Elderly patient >80 with painless right-sided bleeding

→ Think: Angiodysplasia (AVM).

If you see: Young patient <40 with bright red blood per rectum

→ Think: Hemorrhoids or IBD. Action: DRE and Anoscopy/Flex Sig.

If you see: Patient >40 with stable LGIB

→ Think: Rule out Malignancy (Adenocarcinoma). Action: Formal Colonoscopy.
