Diseases of the Gallbladder, Biliary Tree, and Pancreas

I. Bile & Biliary Anatomy / Physiology

Core Concept: Bile facilitates intestinal absorption of dietary fats (especially cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins) via micellar transport mechanisms.

A. Hepatic Bile vs. Gallbladder Bile

Feature Hepatic Bile Gallbladder Bile
Formation/Location Hepatic lobules → canaliculi → bile ducts Sequestered and concentrated in the GB on overnight fast
Tonicity Isotonic fluid like plasma Isotonic but highly concentrated
Solute Concentration 3-4 g/dL 10-15 g/dL (Concentrated via transmucosal absorption of water/electrolytes)
Volume/Secretion Basal secretion: 500-600 mL / 24 hrs ~30 cc / 24 hr period
Electrolytes Normal plasma-like Less Cl⁻ and HCO3⁻

B. Major Solutes of Bile

  • Bile acids (80%)
  • Lecithin and other phospholipids (16%)
  • Unesterified cholesterol (4%)
Pathophysiology of Lithogenic Bile: When unesterified cholesterol reaches >8 to 10%, it renders the bile lithogenic, predisposing to cholesterol stone formation. Solubility depends on total lipid concentration and the molar percentages of bile acids and lecithin forming solubilizing mixed micelles.

C. Regulation of Bile Flow

  • Active transport of bile acids from hepatocytes into bile canaliculi.
  • Active transport of other organic anions.
  • Cholangiocellular secretion: Secretin-mediated and cAMP-dependent → Secretion of Na⁺ and HCO3⁻ rich fluid in the bile ducts.

D. Bile Acids

Primary Bile Acids
Synthesized from cholesterol in the liver.
Conjugated with glycine or taurine.
Cholic acid, Chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA)
Secondary Bile Acids
Formed in the colon as bacterial metabolites of primary bile acids.
Deoxycholate, Lithocholate, Ursodeoxycholic acid (stereoisomer of CDCA)

Diseases of the Gallbladder & Biliary Tree

1. Gallstones (Cholelithiasis)

Primary disease of the gallbladder. Formed because of abnormal bile composition.

Stone Type Composition & Appearance Predisposing Factors / Pathophysiology
Cholesterol Stones (80%) >50% cholesterol monohydrate. Radiotranslucent mostly. Accounts for >90% of stones in Western countries.
  • Obesity/Metabolic Syn: Increased biliary cholesterol secretion.
  • Rapid weight loss: Tissue cholesterol mobilization, decreased enterohepatic circulation.
  • Female sex hormones (Estrogen/OCPs/Pregnancy): Increases dietary cholesterol uptake & biliary secretion. Progesterone inhibits GB contractility.
  • GB Hypomotility: Stasis (prolonged TPN, fasting, Octreotide).
  • Genetics: Defect in CYP7A1 (decreased bile acid) or MDR3 (decreased phospholipid).
Black Pigment Stones (20%) Calcium bilirubinate or polymer-like complexes. <20% cholesterol. Black color. Chronic hemolysis, cirrhosis, pernicious anemia, cystic fibrosis, ileal disease/resection. Increased unconjugated bilirubin.
Brown Pigment Stones Calcium salts of unconjugated bilirubin + cholesterol + protein. Chronic biliary infection/parasites (Asia). Deconjugation of bilirubin by bacterial enzymes (beta-glucuronidase). Forms directly in bile ducts.
Natural History of Asymptomatic Stones: 75-80% remain asymptomatic. Prophylactic cholecystectomy is NOT warranted for asymptomatic stones unless: high risk of GB cancer (porcelain GB), stones >3cm, anomalous GB, or sickle cell anemia.

2. Clinical Presentations of Gallstones

Anatomical Progression of Gallstone Disease
  • Asymptomatic (75%): Stone stays in GB lumen.
  • Biliary Pain / Colic (20%): Intermittent cystic duct obstruction. Steady, poorly localized RUQ/epigastric ache radiating to right scapula. Lasts 1-4 hours. Resolves when stone falls back. (Note: "Colic" is a misnomer, pain is steady).
  • Acute Cholecystitis (10%): Persistent cystic duct impaction >6 hours. GB distends & inflames.
  • Mirizzi's Syndrome (<0.1%): Impaction in GB neck/cystic duct compressing the common hepatic duct.
  • Choledocholithiasis (~5%): Stone in distal CBD. Causes jaundice, biliary pain, risk of cholangitis and gallstone pancreatitis.
  • Cholecystoenteric Fistula (<0.1%): Stone erodes through GB into bowel (usually duodenum). Can cause Gallstone Ileus or Bouveret's syndrome (gastric outlet obstruction).

3. Acute Cholecystitis

Pathophysiology: Mechanical obstruction → distention & ischemia → chemical inflammation (lysolecithin release) → secondary bacterial superinfection (50-85%, mainly E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterococcus).

Acute Cholecystitis Triad: Sudden onset RUQ tenderness (Murphy's sign) + Fever + Leukocytosis.
Diagnosis: Ultrasound is initial test (shows thickened wall, pericholecystic fluid, stones). HIDA scan is the gold standard (non-visualization of GB due to cystic duct obstruction).

Tokyo Guidelines 2018 Severity & Management

Grade Criteria Management
Mild (Grade I) Healthy patient, mild inflammatory changes, no organ dysfunction. Antibiotics + General supportive care → Early Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy (LC).
Moderate (Grade II) WBC >18,000, palpable tender mass, duration >72h, or marked local inflammation (gangrenous, emphysematous, abscess). Antibiotics → Early LC (within 96h) OR Urgent/early GB drainage if failing therapy.
Severe (Grade III) Organ dysfunction (CVS req dopamine/dobutamine, Neuro change, PaO2/FiO2 <300, Creatinine >2.0, PT/INR >1.5, Plt <100k). Antibiotics + Organ Support → Urgent GB drainage → Delayed elective LC.

4. Acute Cholangitis

Pathophysiology: Obstruction of bile duct + bacterial superinfection of stagnant bile. Early bacteremia.

Charcot's Triad (70%): RUQ Pain + Jaundice + Fever.
Reynold's Pentad (15%): Charcot's Triad + Hypotension + Mental Status Changes (indicates Gram-negative sepsis).

Treatment: General supportive care + Antibiotics + Biliary Drainage (ERCP with clearance/stenting is urgent, especially in Grade II/III).

5. Complications & Specific Syndromes

  • Acalculous Cholecystitis: GB inflammation without stones. Seen in critically ill (major trauma, burns, TPN, prolonged fasting). Diagnose early, high mortality.
  • Emphysematous Cholecystitis: Ischemia/gangrene + gas-forming organisms (C. perfringens, E. coli). Elderly diabetic males. Plain film shows gas in GB wall. High mortality. Tx: IV antibiotics + Urgent cholecystectomy or percutaneous drainage.
  • Hydrops (Mucocele): Prolonged cystic duct obstruction. GB distends with sterile clear mucus/transudate.
  • Empyema: Persistent obstruction with superinfection resulting in a GB filled with pus.
  • Gallstone Ileus: Large stone (>25mm) erodes into bowel (cholecystoenteric fistula) and obstructs the narrowest part (Terminal Ileum). Present as SBO.
  • Bouveret's Syndrome: Gallstone impacts in duodenum causing gastric outlet obstruction.
  • Porcelain Gallbladder: Intramural calcification. Asymptomatic but carries 20% risk of GB carcinoma. Requires prophylactic cholecystectomy.
  • Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC): Progressive stricturing of intra/extrahepatic ducts. 70-90% associated with IBD (Ulcerative Colitis). Pathognomonic: "narrowing and beading" on MRCP/ERCP. High risk for Cholangiocarcinoma.
  • Cholesterolosis: "Strawberry gallbladder" - lipid deposition in lamina propria macrophages.
  • Adenomyomatosis: Benign proliferation of epithelium with Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses.

Pancreatic Disorders

1. Acute Pancreatitis

Acute inflammation involving pancreas and peripancreatic tissues. Premature activation of trypsinogen causing autodigestion.

Etiologies:

Gallstones (40-70%), Alcohol (10-30%), Hypertriglyceridemia, Post-ERCP, Drugs (valproate, azathioprine, thiazides).

Diagnosis (Revised Atlanta Criteria - Need 2 of 3):
1. Acute epigastric pain radiating to the back.
2. Serum amylase or lipase ≥ 3x Upper Limit of Normal (ULN).
3. Characteristic imaging findings (CECT, MRI, or US).
Amylase and lipase levels DO NOT correlate with disease severity. Lipase is more sensitive and specific. Routine CECT on admission is NOT indicated unless diagnosis is uncertain. Best time for CT to assess necrosis is 3-5 days after admission.

Severity Classification (Revised Atlanta)

Severity Definition Prognosis & Management Notes
Mild (~80%) No organ failure. No local/systemic complications. Resolves in 1 week. Hydration, analgesia, early oral feeding.
Moderately Severe Transient organ failure (<48 hrs) OR local/systemic complications. Requires longer stay. May develop fluid collections.
Severe (~5-10%) Persistent organ failure (>48 hrs) (SBP <90, PaO2 <60, Crea ≥2.0). High mortality. Requires ICU, aggressive hydration, enteral nutrition (nasojejunal). Prophylactic antibiotics are NOT recommended for sterile necrosis.

Management Principles

  • Hydration: Ringer's lactate preferred. 250-500 mL/hr early.
  • Analgesia: Multimodal, opioids. Avoid NSAIDs if renal impairment.
  • Nutrition: Early enteral feeding (24-48h). Nasojejunal if severe. TPN only if absolutely necessary.
  • ERCP: Urgent (<24h) if severe acute biliary pancreatitis with cholangitis or persistent obstruction.

2. Chronic Pancreatitis

Irreversible damage, fibrosis, and stellate cell activation. 60-70% due to alcohol. Evaluated by the TIGAR-O classification (Toxic, Idiopathic, Genetic, Autoimmune, Recurrent, Obstructive).

Classic Triad (Late Advanced Disease): Pancreatic calcifications + Diabetes Mellitus + Steatorrhea (Maldigestion).

Diagnostic Tests

  • Fecal pancreatic elastase-1 (FPE-1): <100 mcg/g indicates severe exocrine insufficiency.
  • Secretin Stimulation Test: Peak bicarbonate <80 mEq/L is consistent with chronic pancreatitis.
  • Imaging: ERCP/MRCP shows dilated ducts, calcifications, "chain of lakes".

3. Autoimmune Pancreatitis (AIP)

Presents with mild pain, obstructive jaundice (50-60%), diffuse "sausage-shaped" pancreatic enlargement.

  • Type 1: Lymphoplasmacytic sclerosing pancreatitis. Elevated IgG4. Associated with Sjogren's, PSC, UC, Retroperitoneal fibrosis. Responds dramatically to corticosteroids.
  • Type 2: Idiopathic duct-centric. Granulocytic epithelial lesions. No IgG4 elevation. Higher relapse rate, associated with IBD.

4. Pancreatic Cancer (Adenocarcinoma)

  • Location: 70% in the Head. Arises from ductal cells.
  • Pathogenesis: K-ras mutation (90% - hallmark), p53, DPC4/SMAD4, p16/CDKN2A.
  • Tumor Marker: CA 19-9 (Sialylated Lewis antigen).
  • Clinical: Painless jaundice + palpable nontender gallbladder (Courvoisier sign) suggests head of pancreas tumor. Body/tail tumors present late with weight loss, back pain, metastasis.
  • Imaging: Pancreatic protocol helical CT scan is the best initial modality for diagnosis and staging.

5. Cystic Neoplasms of the Pancreas

Neoplasm Demographics & Location Characteristics Malignant Potential
Mucinous Cystic Neoplasm Middle-aged Women (>80%), Body/Tail Viscous mucin, high CEA, ovarian-like stroma. Does NOT communicate with duct. High (Premalignant). Needs resection.
Serous Cystadenoma Women (70%), Body/Tail Clear, watery, high glycogen, low CEA. Low. Often observed.
IPMN (Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm) M=F, Median age 65, Head Mucin hypersecretion. Communicates with main duct or side branches. DPC4 expressed. Main duct: High (57-92%). Side branch: Lower (6-46%). Tx: Resection.
Solid Pseudopapillary Neoplasm Young women (20s-30s) Large, well-circumscribed, solid/cystic. Low malignant potential. Excellent prognosis post-resection.
