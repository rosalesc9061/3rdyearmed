I. Hepatic Anatomy & Physiology

A. Suspensory Ligaments & Boundaries

  • Glisson's Capsule: Fibrous capsule surrounding the liver; stretching causes RUQ pain (referred to right shoulder via Right Phrenic Nerve).
  • Triangular Ligaments (Right & Left): Attach liver to diaphragm.
  • Falciform Ligament: Anchors liver to anterior abdominal wall. Contains the round ligament (Ligamentum teres), which is the remnant of the obliterated umbilical vein. Separates Left Lateral from Left Medial segments along the umbilical fissure.
  • Gastrohepatic Ligament: Extends from liver to lesser curvature. Contains the Porta Hepatis (Hepatoduodenal Ligament):
    • Common Bile Duct (CBD): Anterior and to the right.
    • Hepatic Artery Proper: Anterior and to the left.
    • Portal Vein: Posterior to both CBD and Hepatic Artery.
  • Foramen of Winslow (Epiploic Foramen): Connects directly to lesser sac. Site of Pringle Maneuver (clamping hepatoduodenal ligament for vascular inflow control to limit operative bleeding).

B. Segmental Anatomy (Couinaud Classification)

Divided grossly into Right (60-70%) and Left (30-40%) lobes by Cantlie's Line (plane running from Gallbladder Fossa to IVC).

C. Dual Blood Supply & Venous Outflow

Hepatic Artery (25% volume): Arises from Celiac Trunk → Common Hepatic Artery → Proper Hepatic Artery → Left & Right Hepatic Arteries. Classic arterial anatomy present in only 76% of individuals.

Portal Vein (75% volume): Formed by confluence of Splenic Vein and Superior Mesenteric Vein (SMV). Inferior Mesenteric Vein (IMV) drains into Splenic Vein. Normal pressure: 3-5 mmHg. Valveless system.

Hepatic Veins (3 main):

  • Right Hepatic Vein (RHV): Drains segments V-VIII directly into IVC.
  • Middle Hepatic Vein (MHV): Drains segments IV, V, VIII.
  • Left Hepatic Vein (LHV): Drains segments II, III. MHV & LHV form a common trunk before entering IVC in majority.

D. Innervation, Lymphatics & LFT Diagnostic Interpretation

🧠
Mnemonic for Innervation (LARP): Left Vagus = Anterior hepatic branch; Right Vagus = Posterior hepatic branch.

Referred Pain: Capsular stretch or diaphragmatic irritation causes referred pain to Right Shoulder/Scapula via the Right Phrenic Nerve.

Diagnostic Laboratory Interpretation:

  • AST / ALT (Transaminases): Elevated in thousands → Acute viral hepatitis or ischemic insult.
  • Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP): Secreted by biliary epithelium. Elevated in biliary obstruction/cholestasis.
  • Indirect (Unconjugated) Bilirubin: Hemolysis, pre-hepatic defects, Gilbert syndrome.
  • Direct (Conjugated) Bilirubin: Biliary tree obstruction (surgical jaundice).
  • Prothrombin Time (PT): Reflects factor synthesis (I, II, V, VII, X). Non-response to parenteral Vit K indicates severe parenchymal failure → give Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP).
⚠️
Common Examination Pitfall (Ultrasound Limitation): Ultrasound has >90% accuracy for gallbladder stones but cannot identify stones in the distal Common Bile Duct due to overlying air-filled duodenal gas penetration limits.
II. Cirrhosis & Portal Hypertension

A. Cirrhosis Etiology & Clinical Stigmata

Final sequela of chronic liver injury characterized by diffuse fibrous septa & regenerative parenchymal nodules. 40% are initially asymptomatic.

Etiologies: Alcohol abuse (most common in US), Chronic Viral Hepatitis B/C/D, NASH, Hemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, Alpha-1-Antitrypsin deficiency.

Physical Findings:

  • Sex Hormone Alterations: Spider angiomata, palmar erythema, gynecomastia, testicular atrophy, loss of chest/axillary hair.
  • Portal Hypertension: Caput medusae, splenomegaly, esophageal/gastric varices, hemorrhoids, Cruveilhier-Baumgarten murmur (epigastric venous hum over umbilical vein remnant).
  • Parenchymal Failure: Jaundice (Bilirubin >2-3 mg/dL), Fetor hepaticus (musty odor of rotten eggs/garlic), Asterixis, Dupuytren's contracture, Hypoalbuminemia → peripheral edema & ascites.

Child Class A (5-6 pts): 10% mortality; eligible for liver resection. Class B (7-9 pts): 30% mortality. Class C (10-15 pts): 80% mortality.

MELD Score (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease): Based on Serum Creatinine, Total Bilirubin, and INR. MELD < 10 = Safe for elective surgery; 10–15 = Surgery with caution; > 15 = Contraindicated for elective surgery.

C. Management of Variceal Hemorrhage & Portal HTN

1. Primary Prophylaxis

  • Nonselective Beta-Blockers (Propranolol, Nadolol): Reduces index bleed risk by 45%. Decreases portal inflow via splanchnic vasoconstriction ($\beta_1$) and blockade ($\beta_2$).
  • Endoscopic Variceal Band Ligation (EVL): Surveillance every 1-2 weeks until obliteration, then 1-3 months, then 6 months.

2. Acute Bleeding Resuscitation

  • ICU admission, airway protection.
  • Transfuse PRBC to maintain target Hemoglobin $\approx 8\text{ g/dL}$. CRITICAL Avoid overzealous blood/saline replacement as it raises portal pressure and induces rebleeding!
  • Pharmacology: IV Octreotide (somatostatin analog) to decrease splanchnic blood flow. Short-term Ceftriaxone (1g/day) prophylaxis. Avoid Vasopressin!

3. Refractory Bleeding Interventions

  • Sengstaken-Blakemore Tube: Balloon tamponade controls bleeding in 90%. Temporary bridge ONLY; max duration <36 hours to avoid esophageal perforation & pressure necrosis.
  • TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt): Interventional radiologist places stent between portal vein branch and hepatic vein. Lowers gradient to $\le 12\text{ mmHg}$. Risk: Encephalopathy (25-30%).
  • Sugiura Procedure (Non-shunt): Esophageal transection, gastric devascularization, splenectomy, truncal vagotomy + pyloroplasty. NOTE Left gastric vein is preserved to maintain azygos drainage!

💡 Amebic Abscess Drainage Indications

Medical management with Metronidazole is mainstay. Aspiration/Drainage is indicated ONLY IF:

  1. Failed medical therapy after 3–5 days (persistent fever/pain).
  2. Secondary bacterial superinfection.
  3. Large abscess (> 5 to 8 cm).
  4. Abscess located in the Left Lobe superiorly (High risk of rupture into pericardium $\rightarrow$ fatal pericardial tamponade!).

B. Biliary Parasites: Ascariasis & Schistosomiasis

Ascariasis (Ascaris lumbricoides): Retrograde migration from duodenum into biliary duct. Presentation: Biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, or hepatic abscesses. ERCP shows linear filling defects in bile duct.
Tx: Medical (Piperazine citrate, Mebendazole, Albendazole) + ERCP extraction. Surgery (Common Bile Duct Exploration - CBDE) reserved if ERCP fails.

Schistosomiasis (S. japonicum / S. mekongi): Cercaria skin entry $\rightarrow$ hematogenous portal spread $\rightarrow$ presinusoidal portal fibrosis (Symmers' pipestem / lace-like pattern on US).
3 Clinical Stages: 1. Itching (skin entry) $\rightarrow$ 2. Fever, urticaria, eosinophilia (2-8 wks) $\rightarrow$ 3. Hepatic fibrosis & Portal HTN.
Tx: Praziquantel (40-75 mg/kg single dose). Surgery reserved for portal HTN complications.

IV. Benign & Malignant Liver Neoplasms

A. Benign Hepatic Lesions

LesionCharacteristics & DiagnosticsManagement
Congenital Cyst Most common benign cystic lesion. Asymptomatic if small. Large cause fullness/pain. Percutaneous aspiration + Sclerotherapy (ethanol). If fails: Laparoscopic unroofing/fenestration.
Hemangioma Most common benign solid lesion. Female > Male. Biphasic CT: Asymmetrical nodular peripheral enhancement. Observation. DO NOT BIOPSY due to lethal hemorrhage risk!
Hepatic Adenoma Premenopausal women >30yo; Linked to OCP use. Soft, encapsulated. 10-25% risk of rupture/intraperitoneal bleed. Premalignant (HCC). Surgical Resection indicated for all due to bleed/cancer risk. Stop OCPs.
Focal Nodular Hyperplasia (FNH) Hyperplastic response to anomalous artery. Biphasic CT: Central stellate scar. No rupture/cancer risk. Reassurance & Observation. Stop OCPs. Surgery only if symptomatic or Adenoma/HCC cannot be ruled out.
Biliary Cystadenoma Multiloculated, thick wall with septations. Premalignant potential to Cystadenocarcinoma. Complete Surgical Resection.

B. Primary & Metastatic Malignancies

Metastatic Carcinoma: MOST COMMON overall liver malignancy (Colorectal primary most common). Tumor marker: CEA.

1. Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

5th most common cancer, 3rd leading cause of cancer death worldwide. Risks: HBV, HCV, Alcoholic Cirrhosis, NASH, Hemochromatosis. AFP elevated.

Diagnostic Choice: Triphasic CT Scan (Hypervascular arterial enhancement with venous washout). Tends to invade Portal Vein (Portal Vein Thrombosis).

Management Criteria (Milan Criteria for Liver Transplant):

  • Single tumor $\le 5\text{ cm}$ OR up to 3 tumors all $\le 3\text{ cm}$.
  • No gross vascular invasion; No extrahepatic metastasis.

Resection candidate: Non-cirrhotic or Child-Pugh A with preserved function and no portal HTN.

2. Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA)

Bile duct adenocarcinoma (2nd primary liver cancer). Central (Hilar/Klatskin at duct confluence) presents with painless obstructive jaundice. Surgical resection or transplant if unresectable hilar $\le 3\text{ cm}$ with Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC).

C. Surgical Resection Guidelines & Brisbane 2000 Terminology

Incidental Gallbladder Cancer Resection Rules

  • T1a / T1b: Simple Cholecystectomy is definitive and adequate.
  • T2 / T3: Re-operation required: Central Liver Resection (Segments IVb & V) + Hilar Lymphadenectomy + Cystic duct stump evaluation.
  • T4: Unresectable; dismal survival with hepatectomy.

V. Spleen & Splenectomy

A. Splenic Anatomy & Physiology

Located in Left Upper Quadrant (LUQ), weighs ~150g (7-11 cm). Normal blood flow: 250-300 mL/min.

Arterial Supply: Splenic Artery (Celiac trunk branch). Two types: Distributed type (70%) with short trunk & long terminal branches; Magistrate type (30%) with long main trunk dividing near hilum.

Venous Drainage: Splenic Vein joins SMV to form the Portal Vein.

Suspensory Ligaments: Gastrosplenic, Phrenicocolic, Splenocolic. Must be transected to mobilize spleen during operation.

Functions: Mechanical filtration of senescent RBCs/microorganisms, iron recycling, extramedullary hematopoiesis, production of opsonins (Tuftsin, Properdin).

B. Surgical Indications for Splenectomy

Trauma: MOST COMMON INDICATION overall (blunt, penetrating, or iatrogenic operative injury).

  • Hereditary Spherocytosis: Membrane defect (spectrin/ankyrin). Hemolytic anemia, splenomegaly, gallstones. Splenectomy is curative for anemia. Perform concomitant Cholecystectomy if gallstones present!
  • Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency: Most common RBC enzyme defect causing congenital chronic hemolytic anemia $\rightarrow$ Splenectomy indicated.
  • G6PD Deficiency: Most common RBC enzyme defect overall. PITFALL Splenectomy has NO proven role! Avoid oxidative drugs.
  • Thalassemia Major: Splenectomy indicated if transfusion requirement exceeds $>200\text{ mL/kg/year}$ or painful infarction. AGE RULE Best performed at $\ge 4\text{ years}$ of age.
  • ITP (Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura): Autoimmune anti-platelet antibodies. Steroids first-line for 3 weeks. Splenectomy indicated for refractory ITP (75-85% permanent remission).
  • TTP (Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura): Plasmapheresis/FFP exchange is first line. Splenectomy for relapses.

C. Preoperative & Postoperative Complications

Overwhelming Post-Splenectomy Sepsis (OPSS)

Fulminant sepsis caused by encapsulated organisms:

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (>50% of cases)
  • Haemophilus influenzae type B
  • Neisseria meningitidis
  • Capnocytophaga canimorsus (after dog bites)
⭐
Vaccination Protocol:
• Elective Splenectomy: Administer vaccines 2 weeks PRIOR to surgery.
• Emergency Splenectomy: Delay administration until 2 weeks AFTER surgery to avoid transient postoperative immunosuppression.

Postoperative Complications & Techniques

  • Left Lower Lobe Atelectasis: Most common pulmonary complication post-open splenectomy.
  • Thrombosis (DVT / Portal Vein Thrombosis): High risk due to postoperative thrombocytosis. High suspicion if abdominal pain, fever, leukocytosis. Diagnose via CT; treat with immediate anticoagulation.
  • Surgical Technique: Place spleen in retrieval bag before extraction/morcellation to prevent Splenosis (auto-transplantation of splenic tissue across peritoneal cavity).

⚡ Ultra-Condensed Last-Minute Cram Sheet

Read in under 10 minutes. High-yield buzzwords, triggers, and clinical reasoning shortcuts.

🎯 "If You See X $\rightarrow$ Think Y" Clinical Reasoning

  • Epigastric venous hum: Cruveilhier-Baumgarten murmur $\rightarrow$ Portal Hypertension.
  • Rotten eggs/garlic breath: Fetor Hepaticus $\rightarrow$ Severe parenchymal liver failure.
  • "Anchovy paste" pus: Amebic Liver Abscess (E. histolytica).
  • Ring-like calcifications + cysts: Hydatid Cyst (Echinococcus granulosus).
  • Linear filling defect on ERCP: Biliary Ascariasis (Ascaris lumbricoides).
  • Pipestem periportal fibrosis: Schistosomiasis (S. japonicum).
  • Central stellate scar on CT: Focal Nodular Hyperplasia (FNH).
  • Solid lesion in young woman on OCPs: Hepatic Adenoma (Risk of rupture/bleed!).
  • Hypervascular tumor + portal vein thrombus: Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC).
  • Painless jaundice + hilar confluence mass: Klatskin Tumor (Extrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma).
  • Dog bite after splenectomy sepsis: Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.

⚡ Rapid Board Exam Rules

  • Liver Biopsy Contraindications: Coagulopathy, dilated bile ducts, suspected Hemangioma (bleeding!), suspected Hydatid cyst (anaphylaxis!).
  • Acute Variceal Bleed Hb Target: Transfuse to $\approx 8\text{ g/dL}$. Do NOT over-transfuse!
  • Sengstaken-Blakemore Tube Max Time: <36 hours to avoid esophageal pressure necrosis.
  • Hemangioma Rule: Most common benign solid lesion. NEVER BIOPSY!
  • Amebic Abscess Metronidazole Success: 95% cure. Drainage ONLY if left lobe superior location, failed meds, superinfection, or >5-8cm.
  • Vaccination Timing for Splenectomy: Elective = 2 weeks BEFORE; Emergency = 2 weeks AFTER.
  • Most Common Post-Splenectomy Pulmonary Complication: Left lower lobe atelectasis.
  • G6PD & Splenectomy: NO benefit from splenectomy in G6PD deficiency!
