1. Helminthic Infection Overview

Helminths (Greek helmins, "parasitic worm") are highly prevalent organisms that exist either free-living or as parasites in plant/animal hosts[cite: 1].

Major Groups

  • Nematodes (Roundworms): Long, cylindrical worms, many with tissue-migratory phases[cite: 1]. Includes Tissue Nematodes (Trichinella) and Intestinal Nematodes (Ascaris, Hookworms, Strongyloides, Trichuris, Enterobius), as well as Filariae[cite: 1].
  • Platyhelminthes (Flatworms): Includes Cestodes (tapeworms) and Trematodes (flukes)[cite: 1]. Examples of trematodes: Taenia, Echinococcus, Schistosoma, Fasciola, Paragonimus[cite: 1].
⭐ Must Know: Protozoa vs Helminths
CharacteristicProtozoaHelminths
StructureUnicellular[cite: 1]Multicellular; differentiated organ systems[cite: 1]
Life CycleOften can replicate extensively within humans[cite: 1]Complex; usually require development outside humans (maturation outside human host)[cite: 1]
Increase in parasite burdenA single exposure may rapidly produce high parasite loads[cite: 1]Usually requires repeated exogenous exposure[cite: 1]. Exception: Strongyloides stercoralis & Capillaria philippinensis can cause internal autoinfection[cite: 1].
⚠️ Clinical Pearl: Eosinophilia

Helminths elicit eosinophilia in tissues and blood correlating with the extent of tissue invasion by larvae or adult helminths[cite: 1]. It is utmost pronounced during the early phases (migrations of larvae)[cite: 1]. Acute schistosomiasis (Katayama syndrome), paragonimiasis, hookworm, and ascaris infections strongly stimulate this[cite: 1].

Caveat: Eosinophilia is not always present. Once confined to a specific site (e.g., echinococcal cysts, intestinal lumen), peripheral eosinophilia may become mild, intermittent, or absent[cite: 1].

Approach to a Patient

Use the mnemonic: Exposure → Anatomic site → Specimen → Treatment[cite: 1]. Start with exposure history (travel, freshwater, undercooked meat/fish, soil, insect vectors, animal contact). Then localize the infection based on the parasite's life cycle. Finally, choose the specimen that corresponds to where the parasite/eggs/larvae are expected to be found[cite: 1].

II. Tissue Nematodes

Definition/Etiology: Infection acquired by consumption of encysted larvae in undercooked pork, boar, horse, or bear[cite: 1].

Pathophysiology (Life Cycle): Ingested larvae release in stomach → mature to adults in small intestine → produce larvae that penetrate intestinal wall → enter circulation → penetrate striated muscle, reside in "nurse cells" and encyst[cite: 1].

Clinical Manifestations:

  • Enteric Phase: Diarrhea, abdominal pain, constipation, nausea (first weeks)[cite: 1].
  • Larval migration (2nd week): Marked systemic hypersensitivity (fever, eosinophilia)[cite: 1]. Periorbital/facial edema, subconjunctival/retinal/splinter hemorrhages[cite: 1]. Life-threatening myocarditis, encephalitis, pneumonitis[cite: 1].
  • Larval encystment (2-3 weeks): Myositis, myalgias, muscle edema. Commonly involves extraocular muscles, biceps, jaw, neck, lower back, diaphragm[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Eosinophilia (>90% symptomatic pts, peaking at 2-4 weeks)[cite: 1]. Elevated muscle enzymes (CK/CPK)[cite: 1]. Serology (positive after 3rd week)[cite: 1]. Definitive: Muscle biopsy showing larvae in hyalinized capsule (needs ≥ 1 g of tissue, routine histo may miss it)[cite: 1].

Figure 3: Trichinella larva encysted in a characteristic hyalinized capsule in striated muscle tissue.[cite: 1]

Treatment: Mild: Supportive (bed rest, antipyretics, analgesics)[cite: 1]. Severe myositis/myocarditis: Glucocorticoids (prednisone)[cite: 1]. Antihelminthic: Albendazole/Mebendazole (active against intestinal stages, efficacy against encysted larvae is uncertain)[cite: 1].

Pathophysiology: Tissue migration syndrome caused by nematode larvae of nonhuman hosts (most commonly Toxocara canis from dogs)[cite: 1]. Humans ingest embryonated eggs (contaminated soil/sand) → larvae hatch, penetrate intestine, enter circulation (hematogenous dissemination to Liver, CNS, eyes) but do not mature into adult worms in humans[cite: 1]. Larvae remain developmentally arrested, triggering eosinophilic inflammation[cite: 1].

Visceral Larva Migrans (VLM): Classic presentation is a child with hepatosplenomegaly, respiratory symptoms (cough/wheezing), fever, and marked eosinophilia (up to 90%)[cite: 1]. Diagnosis is clinical + serologic (Toxocara-specific ELISA). Stool examination is NOT useful because larvae don't mature to produce eggs in humans[cite: 1].

Ocular Larva Migrans (OLM): Usually older children/young adults. Unilateral visual disturbance, strabismus, eye pain[cite: 1]. Retinal granuloma at posterior pole that may mimic retinoblastoma (important to recognize to avoid unnecessary enucleation)[cite: 1]. Eosinophilia and serology may be absent/negative[cite: 1].

Treatment: Mild VLM is self-limited[cite: 1]. Moderate/Severe VLM: Albendazole ± corticosteroids[cite: 1]. Severe CNS/myocardial: Glucocorticoids[cite: 1].

III. Intestinal Nematodes

Life Cycle: Ingestion of embryonated eggs → larvae hatch in intestine → penetrate walls → circulate to lungs → ascend respiratory tract → swallowed → mature in small intestine into adult worms producing eggs[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Pulmonary migration causes cough, wheezing, and eosinophilia. Heavy intestinal infection causes anorexia, vomiting, abdominal distension, and partial intestinal obstruction[cite: 1].

Life Cycle: Feces → soil → rhabditiform to filariform larvae → skin penetration → bloodstream to lungs → swallowed → attach to small bowel mucosa[cite: 1]. Adults have buccal teeth (Ancylostoma) or cutting plates (Necator)[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Cutaneous larva migrans (localized itching/serpiginous rash at penetration site)[cite: 1]. Transient mild pulmonary symptoms[cite: 1]. Iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) is the major clinical consequence due to chronic blood loss from attached adult worms, especially with inadequate dietary iron[cite: 1].

Key Concept: Autoinfection. Rhabditiform larvae can transform into filariform larvae inside the host and penetrate intestinal/perianal mucosa, causing internal reinfection[cite: 1].

Hyperinfection: With immunosuppression (high-dose glucocorticoids, HTLV-1), autoinfection becomes uncontrolled. Massive larval dissemination occurs across bowel, lungs, CNS[cite: 1]. Migrating larvae carry enteric gram-negative bacteria, causing polymicrobial sepsis, colitis, pneumonitis, meningitis[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Larvae (not eggs) are the diagnostic stage in stool. Requires repeated testing. Eosinophilia fluctuates[cite: 1].

Treatment: Ivermectin is drug of choice. Urgent and prolonged therapy needed for hyperinfection[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Heavy infections cause IBD-like symptoms (bloody/mucoid diarrhea), anemia, and impaired physical growth[cite: 1]. Massive infections in children cause rectal prolapse due to protracted tenesmus[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Characteristic 50x20 µm lemon-shaped eggs with bipolar plugs in stool[cite: 1].

Treatment: Albendazole or Mebendazole (addition of ivermectin increases cure rates)[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Gravid females (dwelling in cecum) migrate out of the anus nocturnally to release eggs[cite: 1]. Cardinal symptom is nocturnal perianal pruritus[cite: 1]. Eosinophilia is uncommon[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Stool exam NOT useful. Test of choice: Perianal tape test in the morning before bathing. Eggs are D-shaped or planoconcave[cite: 1].

Treatment: Mebendazole, Albendazole, or Pyrantel pamoate. Must repeat after 2 weeks to kill hatched eggs. Treat household contacts[cite: 1].

Etiology/Location: Ingestion of raw fish. Endemic to Philippines and Thailand[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Undergoes autoinfection resulting in severe diarrhea, protein-losing enteropathy, malabsorption, profound weight loss, and death from cachexia or heart failure[cite: 1].

IV. Filarial Infections

Tissue nematodes transmitted by arthropod vectors (mosquitoes, flies). Clinical syndrome depends on location of adults and microfilariae[cite: 1].

1. Lymphatic Filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, Brugia timori)

Adult worms reside in lymphatic vessels/tissue causing obstruction[cite: 1].

  • Hydrocele: W. bancrofti in males causes scrotal fluid accumulation[cite: 1].
  • Elephantiasis: Progressive edema, fibrosis, and skin thickening from chronic damage[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Microfilariae in blood based on periodicity (nighttime sampling)[cite: 1]. High-frequency ultrasound with Doppler can show adult worms in scrotum/lymph nodes[cite: 1].

Treatment: Diethylcarbamazine (DEC)[cite: 1].

2. Loiasis (Loa loa)

Transmitted by Chrysops (deer flies). Microfilariae have diurnal periodicity (daytime blood sampling)[cite: 1]. Adults reside in subcutaneous tissue[cite: 1].

  • Calabar swellings: transient localized angioedema[cite: 1].
  • Subconjunctival worm: Passage of adult worm across the conjunctiva (classic sign)[cite: 1].

Treatment: DEC[cite: 1].

3. Onchocerciasis (Onchocerca volvulus)

Transmitted by Simulium (blackflies) breeding in fast rivers[cite: 1]. Adults in subcutaneous nodules; microfilariae in skin/eye (disease driven by microfilariae)[cite: 1].

  • Skin: Intense pruritus, thickening, atrophy, hyperpigmentation[cite: 1].
  • Eye: Ocular damage, corneal opacity, visual field constriction leading to classic river blindness[cite: 1].

Treatment: Ivermectin is mainstay against microfilariae. (DEC contraindicated as it causes severe inflammatory/ocular reactions)[cite: 1].

V. Platyhelminthes: Trematodes (Flukes)

Transmission: Infective cercariae released from freshwater snails penetrate intact human skin (no ingestion needed)[cite: 1]. Larvae enter circulation, mature into adults, and eggs are passed in stool/urine[cite: 1].

Clinical Phases/Types

  • Acute Schistosomiasis (Katayama Syndrome): 2 weeks to 3 months post-freshwater exposure. Fever, myalgia, hepatosplenomegaly, eosinophilia, transient pulmonary infiltrates[cite: 1].
  • Intestinal (S. mansoni, S. japonicum): Adults in mesenteric veins. Egg passage causes mucosal granulomatous inflammation, bloody diarrhea, pseudopolyposis[cite: 1].
  • Hepatosplenic (S. mansoni, S. japonicum): Eggs trapped in liver. Early: inflammatory hepatosplenomegaly. Late: Symmers (periportal/pipestem) fibrosis leading to portal hypertension, esophageal varices, recurrent hematemesis (fatal bleeding), but preserved hepatic cellular function[cite: 1].
  • Urogenital (S. haematobium): Eggs in urinary tract. Hallmark is painless, terminal hematuria. Chronic disease: sandy patches from calcified eggs, hydronephrosis, SCC of bladder[cite: 1].
  • Genital (S. haematobium): Women (contact bleeding, dyspareunia), Men (hemospermia)[cite: 1].
  • Ectopic/Neuro: S. mansoni/haematobium in spinal plexus (transverse myelitis). S. japonicum in brain (cerebral granulomas, seizures)[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Eggs in stool (mansoni/japonicum) or urine (haematobium). S. haematobium has a prominent terminal spine; S. mansoni has a prominent lateral spine; S. japonicum is rounded with small lateral knob[cite: 1].

Treatment: Praziquantel[cite: 1]. Note: Less effective against young migrating schistosoma; repeat dose 6-12 weeks later if symptoms/eosinophilia persist[cite: 1].

Contracted by ingestion of metacercariae in undercooked aquatic food/plants[cite: 1].

  • Liver Flukes (Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis): Raw fish. Migrate to intrahepatic bile ducts. Chronic infection causes recurrent cholangitis, cholelithiasis, and cholangiocarcinoma. Tx: Praziquantel[cite: 1].
  • Liver Fluke (Fasciola hepatica): Ingestion of aquatic plants (watercress). Larvae migrate through liver capsule causing tissue destruction/bleeding. Adults in bile ducts cause biliary obstruction. Tx: Triclabendazole (Praziquantel is NOT effective)[cite: 1].
  • Intestinal Flukes (Fasciolopsis buski): Aquatic plants/raw fish. Malabsorption, mucosal ulceration. Tx: Praziquantel (differentiates from Fasciola, though eggs look similar)[cite: 1].
  • Lung Flukes (Paragonimus westermani): Raw freshwater crustaceans (crabs/crayfish). Migrate to lungs. Causes fever, chronic cough, hemoptysis, cavitary lesions mimicking Tuberculosis. Tx: Praziquantel or triclabendazole[cite: 1].

VI. Platyhelminthes: Cestodes (Tapeworms)

Adult tapeworms (intestinal taeniasis) live in the GI tract of definitive hosts (humans). Larval cystic forms live in tissues of intermediate hosts[cite: 1].

  • Taenia saginata: Beef tapeworm. Human ingests undercooked beef[cite: 1].
  • Taenia asiatica: Pork tapeworm (Asia)[cite: 1].
  • Taenia solium: Pork tapeworm. Humans can be both definitive and intermediate hosts[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Eggs or proglottids in stool/perianal area. Tx: Praziquantel (single dose)[cite: 1].

⚠️ Pathogenesis Path

Ingestion of T. solium EGGS (not cysticerci) → oncospheres hatch in intestine → penetrate wall → hematogenous spread → cysticerci in CNS (parenchymal brain, CSF, spine)[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Headache, Seizures (most frequent, due to inflammation around cyst), Hydrocephalus/Increased ICP (from ventricular obstruction), Bruns' syndrome (intermittent acute hydrocephalus from ball-valve cyst)[cite: 1].

Treatment Principle: STABILIZE FIRST, TREAT PARASITE SECOND.

  • Manage CNS involvement (seizures, hydrocephalus/ICP) immediately[cite: 1]. Removal of intraventricular cyst via surgery/endoscopy is preferred for hydrocephalus[cite: 1].
  • Wait until stabilized with antiseizure and anti-inflammatory drugs (glucocorticoids) before anti-parasitic therapy, as dying parasites exacerbate cerebral edema[cite: 1]. Exclude ocular cysticercosis to prevent vision loss[cite: 1].
  • Antiparasitic therapy: Albendazole for 1 viable cyst. Albendazole + Praziquantel for >2 cysts[cite: 1].

Caused by larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus. Definitive hosts are canines (dogs shed eggs). Humans are dead-end intermediate hosts who ingest eggs[cite: 1].

Manifestations: Slowly enlarging cysts (mostly Liver, then Lungs). Asymptomatic until large enough to compress organs. Rupture/spillage can cause severe type I allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) and dissemination[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Imaging (Ultrasound shows bilateral well-defined cystic lesions, bilayered cyst walls, septations)[cite: 1].

Treatment: Individualized. PAIR (Puncture, Aspiration, Injection, Re-aspiration) for selected cysts, surrounded by Albendazole treatment 2 days prior and 4 weeks post[cite: 1]. Surgery for complicated cysts, using hypertonic saline drapes to prevent spillage[cite: 1].

Transmitted by raw fish. Tapeworms have avid receptors for Vitamin B12, interfering with absorption and producing Vitamin B12 deficiency (megaloblastic anemia)[cite: 1]. Tx: Praziquantel + Parenteral B12[cite: 1].

Ultimate Cram Sheet

  • Ascaris lumbricoides: Egg ingestion → lung migration (cough/eosinophilia) → swallowed → intestinal obstruction[cite: 1].
  • Hookworm: Filariform larvae skin penetration → lung migration → small intestine attachment → Iron Deficiency Anemia[cite: 1].
  • Strongyloides stercoralis: Skin penetration + Autoinfection. Immunosuppressed = Hyperinfection/dissemination. Tx: Ivermectin[cite: 1].
  • Trichuris trichiura: Lemon-shaped eggs with bipolar plugs. Severe cases = rectal prolapse[cite: 1].
  • Enterobius vermicularis: Pinworm. Nocturnal perianal itching. Diagnosis: morning perianal tape test[cite: 1].
  • Capillaria philippinensis: Raw fish → autoinfection → severe wasting/cachexia/malabsorption[cite: 1].
  • Wuchereria bancrofti: Mosquito vector. Lymphatic filariasis (hydrocele, elephantiasis). Nocturnal periodicity. Tx: DEC[cite: 1].
  • Loa loa: Deer fly. Daytime periodicity. Calabar swellings, conjunctival worm. Tx: DEC[cite: 1].
  • Onchocerca volvulus: Blackfly. Subcutaneous nodules, microfilariae in skin/eyes. River blindness. Tx: Ivermectin[cite: 1].
  • Schistosoma (General): Freshwater snail vector, cercariae penetrate skin. Acute = Katayama syndrome (fever, eosinophilia). Tx: Praziquantel[cite: 1].
  • Schistosoma mansoni/japonicum: Hepatosplenic disease. Symmers (pipestem) fibrosis → portal HTN, bleeding varices. Preserved liver fxn[cite: 1].
  • Schistosoma haematobium: Urogenital. Terminal hematuria, squamous cell CA risk, calcified eggs in bladder[cite: 1].
  • Clonorchis/Opisthorchis: Raw fish → liver flukes → biliary disease & cholangiocarcinoma[cite: 1].
  • Fasciola hepatica: Watercress/aquatic plants → liver migration. Tx: Triclabendazole (not praziquantel)[cite: 1].
  • Paragonimus westermani: Raw crab/crayfish → lung flukes. Mimics TB (cough, hemoptysis, cavitary lesions)[cite: 1].
  • Taenia solium: Ingesting infected pork = adult tapeworm (Taeniasis). Ingesting EGGS = Neurocysticercosis[cite: 1].
  • Neurocysticercosis Mgmt: Stabilize first (control seizures/ICP). Steroids before anti-parasitics. Surgical removal for ventricular cysts[cite: 1].
  • Echinococcus granulosus: Dog feces → humans ingest eggs → Hydatid cysts (Liver > Lungs). Risk of anaphylaxis if cyst ruptures. Tx: PAIR + Albendazole[cite: 1].
  • Diphyllobothrium latum: Raw fish. Competes for Vitamin B12 → Megaloblastic anemia[cite: 1].
  • Visceral Larva Migrans (Toxocara): Dog roundworm. Pica in kids. Hepatomegaly, marked eosinophilia. Stool exam useless (no adults in humans)[cite: 1].
