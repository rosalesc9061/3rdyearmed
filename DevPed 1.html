⭐ High-Yield Executive Summary

Developmental Pediatrics I Overview

Developmental pediatrics emphasizes early recognition to enable early intervention during the critical window of neuroplasticity (first 6 years of life)[cite: 1]. Pediatric disease burden has undergone a major paradigm shift from infectious/communicable conditions to non-communicable neurodevelopmental and behavioral disorders[cite: 1].

🎯 Core Paradigm Shift

Global burden measured via DALYs (Disability-Adjusted Life Years)[cite: 1]. High-risk infant survival (preterms, low birth weight) driven by NICU tech advancements has increased the prevalence of long-term neurodevelopmental morbidity[cite: 1].

🧠 Key Pillars of Evaluation

Combines detailed history (streams of development)[cite: 1], physical exam (anthropometrics, dysmorphisms, hard/soft neuro signs)[cite: 1], and standardized screening (PEDS, PEDS-DM, M-CHAT) at ages 9, 18, and 30 months[cite: 1].

⚡ Priority Pathology

Cerebral Palsy (static encephalopathy)[cite: 1], Language Disorders (ASD is now the #1 cause of language delay)[cite: 1], and Intellectual Disability (Down syndrome is the #1 genetic etiology)[cite: 1].

Primary Learning Objectives

  • Discuss the classification, epidemiology, and local/global prevalence of developmental disabilities[cite: 1].
  • Recognize red flag signs and presenting clinical manifestations of common motor, language, and cognitive disorders[cite: 1].
  • Execute systematic pediatric neurodevelopmental evaluation (history, physical, soft neuro signs, screening tools)[cite: 1].
  • Differentiate patterns of developmental abnormality: Delay vs. Dissociation vs. Deviance vs. Regression[cite: 1].
  • Formulate evidence-based diagnostic workups and multidisciplinary management plans for CP, Hearing Impairment, Language Disorders, and Down Syndrome[cite: 1].

⭐ Core Concept

Paradigm Shift in Pediatric Disease Burden

Over the past two decades, there has been a dramatic shift in pediatric outpatient and inpatient morbidity from communicable/infectious diseases to non-communicable neurodevelopmental and behavioral disorders[cite: 1].

Visual Diagram: Global Disease Burden Shift & DALY Concept

1900s: Communicable Diseases + Perinatal/Nutritional (Dominant)[cite: 1]

⬇ Advances in NICU Technology, Vaccines, Hygiene, Sanitation[cite: 1]

2020s: Non-Communicable Conditions (43%) + Neuropsychiatric Disorders (27%) + Injuries (15%)[cite: 1]

DALY (Disability-Adjusted Life Year) = Years of Life Lost (YLL) + Years Lived with Disability (YLD)

1 DALY = 1 Lost Year of Healthy Life[cite: 1].

💡 Basis for the Shift in Pediatric Consultations:
  • Newborn Consults: Shifted from checking basic physical completeness ("Kumpleto ba fingers/toes?") to evaluating neurological integrity and sensorimotor performance[cite: 1].
  • Well-Child Visits: Shifted from strictly tracking weight/height anthropometrics to assessing developmental milestone acquisition[cite: 1].
  • Chief Complaints: Shifted from standard acute medical illnesses (fever, cough, diarrhea) to primary parental concerns regarding language delays and atypical behaviors[cite: 1].

Factors Driving the Paradigm Shift

1. Advances in Medicine & Technology

  • Enhanced survival of high-risk, low birth weight (LBW), and severely premature infants[cite: 1].
  • Increased survival of neurologically compromised children who subsequently manifest long-term developmental impairments[cite: 1].
  • Establishment of specialized High-Risk Follow-Up Clinics (e.g., PCMC, PGH)[cite: 1].

2. Public Health & Societal Changes

  • Widespread immunization programs and sanitation reducing infant mortality from infectious diseases[cite: 1].
  • Social dynamics: OFW phenomenon, single-parent households, and both parents working leading to altered child-rearing environments[cite: 1].

⭐ Must Know Framework

Pediatric Neurodevelopmental Evaluation

A systematic evaluation consists of four mandatory components: Detailed History, Thorough Physical & Neurological Examination, Standardized Screening, and Targeted Laboratory Investigations[cite: 1].

📜 Component 1: Detailed History & Developmental Streams ▼

Developmental history must cover 4 major streams[cite: 1]:

  1. Gross Motor: MATURATION FOLLOWS A CEPHALOCAUDAL PATTERN (Head control → Sitting → Standing → Walking)[cite: 1].
  2. Fine Motor: Eye-hand coordination, grasping, releasing, writing[cite: 1].
  3. Verbal Speech & Language: Divided strictly into Receptive (comprehension) and Expressive (production)[cite: 1].
  4. Social & Self-Help: Personal-social interactions, dressing, feeding, toileting[cite: 1].
🧠 High-Yield Milestone Memory Rules (Language & Motor):
  • 12 Months: Walks independently (mean: 12m; critical red flag: 18m)[cite: 1]. Speaks 3 words other than "mama" & "dada"[cite: 1].
  • 18 Months: Uses at least 10 words with meaning[cite: 1]; follows simple 1-step directions[cite: 1].
  • 2.5 Years (30m): Combines 2-3 words into short phrases ("Mommy give", "Let's go")[cite: 1].
  • 3 Years: Speaks in simple sentences, states full name, age, and sex; 75% intelligible to strangers[cite: 1].
🩺 Component 2: Physical & Neurological Examination ▼
  • Anthropometry: Weight, Height/Length, Head Circumference (HC reflects brain growth; maximum growth in 1st 2-3 years)[cite: 1].
    • Average HC increase in 1st year = 10 cm[cite: 1].
    • At birth: $HC > CC$. At 6 months: $HC = CC$. At 12 months: $HC < CC$[cite: 1].
  • Dysmorphism Inspection: Look for classic syndromes (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome 45,XO)[cite: 1].
  • Hard vs. Soft Neurological Signs:
    • Hard Signs: Focal, fixed neurologic deficits (spasticity, hemiparesis, hyperreflexia)[cite: 1].
    • Soft Signs: Subtle motor/sensory integration impairments (finger-thumb apposition, left-right orientation beyond 5y, letter/number mirroring)[cite: 1]. Presence of multiple soft signs predicts higher risk of future behavioral/learning disabilities[cite: 1].
📊 Component 3: Screening vs. Surveillance vs. Evaluation ▼
Process Definition Performer & Tools
Surveillance Continuous process of identifying children at risk of delays during routine care[cite: 1] Primary Care Clinician; milestone tracking & observational history[cite: 1]
Screening Administration of brief, low-cost validated tools to refine recognized risk[cite: 1] Primary Care Clinician; PEDS, PEDS-DM, ASQ, M-CHAT at 9, 18, 30m[cite: 1]
Evaluation In-depth diagnostic assessment to formally identify specific disorders[cite: 1] Specialists (Developmental Pediatrician, Neurologist, Neuropsychologist)[cite: 1]
⚠️ Common Pitfalls & Abnormal Patterns

Patterns of Developmental Abnormalities

Screening identifies four distinct pathognomonic patterns of abnormal development[cite: 1]:

1. Delay

Lag in achieving milestones in one or all developmental streams[cite: 1]. Global Developmental Delay (GDD) is reserved for children <5 years with delay in ≥2 domains[cite: 1]. Most common reason for referral![cite: 1]

2. Dissociation

One developmental stream lags significantly behind other intact streams[cite: 1]. Classic Example: Cerebral Palsy (severe motor delay with normal IQ in spastic diplegia) or Learning Disability (normal verbal, poor reading)[cite: 1].

3. Deviance

Non-sequential, atypical skill acquisition[cite: 1]. Classic Example: Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) where a child can recite numbers/alphabets but cannot call parents "Mommy/Daddy" or gesture[cite: 1].

4. Regression

MOST WORRISOME PRESENTATION! Loss of previously acquired milestones[cite: 1]. Indicates severe neurological breakdown: Rett Syndrome, Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy, or neurodegenerative conditions[cite: 1].

⭐ Board Favorite Topic

Motor Delay & Cerebral Palsy (CP)

Motor delay is the single most common presentation of physical developmental disorders and is the sine qua non for the diagnosis of Cerebral Palsy[cite: 1].

Definition of Cerebral Palsy: A group of permanent, non-progressive motor and posture development disorders causing activity limitation, attributed to non-progressive disturbances (static encephalopathy) in the developing fetal or infant brain[cite: 1].

Motor Delay & Walking Rules

  • Mean Walking Age: 12 months (range: 9–17 months)[cite: 1].
  • CRITICAL RED FLAG: Inability to walk independently by 18 months (corrected age for preterms up to 2 years)[cite: 1].
  • Motor Quotient (MQ): $\text{MQ} = (\text{Motor Age} / \text{Chronological Age}) \times 100$. MQ < 70 defines Motor Delay (MQ < 50 is Moderate/Severe)[cite: 1].

Etiology & Timing of CP Insults

  • Prenatal (75%): Brain malformations, intrauterine TORCH infections[cite: 1].
  • Perinatal (8-10%): Birth asphyxia (APGAR 0-3 at 5 min), hypoglycemia, kernicterus[cite: 1].
  • Postnatal (10%): Traumatic brain injury, bacterial meningitis[cite: 1].
  • Preterm Vulnerability: Periventricular Leukomalacia (PVL) and Subependymal/Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH) due to oligodendroglia oxidative vulnerability (20-34w AOG)[cite: 1].

🧠 Diagnostic Criteria & Functional Staging

Levine (POSTER) Diagnostic Criteria for CP

Requires presence of multiple clinical domain abnormalities[cite: 1]:

  • Posture and abnormal movement patterns (extensor thrust)[cite: 1]
  • Oropharyngeal dysfunction (tongue thrust, swallowing difficulty, drooling)[cite: 1]
  • Strabismus[cite: 1]
  • Tone alterations (hypertonia/spasticity or hypotonia)[cite: 1]
  • Evolutional responses (persistent primitive reflexes, absent protective reflexes)[cite: 1]
  • Reflexes (hyperreflexia, clonus, upgoing +Babinski)[cite: 1]

Gross Motor Function Classification Scale (GMFCS)

  • Level I: Walks without restrictions; climbs stairs without handrails[cite: 1].
  • Level II: Walks without mobility devices but limitations outdoors/in crowd[cite: 1].
  • Level III: Walks using hand-held mobility devices (crutches, walker)[cite: 1].
  • Level IV: Self-mobility limited; transported in power chair or adult assistance[cite: 1].
  • Level V: Severely limited; completely dependent for physical mobility[cite: 1].

⭐ Diagnostic Priority Rule

Language & Communication Disorders

Language delay is the most common chief complaint brought by parents during the preschool years[cite: 1]. Affects 20% of 2-year-olds and 6-8% of 5-year-olds (Male:Female ratio = 2:1)[cite: 1].

GOLDEN DIAGNOSTIC RULE: Whenever evaluating ANY child presenting with speech or language delay, the FIRST MANDATORY DIAGNOSTIC STEP is to perform a formal AUDIOLOGICAL EVALUATION to rule out hearing impairment[cite: 1]! Never rely on informal office noise tests[cite: 1].

Language Red Flags by Age

  • 6 Months: No responsive cooing[cite: 1].
  • 10 Months: No babbling[cite: 1].
  • 12 Months: No basic gesturing (waving bye-bye, pointing)[cite: 1].
  • 18 Months: No single words beyond mama/dada (<10 words); fails to follow 1-step directions[cite: 1].
  • 24 Months: <50 words; no spontaneous 2-word phrases; <50% speech intelligibility[cite: 1].
  • 36 Months: No 3-word sentences; <75% speech intelligibility[cite: 1].

High-Risk Indicators for Hearing Loss (AAP)

  • Family history of congenital SNHL[cite: 1].
  • In utero TORCH infections (CMV, Rubella, Syphilis, Toxoplasmosis)[cite: 1].
  • Hyperbilirubinemia requiring exchange transfusion[cite: 1].
  • Birthweight <1500 grams; NICU stay >5 days[cite: 1].
  • Bacterial meningitis (H. influenzae carries 30% SNHL risk)[cite: 1].
  • Ototoxic drugs (Gentamicin) + loop diuretics >5 days[cite: 1].

DSM-5-TR Communication Disorders Subtypes

Disorder Subtype Core Clinical Features Diagnostic Clues & Management
Language Disorder (DLD / SLI) Significant gap between normal nonverbal IQ and impaired verbal comprehension/expression[cite: 1]. Diagnosis of exclusion[cite: 1]. 62% have an affected parent (strong genetic basis)[cite: 1]. Requires speech therapy[cite: 1].
Speech Sound Disorder Phonological impairment (intelligibility) & articulation errors (substitutions/distortions)[cite: 1]. Child speaks fluently but is unintelligible[cite: 1]. At high risk for future reading/learning disabilities[cite: 1].
Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder Deficits in social use of language, nonverbal cues, understanding humor/metaphor/sarcasm[cite: 1]. Core feature of ASD spectrum[cite: 1]. Also seen in right hemisphere brain injury and Williams syndrome[cite: 1].
Selective Mutism Consistently fails to speak in specific social settings (e.g., school) despite speaking normally at home[cite: 1]. Manifestation of an Anxiety Disorder[cite: 1]! Speech therapy NOT primary; requires behavioral/psychiatric therapy[cite: 1].
Isolated Expressive Language Disorder "Late Talkers": normal receptive language & social interaction, but sparse spoken vocabulary[cite: 1]. Benign variant of normal ("late bloomers")[cite: 1]. Male dominance, positive family history[cite: 1]. Diagnosis made in retrospect[cite: 1].
💡 Hearing Loss Classification & Staging

Degree of Hearing Loss (Pure Tone Average)

  • Normal: 10 – 15 dB HL[cite: 1]
  • Slight: 16 – 25 dB HL[cite: 1]
  • Mild: 26 – 40 dB HL (Most common cause: Chronic Otitis Media with Effusion)[cite: 1]
  • Moderate: 41 – 55 dB HL[cite: 1]
  • Moderately Severe: 56 – 70 dB HL[cite: 1]
  • Severe: 71 – 90 dB HL[cite: 1]
  • Profound: >91 dB HL (Deafness definition)[cite: 1]

Hearing Evaluation Methods & Goals

1-3-6 Benchmark Rule: Screening by 1 month, Diagnosis by 3 months, Intervention by 6 months[cite: 1]!

  • OAE (Otoacoustic Emissions): Rapid universal newborn screening[cite: 1].
  • ABR / BERA: Automated Auditory Brainstem Response; differentiates SNHL vs conductive loss at any age[cite: 1].
  • Cochlear Implants: Indicated for severe-profound SNHL. Golden window: 1.0 to 3.5 years old[cite: 1].
⭐ High-Yield Genetic Pathology

Intellectual Disability (ID) & Down Syndrome

Intellectual Disability (formerly Mental Retardation) is defined by onset before 18 years, significant deficits in intellectual functioning (IQ ≥2 SD below mean, typically <70), and impaired adaptive functioning across conceptual, social, and practical domains[cite: 1].

Diagnostic Criteria & Terminology

  • Under 5 Years: Diagnosis of Global Developmental Delay (GDD) is applied when delay is present in ≥2 developmental streams[cite: 1].
  • Global Prevalence: ~2.5% of the general population[cite: 1].
  • Etiology Spectrum: Chromosomal disorders (20%), Genetic syndromes (20%), Unknown/Multifactorial (20%), Environmental/Perinatal insults[cite: 1].

Classification by IQ Level

  • Borderline (IQ 70-84): "Slow learner"[cite: 1].
  • Mild ID (IQ 50-69): "Educable" (85% of ID); can reach 6th-grade academic level, achieve self-support[cite: 1].
  • Moderate ID (IQ 35-49): "Trainable"; can acquire self-help skills and simple supervised work[cite: 1].
  • Severe (IQ 20-34) / Profound (IQ <20): "Subtrainable"; requires continuous lifelong care & supervision[cite: 1].
⭐ Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) Focus

Down Syndrome: Cytogenetics & Clinical Manifestations

Down syndrome is the most common genetic etiology of moderate intellectual disability (1 in 733 live births)[cite: 1].

Cytogenetic Subtype Percentage Genomic Mechanism & Recurrence Risk
Full Trisomy 21 95%[cite: 1] Meiotic nondisjunction (95% maternal origin). Strongly correlated with advanced maternal age[cite: 1].
Robertsonian Translocation 4%[cite: 1] Translocation (usually t(14;21) or t(21;21)). Inherited from carrier parent! High recurrence risk in future pregnancies[cite: 1].
Mosaicism 1%[cite: 1] Post-zygotic mitotic error. Normal + Trisomy cell lines. Milder clinical phenotype and higher IQ potential[cite: 1].

Neonatal Signs & Dysmorphisms

  • HYPOTONIA: Single most important physical finding in neonates[cite: 1]!
  • Poor Moro reflex, joint hyperflexibility, excess nuchal skin[cite: 1].
  • Flat facial profile, epicanthal folds, upward slanting palpebral fissures, flat nasal bridge, protruding tongue[cite: 1].
  • Clinodactyly (inward curving of 5th finger), Sandal gap toes (1st-2nd toe space), Simian crease (single palmar crease - NOT pathognomonic)[cite: 1].

Major Systemic Complications

  • Congenital Heart Disease (40-50%): Most life-threatening! Endocardial cushion defect / AVSD, VSD[cite: 1].
  • GI Anomalies (10%): Duodenal atresia ("double bubble"), Hirschsprung disease[cite: 1].
  • Sensory Deficits (50%): Refractive errors, cataracts, conductive/SNHL hearing loss[cite: 1].
  • Hematologic / Neuro: Acute Leukemia (ALL/AMKL), Early-onset Alzheimer's dementia by age 40 (15-20%)[cite: 1].

10-Minute Rapid Board Cram Sheet

High-yield buzzwords, immediate "If You See X → Think Y" associations, and non-negotiable exam numbers designed for pre-exam blitzing[cite: 1].

🚨 "If You See X → Think Y" Clinical Reasoning Rules

If you see: Premature infant crawling by dragging spastic legs like a rudder ("Commando Crawl")

Think: Spastic Diplegic Cerebral Palsy (Periventricular Leukomalacia targeting lower extremities)[cite: 1].

If you see: Strong hand preference or hand dominance BEFORE 18 months of age

Think: Spastic Hemiplegic Cerebral Palsy (Unilateral stroke/MCA infarct; affected hand is weak)[cite: 1].

If you see: Full-term neonate with severe asphyxia presenting with continuous writhing movements (athetosis)

Think: Dyskinetic CP / Kernicterus (Bilateral Globus Pallidus & Thalamus injury)[cite: 1].

If you see: Marked neonatal hypotonia, poor Moro, hyperflexibility, and flat facial profile

Think: Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) (Order karyotyping; check for endocardial cushion defect)[cite: 1].

If you see: Child speaks fluently and normally at home but has total absence of speech at school

Think: Selective Mutism (Anxiety disorder manifestation; needs behavioral therapy, not speech therapy)[cite: 1].

If you see: Child reciting alphabets/numbers perfectly but unable to say "Mommy" or gesture

Think: Developmental DEVIANCE in Autism Spectrum Disorder[cite: 1].
