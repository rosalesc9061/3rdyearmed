NEUROLOGY 2: CNS INFECTIONS REVIEWER

Fungal, Prion, Brain Abscess, Spirochetal, & Parasitic Pathogens | PLE & Academic Board Standard

Core Learning Objectives & Diagnostic Approach

Mastering central nervous system (CNS) infections requires immediate syndrome identification, precise neuro-anatomical localization, pattern interpretation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profiles, and rapid initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy[cite: 1].

⭐ MUST KNOW: Systematic Diagnostic Algorithmic Sequence
  1. Start with the Clinical Syndrome: Classify into Acute Meningitis (<1 week), Encephalitis, Chronic Meningitis (>2 weeks), Focal Parenchymal Mass/Infection, Myelitis, or Rapidly Progressive Dementia[cite: 1].
  2. Localize the Pathology: Meninges, brain parenchyma, perforating cerebral microvessels, or ventricular CSF pathways[cite: 1].
  3. Rule Out Space-Occupying Lesions FIRST: Contrast Neuroimaging (MRI + DWI or CT) MUST precede Lumbar Puncture (LP) in brain abscess or mass effect to prevent brainstem herniation[cite: 1].
  4. Interpret CSF Patterns Holistically: Never judge an isolated CSF metric; analyze pressure, cell count, differential, protein, and glucose concurrently[cite: 1].

⚠️ Common Examination Pitfall

Do NOT rely on normal/low CSF cell counts to rule out fungal meningitis in severely immunocompromised individuals (e.g., advanced HIV/AIDS, CD4 < 100/µL)[cite: 1]. Muted cell-mediated immunity suppresses inflammatory pleocytosis, yielding CSF leukocyte counts ≤5 cells/µL in up to two-thirds of HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis cases[cite: 1].

Tuberculous Meningitis (TBM)

Highly prevalent in the Philippines[cite: 1]. Pathologically features a thick, gelatinous inflammatory exudate preferentially accumulating in the basal cisterns[cite: 1].

Anatomical Triad of Complications in Basal Meningeal Inflammation

1. Cranial Neuropathies: Direct entrapment of cranial nerves running through basal cisterns (CN III, VI ocular motor palsies)[cite: 1].

2. Vasculitis & Infarctions: Inflammatory arteritis of perforating vessels (e.g., lenticulostriate branches) → Internal capsule and basal ganglia ischemic stroke[cite: 1].

3. Communicating Hydrocephalus: Dense exudates block CSF flow through basal pathways and impair arachnoid villi resorption → Progressive raised ICP and altered consciousness[cite: 1].

💡 Clinical Pearl & Diagnostic Sensitivity

AFB smear sensitivity on initial CSF is low (<30%) unless severe[cite: 1]. Always send CSF for TB PCR / NAAT and obtain contrast MRI showing intense basal leptomeningeal enhancement, ventriculomegaly, and subcortical ischemic lesions[cite: 1]. Initiate empiric anti-TB therapy immediately upon high clinical suspicion[cite: 1].

Cryptococcal Meningitis (Cryptococcus neoformans / C. gattii)

Acquired via inhalation of aerosolized spores from bird/pigeon roosts into the lungs[cite: 1]. Major cause of opportunistic infection in advanced HIV (6–12% prevalence) and immunocompetent adults[cite: 1].

⭐ MUST KNOW: Pathognomonic Features & Management
  • Indistinct/Indolent Presentation: Fever, headache, and meningismus are absent in up to 40% of non-HIV patients[cite: 1]. Frequently presents with confusional states, dementia, papilledema (50%), or pure motor lacunar-like stroke[cite: 1].
  • Neuroimaging Hallmark: Dilated Virchow-Robin perivascular spaces filled with capsular mucopolysaccharides producing "soap-bubble" gelatinous pseudocysts in the basal ganglia and thalamus with minimal enhancement[cite: 1].
  • Primary Rapid Diagnostics: Cryptococcal Antigen Latex Agglutination System (CALAS, >90% sensitivity)[cite: 1]. India Ink preparation demonstrates 5–15 µm halo-encapsulated budding yeast cells in 75% of cases (requires 20–40 mL CSF yield)[cite: 1].
  • ICP Control Protocol: Markedly elevated opening pressure. Require daily therapeutic LPs until opening pressure stays <25 cm H2O for ≥2 consecutive days[cite: 1].
  • Antifungal Regimen:
    • Induction: Liposomal Amphotericin B (3–4 mg/kg/day) + Flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day) x ≥2 weeks[cite: 1].
    • Consolidation: Fluconazole 400–800 mg/day x 8 weeks[cite: 1].
    • Maintenance: Fluconazole 200 mg/day x 1 year[cite: 1]. Discontinue in HIV only if CD4 > 100/µL and viral load suppressed[cite: 1].

Brain Abscess: Pathophysiology & Anatomical Origins

Focal suppurative infection within brain parenchyma starting as localized cerebritis and evolving into a capsule-enclosed purulent cavity[cite: 1].

⭐ Primary Spread Routes & Anatomic Predictions
  • Contiguous Spread (40–50%):
    • Otitis Media / Mastoiditis: Temporal lobe or Cerebellar abscess[cite: 1].
    • Paranasal/Frontal Sinusitis: Frontal lobe abscess[cite: 1].
    • Sphenoid Sinusitis: Temporal lobe or Sella turcica abscess[cite: 1].
  • Hematogenous / Metastatic Spread (25–35%): Secondary to bacteremia, endocarditis, or pulmonary arteriovenous malformations. Characteristically produces multiple lesions located at the gray-white matter junction in the distribution of the middle cerebral artery[cite: 1].
4 Pathological Stages from Cerebritis to Abscess Formation

1. Early Cerebritis (Days 1–3): Uncontained local edema, intense vascular congestion, microglial accumulation, and focal early coagulative necrosis[cite: 1].

2. Late Cerebritis (Days 3–7): Progression to central liquefactive necrosis surrounded by maximal vasogenic edema and early reticulin meshwork network[cite: 1].

3. Early Encapsulation (Days 5–7): Neovascularization and fibroblastic collagen deposition forming a thin peripheral rim[cite: 1].

4. Late Encapsulation (>7 Days): Well-defined, thick, collagenous hyper-enhancing capsule with necrotic central core and variable surrounding edema reduction[cite: 1].

Clinical Presentation, Diagnostics & Management Protocol

⚠️ ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: Lumbar Puncture Warning

DO NOT perform a Lumbar Puncture when a brain abscess or intracranial mass effect is suspected! LP has negligible diagnostic utility for parenchymal abscesses and carries a life-threatening risk of uncal or tonsillar brain herniation due to high pressure gradients[cite: 1].

Anatomical Lobe Dominant Clinical Manifestations Diagnostic & Management Actions
Frontal Lobe Personality changes, apathy, motor weakness, expressive dysphasia, contralateral hemiparesis[cite: 1]. 1. Primary Neuroimaging: Contrast-enhanced MRI with Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI). Hallmark: Central restricted diffusion (bright on DWI, dark on ADC) with a smooth, uniformly hyper-enhancing peripheral ring[cite: 1].

2. Microbiologic Workup: Blood cultures x 2 pairs, sinus/dental evaluation[cite: 1].

3. Empiric Antimicrobial Therapy: High-dose IV 3rd Gen Cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone) + Metronidazole (for anaerobes) +/- Vancomycin (if post-surgical or Staph suspected)[cite: 1].

4. Surgical Intervention: Early neurosurgical stereotactic aspiration or excision for source control and decompression[cite: 1].
Temporal Lobe Receptive Wernicke's aphasia, homonymous superior quadrantanopia ("pie-in-the-sky" visual defect)[cite: 1].
Cerebellum Ipsilateral limb ataxia, nystagmus, dysmetria, threat of 4th ventricle compression → acute obstructive hydrocephalus[cite: 1].
Cortical Regions Focal or generalized epileptic seizures, raised ICP headache[cite: 1].

Prion Biology & Conformational Pathogenesis

Prion diseases are universally fatal transmissible neurodegenerative disorders caused by structural misfolding of normal cellular prion protein ($PrP^C$) into an abnormal disease-associated isoform ($PrP^{Sc}$)[cite: 1].

⭐ Molecular Mechanism & Decontamination Imperative
  • Biophysical Transformation: $PrP^C$ undergoes a conformational shift resulting in a marked loss of $\alpha$-helical content and a major increase in $\beta$-pleated sheet structure[cite: 1]. $PrP^{Sc}$ acts as a template, forcing native $PrP^C$ to misfold and aggregate, causing spongiform neuronal vacuolation, gliosis, and synaptic death without inciting any conventional inflammatory or immune response[cite: 1].
  • Gene Location: Encoded by the PRNP gene located on the short arm of Chromosome 20[cite: 1].
  • Extreme Resistance & Sterilization Standards: Prions resist standard formalin, alcohol, boiling, and UV irradiation[cite: 1]. Mandatory decontamination methods for clinical/surgical tools:
    1. Autoclaving at $132^\circ\text{C}$ at $15\text{ lb/in}^2$ pressure for 1 hour[cite: 1].
    2. Immersion in 5% Sodium Hypochlorite (bleach) for 1 hour[cite: 1].
    3. Incineration of disposable materials[cite: 1].

Sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (sCJD)

The prototypical human prion disease[cite: 1]. Peak onset in late middle age (60–70 years), affecting sexes equally[cite: 1]. Characterized by subacute, rapidly progressive dementia evolving over weeks to months, accompanied by myoclonus[cite: 1].

💡 Clinical Variants & Key Diagnostic Biomarkers
  • Stimulus-Sensitive Myoclonus: Sudden, startle-induced myoclonic jerks triggered by loud noises, bright lights, or tactile stimuli[cite: 1]. Note: Well-formed motor seizures are NOT a standard component[cite: 1].
  • Heidenhain Variant: Early prominent cortical visual disturbances (e.g., visual agnosia, metamorphopsia, cortical blindness) preceding mental decline[cite: 1].
  • Brownell-Oppenheimer Variant: Early prominent cerebellar gait and limb ataxia preceding cognitive decay[cite: 1].
  • Neuroimaging Hallmark (MRI DWI/FLAIR): High sensitivity (>90%). Displays asymmetric focal hyperintensity and diffusion restriction along the cerebral cortex ("Cortical Ribboning") and striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen)[cite: 1].
  • Electroencephalography (EEG): Stereotyped 1–2 Hz periodic, synchronous, sharp-wave complexes (PSWCs) on a slow, low-voltage background[cite: 1].
  • CSF Biomarkers: RT-QuIC (Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion) offers ultra-high diagnostic specificity by amplifying minute prion protein seeds[cite: 1]. Positive CSF 14-3-3 protein and total tau are supportive markers of rapid neuronal necrosis[cite: 1].
⚠️ Common Examination Pitfall: CJD Treatable Mimics

Never diagnose CJD without actively ruling out reversible, treatable mimics of rapidly progressive dementia: Autoimmune Encephalitis (especially anti-LGI1 / anti-NMDAR), HSV Encephalitis, Neurosyphilis, Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus, Hashimoto Encephalopathy, Hepatic/Metabolic Encephalopathy, Wernicke Encephalopathy, CNS Lymphoma, and Primary CNS Vasculitis[cite: 1].

Atypical Human Prion Disease Phenotypes

Disease Entity Genetics / Transmission Clinical Features Pathology & Prognosis
Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker (GSS) Syndrome Autosomal dominant inherited PRNP mutation[cite: 1]. Insidious midlife onset. Prominent progressive cerebellar ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, corticospinal tract signs, leg dysesthesias; dementia is late/mild[cite: 1]. PAS-positive, stellate, amyloid-like "Kuru plaques" in cerebellar cortex[cite: 1]. Prolonged duration (mean 5 years)[cite: 1].
Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI) Autosomal dominant mutation at PRNP Codon 178 combined with Methionine at Codon 129[cite: 1]. Intractable disabling insomnia, severe autonomic dysregulation (hyperhidrosis, tachycardia, hypertension), dysarthria, and dementia[cite: 1]. Severe neuronal loss and astrogliosis localized selectively to the medial thalamic nuclei[cite: 1]. Death in 7–15 months[cite: 1].
Kuru ("Laughing Sickness") Transmitted via endocannibalistic funeral rituals involving handling/ingestion of brain tissue among Fore people of Papua New Guinea[cite: 1]. Afebrile progressive cerebellar ataxia, extraocular movement defects, severe tremors, emotional lability/pathological laughter, mutism[cite: 1]. Noninflammatory neuronal loss and cerebellar Kuru plaques[cite: 1]. Disappeared after cessation of cannibalism[cite: 1]. Death in 3–6 months[cite: 1].

Neurocysticercosis (Taenia solium)

The single leading cause of acquired adult epilepsy in developing nations and endemic regions, including the Philippines[cite: 1]. Acquired via fecal-oral ingestion of T. solium eggs (from human feces), NOT by eating undercooked pork (which causes intestinal taeniasis)[cite: 1].

The 4 Radiographic Stages of Parenchymal Neurocysticercosis

1. Vesicular Stage: Viable fluid-filled cyst containing an eccentric hyperdense scolex ("dot in a cyst"). Little or no host edema[cite: 1].

2. Colloidal Degenerative Stage: Cyst death initiates host immune response → Intense pericystic ring enhancement with prominent vasogenic edema[cite: 1].

3. Granular-Nodular Stage: Cyst collapse, wall thickening, nodular enhancement with decreasing surrounding edema[cite: 1].

4. Calcified Stage: Complete resolution into small, dense, non-enhancing calcified punctate nodules. Best visualized on non-contrast CT[cite: 1].

🧠 Exam Tip: Treatment Protocol & Racemose Variant
  • Anthelminthic Therapy: Albendazole (5 mg/kg TID x 15–30 days) or Praziquantel[cite: 1].
  • MANDATORY Corticosteroids: Co-administer High-Dose Corticosteroids (Prednisone or Dexamethasone) BEFORE starting anthelmintics! Dying parasites trigger an intense host inflammatory flare that acutely exacerbates cerebral edema and seizures[cite: 1].
  • Racemose Variant: Proliferating multi-lobulated cysts located in the basilar subarachnoid space causing malignant arachnoiditis, hydrocephalus, stroke, and cranial nerve palsies[cite: 1]. Poorly responsive to antihelminthics; requires shunt placement[cite: 1].

Protozoal & Rickettsial Infections

Pathogen Epidemiology / Transmission Key Clinical Manifestations Diagnostic & Therapeutic Regimen
Cerebral Toxoplasmosis
(Toxoplasma gondii)		 Ingestion of raw meat or cat feces exposure[cite: 1]. #1 focal brain lesion in AIDS (CD4 < 100/µL)[cite: 1]. Multiple focal ring-enhancing basal ganglia abscesses with mass effect/edema[cite: 1]. Seizures, focal deficits[cite: 1]. (Naegleria fowleri)		 Swimming underwater in warm freshwater lakes[cite: 1]. Amoeba penetrates cribriform plate[cite: 1]. Fulminant necrotizing hemorrhagic meningoencephalitis[cite: 1]. Cerebral Malaria
(Plasmodium falciparum)		 Anopheles mosquito vector[cite: 1]. Parasitized RBCs clog cerebral microvessels → Dürck nodes (glial necrosis foci)[cite: 1]. Encephalopathy, seizures, coma[cite: 1]. Pathognomonic sign: Malarial Retinopathy (macular whitening, vessel discoloration, blot hemorrhages)[cite: 1]. First-line: IV Artesunate[cite: 1]. Alternative: IV Quinine[cite: 1]. African Trypanosomiasis
(T. brucei)		 Tsetse fly vector[cite: 1]. Systemic chancre → Chronic meningoencephalitis[cite: 1]. Winterbottom Sign: Posterior cervical lymphadenopathy[cite: 1]. Kerandel Hyperesthesia: Severe pain from minor trauma[cite: 1]. Circadian sleep-wake reversal ("Sleeping sickness")[cite: 1]. Late CNS Stage: Eflornithine + Nifurtimox[cite: 1]. Melarsoprol carries 10% risk of reactive fatal encephalopathy[cite: 1].
Rickettsioses
(Typhus, RMSF, Q Fever)		 Arthropod vectors (lice, fleas, ticks, mites)[cite: 1]. Vasculitis via microvascular endothelial invasion[cite: 1]. High fever, severe headache, macular rash (day 4-5)[cite: 1]. Scrub typhus: Necrotic eschar at mite site[cite: 1]. Q Fever (Coxiella burnetii): NO skin rash; atypical pneumonia/meningitis[cite: 1]. Doxycycline (or Chloramphenicol)[cite: 1]. High efficacy when started early[cite: 1].
