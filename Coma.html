1. Definition of Terms & Spectrum of Consciousness

Consciousness represents a state of awareness of self and environment with normal responsiveness to external stimulation and inner needs. Unconsciousness is the suspension of mental activities by which people are aware of self and environment.

⭐
MUST KNOW: Differentiating Drowsiness vs. Stupor
• Drowsiness: Verbal command/stimulus is required to overcome drowsiness (e.g., calling their name). Easily brought back to brief alertness.
• Stupor: Noxious, vigorous, or painful stimulation is strictly required to overcome stupor. Upon cessation of pain, the patient immediately drifts back into a sleep-like state.

Detailed Breakdowns of Specific Chronic/Subacute Conditions

A. Vegetative State (Unresponsive Wakefulness) & Persistent Vegetative State (PVS)

An awake but unconscious state appearing after survival from severe TBI or non-traumatic brain injury. Patients regain sleep-wake cycles and spontaneous eye opening, but demonstrate 0 meaningful mental function or environmental awareness.

  • Pathophysiology & Anatomy: Widespread neuronal loss in the cortex, diffuse axonal injury (DAI), or prominent necrosis in the thalamic and subthalamic nuclei.
  • Features: Spontaneous eye opening/blinking (gives family erroneous impression of recognition), primitive automatisms (swallowing, bruxism, grimacing, grunting), loss of sphincter control, lack of consistent visual tracking.
  • Timeframes for PVS: Persists for $\ge 3\text{ months}$ after non-traumatic injury (e.g., stroke, cardiac arrest) OR $\ge 12\text{ months}$ after traumatic injury (TBI).
  • EEG / PET Findings: Polymorphic low-amplitude delta/theta background, burst suppression, alpha coma pattern, sleep spindles, and absence of background EEG change during external stimulation. PET shows cerebral metabolic rate and blood flow reduced by $\ge 50\%$.
💡
CLINICAL PEARL: Visual Tracking
The absence of consistent visual following/tracking of objects is a major hallmark differentiating Vegetative State from Minimally Conscious State (MCS).

B. Minimally Conscious State (MCS)

The patient is inconsistently capable of rudimentary behavior demonstrating awareness—such as following a simple command, gesturing "yes/no", producing single words or brief phrases, or reaching for objects.

C. Locked-In Syndrome (De-efferented State)

Complete disruption of motor output with full preservation of consciousness, cognition, sensation, and arousal systems.

  • Anatomic Lesion: Large bilateral lesion of the ventral pons (basis pontis), most commonly due to basilar artery occlusion or pontine hemorrhage.
  • Sparing: Spares ascending reticular activating system (ARAS), somatosensory tracts, and midbrain structures controlling levator palpebrae (eyelid elevation) and vertical eye movements.
  • Interruption: Bilateral corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts are completely severed $\rightarrow$ quadriplegia and anarthria. Communication occurs strictly via vertical eye movements and blinking.

D. Akinetic Mutism

State where the patient is motionless (akinetic) and silent (mute), appearing awake but completely lacking the psychic drive or impulse to act (abulia). Caused by bilateral anterior frontal lobe lesions or thalamic lesions (e.g., ACA/ACom aneurysms, tumors).

E. Catatonia

A psychiatric condition simulating stupor, light coma, or akinetic mutism. Characterized by waxy flexibility (*flexibilitas cerea* - bending limbs like a wax rod), catalepsy (maintaining uncomfortable postures for long periods), and normal pupillary light reflexes. Oculocephalic responses are muted (Doll's eye may appear negative due to active visual fixation/resistance).

⚠️
COMMON PITFALL: Misinterpreting Eye Opening in PVS
Family members frequently believe a patient in a Vegetative State is "waking up" because they open their eyes or blink when spoken to. Educate families that spontaneous eye opening reflects brainstem sleep-wake reticular circuits, NOT cortical awareness or recognition.

2. Brain Death & Diagnostic Criteria

Historically termed coma dépassé (1950s). Brain death is defined as the permanent, irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, while cardiovascular function is artificially maintained.

Philippine Neurological Association (PNA) & Global Diagnostic Criteria

All three cardinal clinical prerequisites must be satisfied:

  1. Irreversible Coma: Total lack of spontaneous movement, cortical awareness, and motor/vocal responses to all visual, auditory, and cutaneous stimuli. (Spinal deep tendon reflexes may persist; transitional posturing must be absent).
  2. Absence of Brainstem Reflexes:
    • Pupils fixed in midposition or dilated ($\ge 3\text{ mm}$), unresponsive to light.
    • Absent corneal, gag, cough, pharyngeal, and sucking reflexes.
    • Absent oculocephalic (Doll's eyes) and oculovestibular (caloric) reflexes.
  3. Absence of Spontaneous Respiration (Positive Apnea Test): Total failure of medullary respiratory centers to stimulate inspiration under high arterial $PaCO_2$ challenge.
⭐
MUST KNOW: Mandatory Exclusions & Rules
• Exclude: Hypothermia (core temp $< 36^\circ\text{C}$ / $96.8^\circ\text{F}$), Drug/Toxic intoxication, Neuromuscular blocking agents, Shock/Hypotension (MAP $< 70$ or SBP $< 100\text{ mmHg}$), Severe metabolic derangements.
• Declaration must be made by TWO separate physicians, neither of whom is part of the organ transplant team.
• Exercise extreme caution in anencephalic infants and children $< 5$ years of age.

Apnea Testing Protocol (Step-by-Step)

1. Prerequisites Met (Temp $\ge 36^\circ\text{C}$, SBP $\ge 100$, Euvolemic, No Sedatives)
⬇️
2. Pre-oxygenate with 100% $O_2$ for 10 minutes
⬇️
3. Obtain Baseline ABG (Check baseline $PaCO_2$)
⬇️
4. Disconnect Ventilator & Deliver $O_2$ via Insufflation Catheter (6–8 L/min at level of carina)
⬇️
5. Observe closely for 8–10 minutes for any chest/abdominal respiratory effort
⬇️
6. Repeat ABG after 8–10 minutes & Reconnect Ventilator
🧠
EXAM TIP: Positive vs. Aborted Apnea Test
• POSITIVE TEST (Supports Brain Death): $0$ respiratory movements AND final $PaCO_2 \ge 60\text{ mmHg}$ (OR an increase of $\ge 20\text{ mmHg}$ above baseline).
• ABORT / INCONCLUSIVE TEST: If patient develops hypotension (SBP $< 90$), severe hypoxemia ($SpO_2 < 85\%$), cardiac arrhythmia, OR makes ANY breathing effort $\rightarrow$ Immediately stop test, reconnect ventilator, and perform ancillary testing (EEG, cerebral angiography, or TCD).

Electroencephalogram (EEG) Criteria

Provides confirmation of cerebral death by demonstrating Electrocerebral Silence (Isoelectric EEG): Absence of electrical potential exceeding $2\,\mu\text{V}$ during a minimum $30\text{-minute}$ continuous recording (normal EEG voltage background is $\sim 7\,\mu\text{V}$).

3. Neuroanatomy & Physiology of Alertness vs. Coma

Alertness depends on the continuous structural and functional integrity of the Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) and its reciprocal projections to both cerebral hemispheres.

The Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) Pathway

• Anatomic Location: Paramedian tegmentum of the upper (rostral) pons, midbrain, and caudal diencephalon.
• Thalamic Relay Nuclei: Centromedian, intralaminar, parafascicular, and posterior paramedian thalamic nuclei.
• Projections: Receives collateral sensory inputs (spinothalamic, trigeminothalamic) and projects diffusely to all regions of the cerebral cortex. The cortex sends reciprocal corticofugal fibers back to the reticular formation.

⭐
MUST KNOW: Two Anatomic Mechanisms Producing Coma
Coma occurs ONLY through one of two mechanisms:
1. Direct Structural Lesion of the Upper Brainstem / Diencephalon: Direct damage to the RAS neurons in the upper pons or midbrain tegmentum.
2. Widespread Bilateral Cortical / White Matter Damage: Diffuse destruction or metabolic suppression of BOTH cerebral hemispheres simultaneously (a unilateral hemispheric lesion DOES NOT cause coma unless it exerts mass effect compressing the brainstem RAS!).

Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF) & Ischemic Thresholds

Normal CBF = $55\text{ mL/100g/min}$. Brain oxygen consumption = $3.3\text{ mg/100g/min}$.

CBF Level (mL/100g/min) Physiologic & Clinical Manifestation
55 Normal baseline cerebral perfusion and alertness.
25 EEG wave slowing, syncope, or impaired consciousness.
12 – 15 Electrocerebral silence, coma, total loss of synaptic function.
< 8 – 10 Irreversible neuronal death and structural tissue necrosis.

Structural vs. Metabolic / Toxic Encephalopathies

Feature Structural Coma (Mass / Stroke) Metabolic / Toxic Coma
Onset Sudden or rapidly progressive Variable, usually insidious/gradual
Focal Signs Prominent, asymmetric (hemiparesis, asymmetric reflexes) Usually ABSENT (symmetric presentation)
Pupils Asymmetric, fixed, unreactive Small, equal, REACTIVE to light (Equal/preserved)
Brainstem Signs Asymmetric loss of reflexes early Relatively preserved early
CT / MRI Focal lesion, mass effect, stroke, hemorrhage Normal or diffuse cerebral swelling/atrophy
EEG Focal slowing or unilateral suppressions Diffuse slowing (delta/theta), triphasic waves

4. Herniation Syndromes & Kernohan-Woltman Phenomenon

Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP) forces brain tissue across rigid dural partitions (falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli) or bone openings (foramen magnum), leading to catastrophic brainstem compression.

The Four Major Brain Herniation Types

Type Herniated Brain Structure Compressed Structures Clinical Manifestations
1. Subfalcine (Transfalcine) Cingulate Gyrus beneath Falx Cerebri Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA) ACA territorial infarct $\rightarrow$ Contralateral leg weakness.
2. Transtentorial (Uncal) Uncus / Medial Temporal Lobe through Tentorial Notch Ipsilateral CN III, Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA), Midbrain • Ipsilateral dilated fixed pupil ("blown pupil")
• Homonymous hemianopia (PCA infarct)
• Coma & contralateral hemiparesis.
3. Central Syndrome Diencephalon / Temporal lobes downward through Tentorial Notch Midbrain $\rightarrow$ Pons $\rightarrow$ Medulla (Rostral-caudal deterioration) Progressive deterioration: Cheyne-Stokes $\rightarrow$ CNH $\rightarrow$ Decorticate $\rightarrow$ Decerebrate posturing $\rightarrow$ Fixed midposition pupils.
4. Tonsillar Herniation Cerebellar Tonsils downward through Foramen Magnum Dorsomedial Medulla Oblongata Rapidly fatal! Ataxic breathing, sudden respiratory arrest, circulatory collapse.
⭐
MUST KNOW: Kernohan-Woltman Phenomenon (Kernohan Notch)
• Mechanism: In massive uncal herniation, the displacement of the midbrain pushes the opposite cerebral peduncle against the hard free edge of the tentorium cerebelli on the contralateral side (creating the "Kernohan notch").
• Clinical Result: Causes motor weakness (hemiparesis/hemiplegia) and Babinski sign IPSILATERAL to the primary hemispheric mass/lesion!
• Significance: A classic false localizing sign. If a patient with a right epidural hematoma presents with right-sided weakness, think Kernohan notch!

Mechanistic Logic of Kernohan Notch:

Right Mass Lesion $\rightarrow$ Pushes Midbrain to the Left $\rightarrow$ Left Cerebral Peduncle crushed against Left Tentorial Edge $\rightarrow$ Impairs Left Corticospinal Tract $\rightarrow$ RIGHT-Sided Weakness (Ipsilateral to Lesion!)

5. Clinical Approach & Systematic Neurological Examination

Coma is a critical neurological emergency. Management begins with immediate resuscitation (ABCs) performed simultaneously with rapid diagnostic localization.

Initial Resuscitative Approach (ABCs)

  • Airway: Assess patency, pooling secretions. If GCS $\le 8$ or respiratory fatigue $\rightarrow$ Endotracheal intubation. Protect C-spine if trauma suspected.
  • Breathing: Monitor $SpO_2$, ABG, respiratory pattern. Ensure $SpO_2 > 90\%$.
  • Circulation: Maintain MAP $> 70\text{ mmHg}$ (SBP $> 100$). Secure IV access.
  • Reversible Cocktail / Empiric Bedside: Check fingerstick capillary blood glucose. If $< 70\text{ mg/dL}$ ($< 45\text{ mg/dL}$ in severe hypoglycemia), give 50 mL of 50% Dextrose (D50W) IV + Thiamine 100 mg IV (to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy). Administer Naloxone for suspected opioid tox.

Key Neurological Examination Components

1. Pupillary Light Reflexes (Localization)

  • Unilateral Dilated Fixed Pupil ("Blown Pupil"): Transtentorial Uncal Herniation compressing ipsilateral CN III.
  • Midposition Fixed Pupils (4-6 mm): Midbrain lesion / Midbrain RAS damage.
  • Bilaterally Pinpoint Reactive Pupils: Pontine lesion (destroys sympathetic fibers, sparing parasympathetics).
  • Small Equal Reactive Pupils: Metabolic Encephalopathy or Toxic states.
  • Pinpoint Unreactive Pupils: Opiate overdose (or severe pontine destruction).

2. Ocular Movements & Reflexes

• Oculocephalic Reflex (Doll's Eyes): Rotate head horizontally/vertically (ONLY after clearing C-spine). Intact pons/PPRF $\rightarrow$ Eyes move in direction opposite to head movement. Lost in pontine lesions.
• Oculovestibular Reflex (Caloric Test - COWS): Elevate head $30^\circ$. Instill ice cold water into external auditory meatus.
  - Conscious Patient: Cold water produces tonic gaze toward cold ear with fast nystagmus to the OPPOSITE side (COWS: Cold Opposite, Warm Same).
  - Comatose Patient (Intact Brainstem): Fast nystagmus lost; observe continuous tonic deviation TOWARD the cold water ear.

3. Motor Posturing (Decorticate vs. Decerebrate)

Feature Decorticate Posturing Decerebrate Posturing
Pattern Arms flexed/adducted, wrists flexed, legs extended Arms extended/internally rotated, legs extended, feet plantarflexed
Anatomic Level Lesion ABOVE Vestibular Nuclei (Rostral midbrain / diencephalon) Lesion BELOW Vestibular Nuclei (Midbrain-pons / caudal diencephalon)
Prognosis Serious, but better than decerebrate Grave prognostic sign (profound brainstem failure)

4. Abnormal Respiratory Patterns

  • Cheyne-Stokes Respiration: Cyclic waxing-and-waning hyperpnea alternating with apnea. Locates to bilateral hemispheric or diencephalic lesions (also seen in metabolic coma & CHF).
  • Central Neurogenic Hyperventilation (CNH): Persistent, rapid, deep breathing causing advanced primary respiratory alkalosis. Locates to lower midbrain to upper pontine tegmentum.
  • Apneustic Breathing: Prolonged inspiratory pause (2-3 sec) after full inspiration. Locates to low pontine lesions (classic in basilar artery occlusion).
  • Ataxic (Biot's) Breathing: Completely chaotic, irregular rhythm and depth. Locates to medullary lesions. Quickly progresses to respiratory arrest.

5. Scoring Systems: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) vs. FOUR Scale

• GCS (3–15): Eye Opening (1-4), Verbal Response (1-5), Motor Response (1-6). Mild (13-15), Moderate (9-12), Severe ($\le 8$). Note: An arbitrary sum (e.g. GCS 8) is insufficient; individual subscores must be documented.
• FOUR Scale (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness, 0–16): Evaluates Eye (0-4), Motor (0-4), Brainstem Reflexes (0-4), and Respiration (0-4). Superior for intubated patients.

6. Comprehensive Clinical Case Study: Uremic Encephalopathy

Detailed analysis of the 60-year-old female patient presented in the lecture transcripts.

Patient Profile & Clinical Summary

Demographics: 60-year-old female, right-handed, Filipino, presenting with decreased sensorium.
HPI: 1 month history of progressive dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, oliguria, ascending bipedal edema, and post-prandial vomiting.
Past Medical History: 10-year history of poorly controlled Diabetes Mellitus Type 2 (on Metformin 500mg BID) and Hypertension (usual BP 160-180/100 mmHg on Metoprolol 50mg OD).

Physical & Neurological Examination Findings

  • Vitals: BP 130/70 mmHg, HR 92 bpm, RR 28 cpm (tachypneic), Temp 36.8°C, $SpO_2$ 99%.
  • General PE: Pale conjunctiva, dry skin, bibasal lung crackles, grade III bipedal edema, hyperpigmented leg macules. No focal cranial trauma. Distended JVP. Uriniferous breath odor.
  • Mental Status: Stuporous. No eye opening to sternal rub (E1), no verbal output (V1). Spontaneous, purposeful movements of all 4 extremities (M5). GCS = 7.
  • Cranial Nerves: Pupils 2-3 mm, equal, briskly reactive to light. EOMs full on Doll's eye maneuver (oculocephalic intact). Corneal reflexes intact bilaterally. No facial asymmetry.
  • Motor/Reflexes: Normotonic, motor strength $3/5$ throughout. Localizes to pain in upper extremities; grimaces to pain. DTRs 2+ upper, 1+ patellar, 0 Achilles. Babinski absent bilaterally. Supple neck (no meningismus).

Anatomic Localization & Clinical Reasoning

⭐
LOCALIZATION OF LESION: DIFFUSE CEREBRAL CORTEX
• Reasoning: The patient has stupor (altered sensorium) BUT has intact brainstem reflexes (pupils reactive, Doll's eye full) and preserved spontaneous/purposeful movements in all extremities without focal motor deficits or cranial nerve palsies. The ARAS in the brainstem is structurally intact. Sensorium impairment stems from global, bilateral cortical suppression driven by severe uremic toxins, metabolic acidosis, and electrolyte shifts.
• Neuroimaging Signature: MRI may demonstrate the "Lentiform Fork Sign"—bilateral symmetric basal ganglia hyperintensities bounded by a hyperintense rim, representing vasogenic/cytotoxic edema from uremic toxins.

Primary Diagnosis & Management Plan

Primary Diagnosis: Uremic Encephalopathy secondary to End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD Stage V) due to Diabetic Nephropathy and Hypertensive Nephrosclerosis; Severe Hyperkalemia; Severe Metabolic Acidosis; Anemia of CKD; Congestive Heart Failure.
Management: Emergency hemodialysis, mechanical ventilation, IV calcium gluconate/insulin-glucose for hyperkalemia stabilization, strict fluid restriction, and supportive care.

⚡ Rapid Rules for Board Examinations

  • GCS $\le 8$: Intubate immediately to protect the airway!
  • Emergency Coma Cocktail: D50W (50 mL) + Thiamine (100 mg) + Naloxone if indicated. Always give Thiamine BEFORE Dextrose in alcoholics to prevent precipitation of Wernicke's encephalopathy!
  • Unilateral Mass: Unilateral hemispheric lesions DO NOT cause coma unless there is mass effect compressing the brainstem ARAS or secondary herniation.
  • COWS Mnemonic: Cold Opposite, Warm Same (Refers to fast-phase nystagmus direction in conscious patients). In comatose patients with intact brainstem, cold water produces tonic deviation TOWARD the cold ear.
