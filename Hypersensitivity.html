Overview & Core Concepts

Primary source: UERM Medicine II Physical Diagnosis Lecture by Dr. Hyacinth Claire Tanseco-Chacon

🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Identify and differentiate Gell & Coombs Gell & Coombs Types I, II, III, and IV hypersensitivity reactions.
  • Understand the cellular, cytokine, and molecular immunologic mechanisms underlying immediate vs. delayed responses.
  • Classify Adverse Drug Reactions into Type A (Predictable) and Type B (Unpredictable/Immunologic).
  • Recognize severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs): SJS, TEN, DIHS/DRESS, AGEP, and Fixed Drug Eruptions.
  • Evaluate Primary Inborn Errors of Immunity (Phagocytic, Complement, T-cell, B-cell, Regulatory) vs Secondary Immunodeficiencies.
  • Master the diagnostic criteria, acute 10-step WAO resuscitation protocol for Anaphylaxis, and Systemic Mastocytosis.
🔑 Core Definitions
  • Hypersensitivity Reactions: Injurious or pathologic immune responses occurring upon exposure to stimuli tolerated by normal individuals.
  • Allergy: Hypersensitivity reaction initiated by specific immunologic mechanisms (antibody- or cell-mediated). Must be reproducible and specific.
  • Allergen: Immunogenic foreign antigen capable of eliciting an immediate hypersensitivity reaction.
  • Atopy: Personal or familial genetic tendency to produce IgE antibodies in response to low doses of environmental allergens, predisposing to asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and urticaria.
⭐ MUST KNOW: Immunopathology Core Principles

The immune system serves three main physiological functions: Defense, Homeostasis, and Surveillance. Dysregulation leads to disease states:

  • Exaggerated Defense → Allergy & Hypersensitivity | Diminished Defense → Immunodeficiency
  • Exaggerated Homeostasis → Autoimmunity
  • Diminished Surveillance → Malignancy

Gell & Coombs Hypersensitivity Mechanisms

Immunologic cellular cascades and target pathology

Type I: Immediate Hypersensitivity

Hallmark: Production of allergen-specific IgE antibody dependent on activation of IL-4-producing T-helper 2 (Th2) and follicular helper T-cells (Tfh).

CELLULAR CASCADE OF TYPE I SENSITIZATION & RE-EXPOSURE:
Allergen Exposure → Dendritic Cell (APC) captures & presents to Naive CD4+ T-cell
↓
CD4+ T-cell differentiates to Th2 / Tfh & releases IL-4, IL-13
↓
B-cells undergo IgE Heavy Chain Class Switching & produce allergen-specific IgE
↓
SENSITIZATION PHASE: IgE enters blood & binds high-affinity FcεRI receptors on Mast Cells & Basophils
↓
RE-EXPOSURE / IMMEDIATE PHASE: Multivalent allergen binds & cross-links FcεRI-bound IgE
↓
Mast Cell Degranulation: Biogenic Amines (Histamine), Lipid Mediators (PGD2, LTC4/D4/E4, PAF), Late-phase Cytokines (TNF, IL-1, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6)
💡 Clinical Pearl: Mediators & Clinical Effects
  • Histamine & PGD2: Rapid vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, smooth muscle contraction (bronchospasm).
  • Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4): 1000x more potent than histamine in causing prolonged bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion.
  • Late-Phase Reaction (4-8 hours post-exposure): Cytokine-driven recruitment of eosinophils and neutrophils, causing tissue inflammation and bronchial hyperreactivity.
Type II: Antibody-Mediated Disease

Caused by IgG or IgM antibodies binding directly to specific fixed cell-surface or tissue extracellular matrix antigens.

Mechanisms of Tissue Injury:
  1. Opsonization & Phagocytosis / Cell Death: Complement activation (C3b) or Fc-receptor binding on macrophages/NK cells causing clearance (e.g., Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia).
  2. Complement & Fc Receptor-Mediated Inflammation: Antibody deposition recruits neutrophils/monocytes, releasing ROS and lysosomal enzymes (e.g., Goodpasture Syndrome).
  3. Cellular Neutralization (Antagonism): Autoantibody blocks receptor without causing destruction (e.g., Myasthenia Gravis: anti-AChR antibody blocks neuromuscular junction).
  4. Cellular Stimulation (Agonism): Autoantibody stimulates receptor continuously (e.g., Grave's Disease: anti-TSH receptor antibody stimulates thyroid hormone synthesis).
Disease Target Antigen Mechanism Clinical Manifestations
Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA) RBC membrane proteins (Rh antigens) Opsonization & Phagocytosis Hemolysis, anemia, jaundice, splenomegaly
Goodpasture Syndrome Non-collagenous domain of α3 chain of Type IV Collagen in BM Complement & Fc receptor-mediated inflammation Glomerulonephritis (nephritic syndrome), pulmonary alveolar hemorrhage
Grave's Disease TSH Receptor Cellular stimulation (Agonist antibody) Hyperthyroidism, exophthalmos, pretibial myxedema
Myasthenia Gravis Muscle Acetylcholine Receptor (AChR) Cellular neutralization (Antagonist/blocking) Fluctuating muscle weakness, ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia
Rheumatic Fever Streptococcal cell wall antigen (Cross-reacts with myocardium) Antibody cross-reactivity & inflammation Myocarditis, polyarthritis, Sydenham chorea, erythema marginatum
Type III: Immune Complex-Mediated Disease

Disease is caused by circulating Ag-Ab (IgG/IgM) complexes formed in antigen excess. Soluble complexes fail to be cleared by phagocytes and deposit in high-pressure vascular/filtration beds (renal glomeruli, synovia, small vessel walls).

Pathophysiology: Deposited complexes activate complement (C3a, C5a anaphylatoxins) → neutrophil recruitment & release of lysosomal enzymes/ROS → vasculitis, tissue damage, necrosis.

Disease Target / Complex Antigens Clinical Manifestations
Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) Native DNA, nucleoproteins, nuclear matrix Nephritis, arthritis, vasculitis, butterfly malar rash
Serum Sickness Heterologous proteins / Drug haptens Systemic vasculitis, fever, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, urticaria
Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis Streptococcal cell wall / exoprotein antigens Nephritic syndrome (hematuria, hypertension, oliguria, periorbital edema)
Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) Hepatitis B/C viral antigens, microbial proteins Necrotizing vasculitis of medium vessels, hypertension, abdominal pain
Type IV: Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity (DTH / Cell-Mediated)

Mediated by antigen-specific T-lymphocytes (CD4+ or CD8+). Named delayed because clinical lesions require 24 to 48 hours to manifest post-exposure (time required for T-cell recruitment and cytokine release).

🧠 Exam Tip: Gell & Coombs Type IV Subclassifications (Lecture Appendix)
  • Type IVa (Th1 Driven): Mediated by Th1 cells, IFN-γ, TNF-α. Activates Macrophages. Example: Tuberculin (PPD) test, Contact Dermatitis.
  • Type IVb (Th2 Driven): Mediated by Th2 cells, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13. Recruits Eosinophils. Example: DRESS / DIHS.
  • Type IVc (CTL Mediated): Mediated by CD8+ Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes, Perforin/Granzyme B. Causes direct epithelial cell killing. Example: SJS / TEN, Fixed Drug Eruption.
  • Type IVd (T-cell Neutrophilic): Mediated by T-cells releasing CXCL8 & GM-CSF. Recruits Neutrophils. Example: AGEP.

Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs) & Cutaneous Syndromes

WHO Definition: Any noxious, unintended, and undesired effect of a drug at doses used for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy.

Type A vs. Type B Classification
Feature Type A (Predictable) Type B (Unpredictable)
Frequency ~80% of all ADRs (Very Common) ~10-15% of ADRs (Uncommon)
Dose Dependency Dose-dependent Not directly dose-dependent
Pharmacology Related to known drug pharmacology Unrelated to known drug pharmacology
Target Host Occurs in normal individuals Occurs only in genetically susceptible hosts
Examples • Overdosage (Acetaminophen toxicity)
• Side effects (Sedation from Antihistamines)
• Secondary effects (C. difficile colitis after antibiotics)
• Drug-Drug interactions (Warfarin clearance reduction) 		• Drug Intolerance (Tinnitus with low-dose Salicylates)
• Idiosyncratic (Hemolytic anemia in G6PD given Primaquine)
• Drug Allergy (Immunologic hypersensitivity)
Core Cutaneous ADR Syndromes
1. Maculopapular Rash / Morbilliform Eruption (Most Common) ▼

Clinical Features: Symmetric, blanching erythematous macules and papules. Starts on trunk/intertriginous areas, spreads peripherally. Rarely involves face (facial edema = think DIHS!). Mild-moderate pruritus and low-grade fever.

Timing: Begins within 1 week of initiation; lasts < 2 weeks. May progress for a few days even after stopping drug.

Offending Drugs: Aminopenicillins, Cephalosporins, Sulfonamides, Allopurinol, Antiepileptics, Nevirapine, Lamotrigine.

Management: Discontinue drug. Symptomatic treatment with oral H1-antihistamines and topical steroids. Systemic steroids rarely needed.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Differentiate from viral exanthems! A trunkal rash during Ampicillin therapy for Infectious Mononucleosis (EBV) or Streptococcal pharyngitis (sandpaper rash) is disease-related, not a true lifelong penicillin allergy.

2. Urticaria, Angioedema & Anaphylaxis ▼

Urticaria: Pruritic red wheals with central blanching lasting < 24 hours per individual lesion.

Angioedema: Deep dermal/subcutaneous edema in thin skin (lips, periorbital, tongue, genitalia).

3 Mechanisms:

  • IgE-Dependent (Type I): Minutes to 36h post-exposure. (Penicillin, NSAIDs, Blood products).
  • Immune Complex (Type III): 6-12 days onset. Associated with serum sickness (fever, arthralgia, hematuria).
  • Non-Immunologic (Direct Mast Cell Release): Opiates, Taxanes, Radiocontrast Media (RCM), ACE-Inhibitors. High osmolality RCM has 5x higher risk vs. low osmolality RCM. Pre-treat RCM with Prednisone + Antihistamines.
3. Fixed Drug Eruption (FDE) ▼

Clinical Features: Sharply demarcated, round, dull red-to-brown macules with dusky violaceous center or central bulla. Burning/stinging sensation. Leaves post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

Pathognomonic Hallmark: Reappears in the EXACT SAME fixed anatomic location upon drug re-challenge!

Common Sites: Lips, glans penis, genitalia, hands, legs, oral mucosa.

Offending Drugs: Sulfonamides, Tetracyclines, NSAIDs, Barbiturates, Pseudoephedrine, Phenolphthalein.

4. Drug-Induced Hypersensitivity Syndrome (DIHS / DRESS) ▼

Clinical Features: Triad of Fever & flu-like prodrome + Diffuse morbilliform rash with Facial Edema + Multi-organ dysfunction. Systemic involvement: Lymphadenopathy, leukocytosis with Eosinophilia or atypical lymphocytes.

Onset: Delayed! Begins 2 to 8 weeks after drug initiation and persists for weeks after drug cessation.

Organ Failure Patterns:

  • Liver: Most common organ involved. Hepatitis is the leading cause of the 10% mortality!
  • Renal: Prominent in Allopurinol-induced DIHS.
  • Cardiac/Lung: Prominent in Minocycline-induced DIHS.
  • Gastrointestinal: Prominent in Abacavir-induced DIHS.

Pathogenesis: Reactive drug metabolites + viral reactivation (HHV-6, HHV-7, EBV, CMV) driving CD8+ T-cell expansion.

Management: Withdraw drug immediately. Systemic Prednisone (1.5-2.0 mg/kg/day) tapered SLOWLY over 8 to 12 weeks! Rapid tapering causes fatal disease rebound. Monitor thyroid panel up to 6 months post-recovery (risk of late Autoimmune Thyroiditis/Diabetes).

5. Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) & Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) ▼

Pathophysiology: Type IVc cytotoxic T-cell reaction causing full-thickness epidermal necrosis and mucosal detachment in the absence of dermal inflammation.

Clinical Presentation: High fever (>39°C), severe painful dusky atypical targetoid macules, mucosal detachment (lips, mouth, eyes, genital erosions), Positive Nikolsky sign.

Classification by Body Surface Area (TBSA) Detachment:

  • SJS: < 10% TBSA epidermal detachment. (Mortality ~10%)
  • SJS-TEN Overlap: 10% to 30% TBSA involvement.
  • TEN: > 30% TBSA epidermal detachment. (Mortality ~30%)

Offending Drugs: Sulfonamides, Allopurinol, Aromatic Anticonvulsants (Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Phenobarbital), Lamotrigine, Oxicam NSAIDs, Nevirapine.

⚠️ Differential Diagnosis: SJS/TEN vs. Erythema Multiforme (EM)

Erythema Multiforme: Associated with Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) or Mycoplasma. Features typical acral target lesions (3 concentric rings) with NO widespread epidermal detachment.

6. Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis (AGEP) ▼

Clinical Features: Sudden onset of high fever, leukocytosis with marked neutrophilia, erythroderma followed by dozens to hundreds of non-follicular pinpoint sterile pustules localized in body folds (flexural/intertriginous areas).

Timing: Rapid! Begins 24-48 hours to 1-2 weeks post-exposure.

Histology: Superficial subcorneal neutrophilic pustules vs. SJS full-thickness epithelial necrosis.

Offending Drugs: β-lactam antibiotics, Macrolides, Calcium Channel Blockers (Diltiazem). Patch testing reproduces pustular eruption.

Inborn Errors of Immunity & Secondary Immunodeficiencies

Primary (Genetic single-gene defects) vs Secondary (Acquired environmental/disease causes)

⭐ 10 Warning Signs of Primary Immunodeficiency in Adults
  1. 2+ new ear infections within 1 year
  2. 2+ new sinus infections/yr (no allergy)
  3. 1 pneumonia per year for > 1 year
  4. Chronic diarrhea with weight loss
  5. Recurrent viral infections (colds, herpes)
  6. Recurrent need for IV antibiotics
  7. Recurrent deep skin/organ abscesses
  8. Persistent oral thrush or fungal skin infection
  9. Infection with harmless TB-like mycobacteria
  10. Family history of Primary Immunodeficiency

Secondary Immunodeficiencies (Acquired)

Far more common than primary immunodeficiencies in adult clinical practice.

  • Malnutrition: The most common cause of immunodeficiency worldwide. Protein-calorie malnutrition impairs T-cell production, cell-mediated immunity, and mucosal barriers.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis, T-cell anergy, and poor lymphoproliferation due to hyperglycemia and microvascular insufficiency.
  • Immunosuppressive Drugs:
    • Corticosteroids: Alters inflammatory gene expression, impairs cytokine production, chemotaxis, and cellular adhesion. Broad susceptibility.
    • Calcineurin Inhibitors (Cyclosporine, Tacrolimus): Selectively inhibits calcineurin → blocks transcription of IL-2 gene required for T-cell activation. Spares neutrophils/macrophages (narrower infection spectrum).
  • HIV / AIDS: Tropism for CD4+ T-cells and macrophages. Structural genes: GAG (p24 capsid, matrix), POL (reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease), ENV (gp120 attachment, gp41 fusion).
    • CD4 < 500 cells/μL: Initial clinical symptoms manifest (thrush, zoster, oral hairy leukoplakia).
    • CD4 < 200 cells/μL: Diagnostic threshold for AIDS; high susceptibility to opportunistic infections (PCP, Kaposi sarcoma, Toxoplasmosis, Cryptococcus).

Anaphylaxis, Systemic Mastocytosis & Inducible Urticarias

Emergency diagnosis and management protocols based on WAO Guidelines

Anaphylaxis: Clinical Criteria & Management

An acute, sudden-onset, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction.

⭐ WAO Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Anaphylaxis (Highly Likely if ANY 1 of 3 is met):
  1. Acute onset (minutes to hours) involving skin/mucosa (hives, itch, flushing, swollen lips/tongue) AND AT LEAST ONE OF:
    • a) Sudden Respiratory compromise (dyspnea, wheeze, stridor, hypoxemia)
    • b) Sudden Reduced BP or end-organ collapse (syncope, hypotonia, incontinence)
  2. TWO OR MORE of the following occurring suddenly after exposure to a likely allergen:
    • a) Sudden skin/mucosal symptoms
    • b) Sudden respiratory compromise
    • c) Sudden reduced BP / end-organ dysfunction
    • d) Sudden crampy gastrointestinal symptoms (severe abdominal pain, vomiting)
  3. Reduced blood pressure after exposure to a known allergen for that patient (Adults: Systolic BP < 90 mmHg or > 30% drop from baseline).
📋 WAO 10-Step Initial Management Protocol
Step Intervention / Action Clinical Specification & Dosage
Step 1 Emergency Protocol Have a written emergency protocol for anaphylaxis recognition and rehearse regularly.
Step 2 Remove Trigger Discontinue offending IV infusion, diagnostic agent, or therapeutic drug immediately.
Step 3 Assess Patient Simultaneously evaluate Circulation, Airway, Breathing, Mental status, Skin, and Body Weight.
Step 4 Call for Help Summon resuscitation team or emergency medical services immediately.
Step 5 FIRST-LINE DRUG: Epinephrine (Adrenaline) Inject Epinephrine 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) INTRAMUSCULARLY into the mid-anterolateral thigh!
• Adult Dose: 0.3 mg to 0.5 mg IM.
• Pediatric Dose: 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.3 mg) IM.
• Repeat every 5 to 15 minutes if symptoms persist. (Most respond to 1-2 doses).
Step 6 Patient Positioning Place patient flat on back with lower extremities elevated. (Fatality can occur within seconds if sitting/standing suddenly).
Step 7 Oxygenation Administer high-flow supplemental oxygen (6-8 L/min) via face mask.
Step 8 IV Access & Fluids Establish wide-bore IV access (14-16G). Infuse rapid 0.9% Normal Saline bolus (5-10 mL/kg in 5-10 mins).
Step 9 CPR Perform continuous CPR if cardiac arrest or circulatory collapse ensues.
Step 10 Monitoring Continuously monitor blood pressure, pulse oximetry, cardiac rhythm, and respiratory status.
⚠️ Absolute Contraindications / Risk Factors in Anaphylaxis

Beta-Blockers and ACE-Inhibitors are relative contraindications in high-risk allergic patients. Beta-blockers block epinephrine receptors, making anaphylaxis refractory to epinephrine treatment. ACE-inhibitors prevent bradykinin degradation, worsening angioedema.

Inducible Urticarias & Hereditary Angioedema (HAE)

Physical / Inducible Urticarias:

  • Dermatographism: Linear wheal appearing at the site of a stroke or scratch.
  • Pressure Urticaria: Delayed wheals triggered by sustained pressure (belts, shoulder straps, running).
  • Cholinergic Urticaria: Unique 1-2 mm small papular wheals with large surrounding erythema, triggered by heat, exercise, hot showers, or sweating.
  • Cold Urticaria: Triggered by cold exposure. High risk of fatal vascular collapse during swimming in cold water!

Hereditary Angioedema (HAE):

  • Autosomal dominant deficiency (Type 1, 85%) or dysfunction (Type 2, 15%) of C1-Inhibitor (C1-INH).
  • Key Clinical Feature: Recurrent angioedema WITHOUT urticaria and WITHOUT pruritus!
  • Prominent severe recurrent gastrointestinal colic attacks and life-threatening laryngeal edema.
  • Labs: Low C4, low C1-INH antigen/function, normal C1q.
Systemic Mastocytosis

Clonal expansion of abnormal mast cells driven by c-KIT D816V mutation.

Cutaneous Manifestations:

  • Urticaria Pigmentosa: Tan-brown macules/papules exhibiting Darier's Sign (rubbing or stroking the lesion induces localized wheal and erythema).
  • Telangiectasia Macularis Eruptive Perstans (TMEP): Tan-brown macules with striking telangiectasia (~1% of ISM).
🧠 Diagnostic Criteria for Systemic Mastocytosis (Requires 1 Major + 1 Minor OR 3 Minor)
  • MAJOR CRITERION: Multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥ 15 mast cells in aggregates) in bone marrow or extracutaneous biopsy.
  • MINOR CRITERIA:
    1. Spindle-shaped or atypical mast cell morphology.
    2. Expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells (c-KIT / CD117 positive).
    3. Detection of c-KIT codon 816 mutation in marrow/peripheral blood.
    4. Total serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL.

If you see: Non-malignant lymphadenopathy + Double-negative T-cells ($CD3^+ CD4^- CD8^-$)
Think: Autoimmune Lymphoproliferative Syndrome (ALPS)
If you see: Rash 2-8 weeks post-drug + facial edema + eosinophilia + hepatitis
Think: DIHS / DRESS Syndrome
If you see: Full-thickness skin detachment <10% TBSA + painful mucosal erosion
Think: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS)
If you see: Full-thickness skin detachment >30% TBSA + Nikolsky sign
Think: Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)
If you see: Dozens of non-follicular pinpoint pustules on flexural erythema 24-48h post-drug
Think: Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis (AGEP)
If you see: Lesion recurring in the exact same anatomic site upon drug re-challenge
Think: Fixed Drug Eruption (FDE)
If you see: Recurrent angioedema WITHOUT urticaria and WITHOUT pruritus
Think: Hereditary Angioedema (C1-INH deficiency)
If you see: Tan-brown macules that wheal upon stroking (Darier's Sign)
Think: Urticaria Pigmentosa (Systemic Mastocytosis)
If you see: Pinpoint 1-2mm papular wheals triggered by heat, hot shower, or sweating
Think: Cholinergic Urticaria
💡 Must-Remember Medical Mnemonics
  • W.A.T.E.R. (Wiskott-Aldrich): Wiskott-Aldrich, Thrombocytopenia (microplatelets), Eczema, Recurrent infections.
  • ACID (Gell & Coombs Types I-IV):
    Type I = Anaphylactic / Allergic (IgE)
    Type II = Cytotoxic / Antibody (IgG/M)
    Type III = Immune Complex (Ag-Ab)
    Type IV = Delayed-type (T-cell)
  • Catalase Positive Pathogens in CGD (SPACE): Saphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas, Aspergillus, Candida, Enterobacteriaceae (Serratia/Burkholderia).
