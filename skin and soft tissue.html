  • Differentiate inflammatory skin conditions (Hidradenitis Suppurativa, Pyoderma Gangrenosum, SJS/TEN).

Skin Weight: Comprises 15% of total body weight.

Epidermal Renewal: 30-day keratinocyte life cycle; completely avascular (diffusion-dependent).

Pressure Necrosis Threshold: Microvascular capillary pressure = 30 mmHg (pressures above this cause ischemia).

Burn Mortality: Caustic injuries account for 10.7% of burns but 30% of all burn deaths.

01. Review of Skin Anatomy, Histology & Physiology

Complete breakdown of the Epidermis, Dermis, Hypodermis, Cellular Components, and Appendages.

Cross-Sectional Schematic of Cutaneous Layers & Vascular Plexuses
1. Epidermis (5 Layers) Avascular

Obtains all nutrients via diffusion from dermal vessels. Contains 5 distinct strata (deep to superficial):

  1. Stratum Basale (Germinativum): Deepest layer; cuboidal/columnar cells bound by desmosomes. High mitotic index, large basophilic nuclei. Continuous epidermal replication. Origin of melanocytes.
  2. Stratum Spinosum ("Spiny Layer"): Characteristic spinous appearance due to desmosomal attachments. Active keratin production. Langerhans cells interdigitate here.
  3. Stratum Granulosum: Contains lamellar granules packed with lipids and glycolipids that undergo exocytosis to form an impermeable lipid layer.
  4. Stratum Lucidum: Flat, translucent, dead keratinocytes. Present ONLY in thick skin (palms and soles).
  5. Stratum Corneum: Outermost thinnest layer of anucleate keratinocytes surrounded by a lipid-rich matrix. Protects against shear, mechanical disruption, and insensible water loss.
🧠
Mnemonic (Superficial to Deep): Come, Let's Get Sun Burned (Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale).
2. Specific Epidermal Cells Histology
  • Keratinocytes: Basic functional building blocks of skin. Undergo 30-day maturation cycle.
  • Melanocytes: Neural crest-derived cells residing in stratum basale (500–2,000/mm²). Produce melanin to shield nuclear DNA from UV damage.
    Staining markers: Express S100 protein, BCL2, and Vimentin.
    Clinical note: Filipinos have higher melanin density than Caucasians, resulting in lower baseline rates of skin cancer.
  • Langerhans Cells: Dendritic immunologic antigen-presenting cells (3–6% of epidermal cells) located in stratum spinosum. Readily impaired by UV radiation.
  • Merkel Cells: Mechanoreceptors for light touch/tactile perception. Aggregated in fingertips, lips, hair follicle bases. Origin of Merkel Cell Carcinoma.
  • Toker Cells: Specialized clear cells present in 10% of nipple epidermis; potential cell of origin for Paget's Disease of the Nipple.
3. Epidermal Appendages Ectodermal

Sweat Glands (Exocrine Tubular): Embryologically derived from ectoderm; bulk sits in dermis.

  • Eccrine Glands: Majority of body surface; essential for thermoregulation. Excrete solids via exocytosis.
  • Apocrine Glands: Axilla, perineum, anus, areola, eyelids, external auditory canal. Excretion involves decapitation of apical cell portion.
  • Apoeccrine Glands: Open directly to skin surface; become functional at puberty.

Pilosebaceous Unit: Multicomponent structure consisting of hair follicle, sebaceous gland, arrector pili muscle, and sensory nerve.
⭐ Crucial Surgical Concept: Lined by germinal epithelium; serves as the primary source of epidermal re-epithelialization following partial-thickness injuries!

4. Dermis & Vascularity Mesodermal

Dermal Collagen: Type I & Type III collagen in 4:1 ratio. Type I is stiffer (skin, bone, tendons); Type III is flexible (skin, vascular walls).

Dermal Layers:

  • Papillary Layer: Interdigitates with epidermal rete ridges; contains loose fibroblasts and high cell density.
  • Reticular Layer: Deep 80-90% of dermis; coarse network of Type I collagen and elastic fibers responsible for skin retractility.

Fibroblasts & Myofibroblasts: Workhorse of wound healing; myofibroblasts drive wound contraction.

Dual Vascular Plexus:

  • Superficial Subpapillary Plexus: Located between papillary and reticular dermis.
  • Deep Dermal Plexus: Located at dermal-hypodermal junction.
💡
Surgical Pearl (Skin Grafting Principles): When performing Split-Thickness Skin Grafting (STSG), removing the epidermis and superficial papillary dermis leaves the deep dermal plexus and pilosebaceous units intact at the donor site. This allows rapid spontaneous re-epithelialization without secondary defect creation.

02. Inflammatory Skin Pathologies

In-depth coverage of Hidradenitis Suppurativa, Pyoderma Gangrenosum, and Epidermal Necrolysis (SJS/TEN).

Hidradenitis Suppurativa Acne Inversa

Definition: Chronic, painful inflammatory disorder of apocrine gland-bearing skin characterized by deep nodules, recurrent abscesses, and subterranean sinus tracts.

Sites: Axilla, perineum, inguinoperineal folds, inframammary regions (Female:Male = 3:2).

Pathophysiology: Inward follicular occlusion, sebaceous gland atrophy, TNF-alpha / IL-12 / IL-23 dysregulation. Exacerbated by obesity, smoking, and diabetes.

Hurley Staging & Management:
  • Stage 1: Solitary/multiple abscesses, NO sinus tracts/scarring.
    → Tx: Topical & systemic Clindamycin.
  • Stage 2: Recurrent abscesses with sinus tracts & scarring.
    → Tx: Antibiotics insufficient alone; radical resection / biologics / reconstruction.
  • Stage 3: Diffuse interconnected sinus tracts across whole region.
    → Tx: Wide radical excision + local flaps / STSG + Biologics (Adalimumab/Infliximab).
Pyoderma Gangrenosum Violaceous Edge

Definition: Non-infectious, destructive inflammatory neutrophilic dermatosis (5th to 7th decades of life).

Clinical Hallmarks: Starts as sterile pustules that rapidly breakdown into painful, expanding ulcerations with raised, purple/violaceous overhanging borders. Most common site: Legs.

Systemic Associations: Strongly tied to Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Crohn's, Ulcerative Colitis), Rheumatoid Arthritis, and Hematologic malignancies.

Management: Primary mainstay is medical: treat underlying systemic condition + high-dose corticosteroids, Cyclosporine, or anti-TNF agents (Infliximab, Etanercept, Ustekinumab). Surgical debridement must be cautious due to pathergy.

Epidermal Necrolysis SJS / TEN

Definition: Severe acute mucocutaneous reaction causing apoptosis and extensive detachment at the dermoepidermal junction. 90% mucosal involvement (oral, ocular, genital).

TBSA Spectrum:

  • SJS: <10% Body Surface Area detachment.
  • SJS/TEN Overlap: 10%–30% TBSA.
  • TEN: >30% TBSA sloughing.

Key Physical Sign: (+) Nikolsky Sign — lateral finger pressure causes sloughing of epidermis from dermis.

Etiology & Drugs: Presents ~8 weeks after exposure to high-risk drugs: Aromatic Anticonvulsants (Carbamazepine, Phenytoin), Sulfonamides (Co-trimoxazole), Allopurinol, NSAIDs, Nevirapine.

Treatment: Immediate withdrawal of offending drug, burn-unit support, non-sulfonamide antimicrobial dressings, maintain euvolemia, early enteral nutrition.

⚠️
Exam Pitfall (Epidermal Necrolysis Therapy): Never administer Sulfonamide antibiotics to a patient suffering from TEN/SJS! If an infectious complication arises in TEN, select non-sulfonamide, non-cross-reacting broad-spectrum antimicrobials. Systemic steroids in TEN remain controversial due to increased sepsis risk.

03. Radiation, Trauma, Caustic, Thermal & Pressure Injuries

Complete physical breakdown of skin trauma and burn wound mechanics.

1. Radiation-Induced Injuries UVA / UVB

UVA (320–400 nm): Represents 95% of UV rays reaching Earth. Longer wavelength → penetrates deep into the reticular dermis (20-30%). Causes photooxidation of pre-existing melanin leading to immediate pigmentation, melasma, actinic lentigines, and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

UVB (290–320 nm): Shorter wavelength → mostly absorbed in epidermis (<10% reaches papillary dermis). Induces neomelanization (synthesis of new melanin) and direct DNA damage (primary sunburn/cancer wave).

Therapeutic Radiation: Used for malignancies, keloids, psoriasis. Causes basal keratinocyte and fibroblast destruction → chronic erythema, radiation fibrosis, non-healing ulcers. Surgical excision must encompass collateral irradiated surrounding tissue!

2. Trauma & Bite Wounds Microbiology

Wound Depth Axiom: Partial-thickness mechanical injuries preserve pilosebaceous units and heal spontaneously if kept clean and moist. Full-thickness injuries obliterate germinal epithelium and demand skin grafting or flap coverage.

Bite Microbiology Clues: Polymicrobial early; single pathogen late.

  • Human Bites: High bacterial load! S. aureus, S. epidermidis, Peptococcus.
  • Cat Bites: Pasteurella multocida (high rate of rapid cellulitis).
  • Dog Bites: Capnocytophaga canimorsus (severe sepsis in splenectomized/immunocompromised).

Prophylaxis: Amoxicillin-clavulanate (1st line), Doxycycline, or Clindamycin + Ciprofloxacin for 3-7 days.

3. Caustic Chemical Injuries Acid vs Alkaline

Chemical burns comprise 10.7% of all burns but cause 30% of burn deaths!

Alkaline (Basic) Solutions: MORE severe than acids! Cause Liquefactive Necrosis — saponifies tissue lipids and dissolves proteins, leaving no protective barrier, resulting in continuous deep tissue melting.

Acidic Solutions: Cause Coagulative Necrosis — forms a hard, thick eschar/coagulum that self-limits deeper penetration.

⭐ Mandatory First-Line Treatment: Immediate copous water/saline irrigation. Acid burns: 30 minutes; Alkaline burns: 2 hours minimum. NEVER NEUTRALIZE (chemical neutralization generates an exothermic reaction that drastically deepens tissue destruction!).

Specific Chemical Antidotes:
  • Hydrofluoric Acid: Neutralize with Calcium Gluconate.
  • Phenol Burns: Wash with Polyethylene Glycol (PEG).
  • Chemotherapy Extravasation: Conservative initial observation until clear tissue demarcation, then debridement + grafting.
4. Thermal Injuries & Zones Jackson's Zones

Full-thickness destruction occurs in <1 second at temperatures >80°C.

  • Zone of Coagulation: Innermost core; irreversible necrotic tissue. Requires surgical debridement.
  • Zone of Stasis: Middle zone of vascular compromise and sluggish perfusion. Tissue is at risk; can recover or progress to necrosis over 3-4 days depending on resuscitation!
  • Zone of Hyperemia: Outermost zone; characterized by intense inflammation. Tissue remains fully viable.
5. Pressure Injuries (Decubitus Ulcers) Capillary Pressure = 30 mmHg

Occur in 22–49% of critically ill ICU patients over bony prominences (Sacrum, Ischium, Coccyx, Malleolus). Normal skin microvascular capillary perfusion pressure = 30 mmHg. Pressures exceeding 30 mmHg lead to microvascular occlusion and tissue ischemia.

Official Staging Criteria:
  • Stage 1: Non-blanchable erythema over intact skin.
  • Stage 2: Partial-thickness skin loss with exposed dermis or intact/ruptured serum blisters.
  • Stage 3: Full-thickness skin loss extending down to (but NOT through) underlying fascia.
  • Stage 4: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss with direct exposure/palpation of bone, tendon, muscle, or joint capsule.
Management & Surgical Rules:
  • ⭐ Reposition patient every 2 hours (q2h); use off-loading mattresses.
  • Nutritional buildup is essential (hypoalbuminemia severely impairs healing).
  • Fever in chronic pressure ulcer patients is most commonly from urinary or pulmonary sources, not the ulcer itself!
  • Stage 3 or 4 ulcers do NOT automatically mandate surgery. Surgical goals: wide debridement, pseudobursa excision, ostectomy of involved bone, and tension-free vascularized flap closure.

04. Cutaneous Bacterial & Viral Pathologies

Uncomplicated vs. Complicated Infections, Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections (NSTIs), Actinomycosis, HPV, and HIV skin surgical lesions.

1. Uncomplicated Infections <75 cm² Area
  • Impetigo: Superficial vesiculopustular infection with classic honey-colored crusting.
  • Erysipelas: Acute lymphatic infection restricted to the upper papillary dermis; raised, sharply demarcated borders.
  • Cellulitis: Infection extending into deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue; ill-defined non-demarcated erythema.
  • Folliculitis: Localized infection of hair follicle.
  • Furuncle (Boil): Painful purulent nodule originating from a hair follicle.
  • Carbuncle: Coalescence of multiple adjacent furuncles presenting with multiple draining sinus tracts.
2. Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections (NSTI) Surgical Emergency

Complicated infections (>75 cm² or deep fascia involvement) carrying high mortality (25–40%). Rapid, fulminant tissue destruction.

Physical Exam Signs:

  • Crepitus: Indicates gas-forming anaerobes/Gram-negative bacilli.
  • Fluctuance: Denotes deep abscess accumulation.
  • Purpura & Bullae: Denotes underlying microvascular thrombosis, tissue ischemia, and severe sepsis (e.g., Streptococcal, Vibrio vulnificus).

Fournier's Gangrene: Fulminant NSTI of the perineum, scrotum, and genitalia.

⭐ Management Axiom: Extremely low threshold for surgical exploration! Immediate aggressive debridement of ALL devitalized tissue and bullae + empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (Cephalosporins + Vancomycin/Linezolid for MRSA coverage).

3. Actinomycosis Sulfur Granules

Etiology: Caused by Actinomyces israelii (Gram-positive anaerobic branching filamentous bacilli). Normal inhabitant of oropharynx, GI, and female genital tract.

Presentation: Acute or chronic indurated pyogenic abscesses in the submandibular/paramandibular area ("lumpy jaw") that rupture to form sinus tracts discharging yellow "sulfur granules".

Treatment: High-dose intravenous Penicillin G followed by extended oral Penicillin. Surgical debridement indicated for dense necrotic tissue and sinus tracts.

4. Viral Pathologies (HPV & HIV) Surgical Risks

Human Papillomavirus (HPV):

  • Verruca Vulgaris: Common warts caused by HPV types 1, 2, and 4.
  • Epidermodysplasia Verruciformis: Rare autosomal recessive condition predisposing to widespread HPV infection (HPV 5 and 8) with extremely high risk of transformation to Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC).

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV):

  • Acute Retroviral Syndrome: Morbilliform rash on face/trunk in 30-50%.
  • Malignancies: Kaposi's Sarcoma (<5% of HIV patients). ⭐ Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) is the MOST COMMON skin cancer associated with HIV!

05. Benign Cutaneous & Soft Tissue Neoplasms

Hemangiomas, Nevi, Cysts, Keratoses, Soft Tissue Tumors, and Neural Neoplasms.

Hemangioma Propranolol

Benign vascular endothelial cell proliferation. Rapid growth during 1st year of life, followed by gradual childhood involution.

Involution Timeline:

  • 50% involute by age 5.
  • 70% involute by age 7.

⭐ First-Line Therapy: Propranolol (oral beta-blocker).

Surgical Resection: Reserved for high-risk functional areas (upper eyelid obstructing vision, nasal airway, perioral), refractory hemorrhage, or residual cosmetic defects in adolescence.

Melanocytic Nevi Depth Layers

Benign melanocytic hyperplasias. UV exposure increases density.

  • Junctional Nevi: Confined to dermoepidermal junction.
  • Compound Nevi: Present in both epidermis and dermis.
  • Dermal Nevi: Completely within the dermis.

⭐ Giant Congenital Nevi: Defined as >20 cm in size. Possesses a 5% risk of malignant transformation into Melanoma → Surgical excision recommended.

Cystic Lesions Foul Odor
  • Epidermoid Cysts (Epidermal Inclusion / Sebaceous Cyst): Arise from keratin-plugged pilosebaceous units. Contain cheesy keratinaceous debris with a characteristic foul-smelling/malodorous discharge.
  • Trichilemmal Cyst (Pilar Cyst): Originates from the outer root sheath of hair follicles (most common on the scalp).
  • Dermoid Cyst: Congenital inclusion of ectodermal elements containing hair follicles and skin appendages. Most common at the lateral brow area.
Keratoses Actinic vs Seborrheic

Actinic Keratosis:

Premalignant neoplasm of keratinocytes in sun-exposed elderly. ⭐ 10% transform into SCC within 2 years (60-65% of all SCCs originate from Actinic Keratosis).
Tx: Excision, topical 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), cryotherapy.

Seborrheic Keratosis:

Benign, brown "stuck-on" waxy papules/plaques in elderly. NOT premalignant!
Tx: Excision for cosmetics/irritation; dermoscopy to rule out BCC.

Soft Tissue Tumors Pucker Sign
  • Acrochordon (Skin Tag): Benign epidermal outpouching with a central collagenous core.
  • Dermatofibroma: Firm pink-to-brown papule/nodule that puckers or dimples inward at the center when pinched ("Fitzpatrick's dimple sign").
  • Lipoma: Most common subcutaneous neoplasm. Benign fat cell tumor; no malignant potential.
  • Liposarcoma: Malignant fatty tumor; deep-seated, rapidly growing, fixed, painful, and invasive.
Neural Tumors NF-1 & NF-2
  • Neuroma: Disordered proliferation of Schwann cells and axons, commonly post-traumatic and painful.
  • Schwannoma: Benign sheath tumor of Schwann cells. Arises sporadically or with Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF-2).
  • Neurofibroma: Composed of all nerve elements. Arises sporadically or with NF-1 (Café-au-lait spots, Lisch nodules).
    ⭐ Red Flag: Sudden pain or rapid growth in a previously asymptomatic neurofibroma indicates malignant transformation!

06. Cutaneous Malignancies & Sarcomas

Complete surgical oncology details: BCC, SCC, Melanoma, Merkel Cell, DFSP, Angiosarcoma, and EMPD.

1. Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) Most Common Skin Cancer

Most common malignant skin tumor (>1 million cases/yr in US). Males > Females, >60 years old. Primary risk factor: UVB exposure. Mutated p53 tumor suppressor gene present in 50% of cases. Indolent growth, extremely low metastatic rate (<0.1%).

BCC Subtypes:
  • Nodular BCC: Most common overall. Pearly pink papule with telangiectasias, central umbilication/ulceration ("Rodent Ulcer").
  • Superficial Spreading BCC: Flat, pink scaling lesion on trunk; often mistaken for eczema or psoriasis.
  • Micronodular / Infiltrative BCC: Yellow-white, opaque, aggressive subtypes.
  • Morpheaform (Sclerosing) BCC: Indurated scar-like papule/plaque. Most aggressive subtype!
  • Basosquamous BCC: Hybrid of BCC and SCC with metastatic potential.
Surgical Management Rules:
  • Small / Low-Risk BCC: Surgical excision with 4 mm clear margins.
  • High-Risk / Aggressive / Facial BCC: Excision with 10 mm (1 cm) margins or Mohs Micrographic Surgery.
  • ⭐ Mohs Surgery: Treatment of choice for Morpheaform BCC and facial aesthetic zones; achieves lowest recurrence rate (<1%).
  • Topical Imiquimod / 5-FU: Option for small superficial BCCs in non-surgical candidates.
2. Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) Marjolin's Ulcer

Second most common skin cancer. Risk factors: UV radiation (Fitzpatrick type I/II fair skin), chemical carcinogens, chronic immunosuppression, chronic non-healing wounds, and burn scars.

SCC In Situ Subtypes:
  • Actinic Keratosis: Premalignant lesion (10% progress to invasive SCC).
  • Bowen's Disease: In situ SCC presenting as a scaly erythematous plaque with fissuring.
  • Erythroplasia of Queyrat: In situ SCC specifically involving the glans penis (10% progress to invasive SCC).
  • ⭐ Marjolin's Ulcer: Aggressive SCC arising in chronic burn scars, non-healing ulcers, or osteomyelitis sinus tracts (transformation latency: 30–40 years!). Metastatic rate: 20–30%.
Surgical Excision Margins:
  • Low-Risk SCC: Surgical excision with 4 mm margins.
  • High-Risk SCC (>2 cm, deep, high grade): Excision with 6 mm margins down to healthy tissue.
  • Lymph Node Dissection: Performed following positive FNAB or core needle biopsy of clinically palpable regional lymph nodes (or CT-proven nodes).
3. Malignant Melanoma Most Dreaded Cutaneous Malignancy

Arises from melanocytes at dermoepidermal junction or mucosal surfaces. High early metastatic potential; median survival with distant metastasis = 6–8 months. 30% arise from pre-existing nevi. Risk factor: Intermittent intense UV exposure / childhood sunburns (>10 tanning bed sessions doubles relative risk). Pathways: BRAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K-Akt, p16/CDK4,6.

Melanoma Subtypes:
  • Superficial Spreading: Most common overall (50–70%).
  • Nodular: 15–30%; aggressive vertical growth phase.
  • Lentigo Maligna: 10%; sun-damaged facial skin of elderly.
  • ⭐ Acral Lentiginous: 29–72% in dark-skinned individuals (Filipinos); occurs on palms, soles, and subungual nail beds.
Prognosis & Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy:
  • ⭐ Breslow Tumor Thickness: Microscopic depth measurement in mm; the MOST IMPORTANT prognostic factor in melanoma.
  • Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (SLNB): Standard staging procedure for clinically node-negative melanoma. Identifies first draining node using lymphoscintigraphy with Technetium-99m sulfur colloid tracer and/or isosulfan blue dye.
  • Isolated Limb Infusion (ILI) / Perfusion (ILP): Delivers high-dose Melphalan chemotherapy under tourniquet control for recurrent/in-transit extremity disease.

Ultra-condensed bullet summaries, diagnostic "If you see X → think Y", buzzwords, and high-yield surgical memory tricks designed for a 10-minute read before the exam.

🎯 "If You See X → Think Y" Trigger Rules
  • If you see: Maculopapular rash after Sulfonamide/Carbamazepine + lateral skin pressure epidermal sloughing → Think: Epidermal Necrolysis (SJS/TEN) [(+) Nikolsky Sign].
  • If you see: Axillary/groin deep tender nodules, recurrent purulent discharge, subterranean sinus tracts → Think: Hidradenitis Suppurativa (Hurley Stage 3).
  • If you see: Painful leg ulceration with raised, overhanging purple/violaceous borders in a patient with Crohn's disease → Think: Pyoderma Gangrenosum.
  • If you see: Chemical burn from Liquid Sosa (NaOH) → Think: Liquefactive Necrosis → Needs 2 hours continuous saline flush (DO NOT NEUTRALIZE!).
  • If you see: Pearly pink papule with telangiectasia and rolled border on face → Think: Nodular Basal Cell Carcinoma.
  • If you see: Indurated scar-like plaque on head/neck without raised edges → Think: Morpheaform BCC (Most aggressive → Needs Mohs Surgery).
  • If you see: Non-healing ulcer/mass arising from a 40-year-old burn scar → Think: Marjolin's Ulcer (Aggressive SCC).
  • If you see: Pigmented lesion on palm/sole/subungual area of a dark-skinned Filipino patient → Think: Acral Lentiginous Melanoma.
  • If you see: Rapidly expanding purple skin papule in elderly + Cytokeratin-20 (CK-20) positive → Think: Merkel Cell Carcinoma (Wide excision 1-3 cm margins).
  • If you see: Violaceous trunk plaque + CD34 positive → Think: Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP) → Treat with 3 cm margins / Imatinib.
  • If you see: Violaceous arm plaque in a female with chronic non-pitting lymphedema 12 years post-mastectomy → Think: Stewart-Treves Syndrome (Angiosarcoma).
  • If you see: Eczema-like scaly plaque on perineum/axilla refractory to steroids → Think: Extramammary Paget's Disease (40% tied to internal GI/GU cancer).
  • If you see: Submandibular indurated mass discharging yellow sulfur granules → Think: Actinomycosis (A. israelii) → Treat with high-dose Penicillin.
  • If you see: Hydrofluoric acid chemical burn → Think: Neutralize with Calcium Gluconate.
  • If you see: Phenol chemical burn → Think: Wash with Polyethylene Glycol (PEG).
⚡ High-Yield Buzzword & Numerical Summary

15%: Proportion of body weight comprised by skin.

4:1 Ratio: Ratio of Type I to Type III Collagen in human dermis.

30 mmHg: Normal capillary perfusion pressure (threshold for pressure necrosis).

30 Days: Epidermal keratinocyte maturation life cycle.

50% at 5 yo / 70% at 7 yo: Involution rates for Infantile Hemangiomas.

>20 cm: Size defining Giant Congenital Nevi (5% melanoma transformation risk).

10% in 2 Years: Transformation rate of Actinic Keratosis to SCC.

10.7% vs 30%: Chemical burns comprise 10.7% of burn admissions but 30% of burn deaths.

4 mm Margin: Surgical excision margin for low-risk BCC and low-risk SCC.

6 mm Margin: Surgical excision margin for high-risk SCC.

10 mm (1 cm) Margin: Surgical excision margin for high-risk BCC.

1–3 cm Margin: Surgical excision margin for Merkel Cell Carcinoma down to fascia.

3 cm Margin: Surgical excision margin for Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP).

Propranolol: 1st line medical therapy for infantile Hemangioma.

Breslow Thickness: Single most important prognostic factor in Malignant Melanoma.
