💡 Core Concepts Snapshot

Neurodiagnostics: Cranial CT is best for acute hemorrhage & bones; Cranial MRI is preferred for brainstem, posterior fossa, and white matter demyelination.

Lumbar Puncture: Intervertebral space L4-L5 (Tuffier's line). Traverses 9 distinct tissue layers. Never perform if there is an intracranial mass (risk of fatal brain herniation)!

Sleep Regulation: Flip-flop switch between VLPO (GABAergic, Sleep-ON) and Monoaminergic nuclei (LC, TMN, Raphe = Wake-ON), stabilized by lateral hypothalamic Orexin (Hypocretin) neurons.

Narcolepsy Type 1: Characterized by loss of orexinergic neurons, cataplexy, CSF hypocretin $\le 110\text{ pg/mL}$, and $\ge 2$ SOREMPs on MSLT.

🧠 Core Topic: Neuroimaging & Diagnostic Modalities

1. Cranial Computed Tomography (CT Scan)

Cranial CT measures attenuation of radiation expressed in Hounsfield Units (HU). Anything containing calcium or acute blood appears hyperdense (white).

Simplified Hounsfield Scale (HU)

Air (-1000)
Fat (-100)
Water (0)
Soft Tissue (30-45)
Blood (60-90)
Contrast (100-500)
Bone (+1000)
Figure 2.1: Hounsfield Unit spectrum. Anything high in density (calcium, bone, acute blood) is hyperdense (white); air/fat is hypodense (black).

Common Anatomical Views

  • Axial: Cross-sectional cut from top to bottom.
  • Coronal: Frontal cut from front to back.
  • Sagittal: Side cut separating left and right.

Axial Cut Sequence Landmarks

  • Foramen Magnum: Bottom opening.
  • Cerebellum & Brainstem: Rounded structure.
  • Midbrain: Looks like Mickey Mouse.
  • Ventricles: Slits representing lateral ventricles.

Plain vs. Contrast CT

  • Plain CT: Best for hyperdense acute hemorrhage and bone fractures.
  • Contrast CT: Uses iodinated contrast to highlight vasculature, blood-brain barrier breakdown (tumors, abscesses).
  • CT Angiography (CTA/CTV): Visualizes arterial aneurysms or draining venous sinuses.
⭐
Must Know: CT Densities CSF/Ventricles → Hypodense (Black, -10 to +20 HU) | Brain Parenchyma → Gray (30-45 HU) | Acute Blood → Hyperdense (White, 60-90 HU) | Bone → Very White (+1000 HU).

2. Cranial Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI uses nuclear magnetic resonance of hydrogen atoms (nonionizing energy). It provides higher soft-tissue contrast resolution and is the preferred modality for the posterior fossa and brainstem.

TABLE 2.1: MRI Sequences & Tissue Characteristics
Tissue / Structure CT Gray Scale MRI T1 Signal MRI T2 Signal FLAIR Signal
Brain Parenchyma Gray Gray Gray Gray
CSF Black Black (Hypointense) White (Hyperintense) Suppressed (Black)
Acute Ischemia / Lesion Dark Gray Gray / Hypointense White / Hyperintense Accentuated (Bright White)
Fat Black White Less White Gray/White
Bone / Calcium Very White Black Black Black

Special MRI Sequences

DWI & ADC (Stroke Workup)

  • Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI): Acute ischemic lesions appear hyperintense (bright white) due to restricted water diffusion.
  • Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC): Acute lesions appear hypointense (dark). Confirms true acute stroke (prevents T2 shine-through).

Gradient Echo (GRE) & MRS

  • Gradient Echo (GRE): Sensitive for microhemorrhages and hemosiderin deposits (dark spots).
  • MR Spectroscopy (MRS): Measures metabolic signatures.
    • Tumors: High Choline peak, Low NAA peak. Lactate/lipid indicates necrosis.
    • Pyogenic Abscess: Amino acid, acetate, and succinate peaks (anaerobic metabolism).
    • Tuberculoma: Prominent lipid peaks without amino acids.

Functional MRI (fMRI)

Measures local cerebral blood oxygenation using the BOLD (Blood-Oxygen Level Dependent) signal. Used clinically in presurgical planning for brain tumors or epilepsy (e.g., awake craniotomy to map speech/motor areas).

Spine MRI

Uses T1 and T2 sequences. T1 CSF is dark; T2 CSF is white. FLAIR suppresses normal CSF signal to accentuate spinal cord lesions (demyelination, myelopathy, plaques).

⚠️
Common Pitfall: Hemorrhage Detection Acute hyperacute intracranial hemorrhage is detected faster and more reliably on plain Cranial CT than on standard MRI sequences!

⚡ Core Topic: Neurophysiological Tests

1. Electroencephalogram (EEG)

Records electrical activity generated by cerebral cortical neurons (thalamocortical relays). Electrodes are placed using the International 10-20 System.

Electrode Placement Conventions

  • Odd-numbered leads (F3, C3, P3, T3, O1): Left hemisphere.
  • Even-numbered leads (F4, C4, P4, T4, O2): Right hemisphere.
  • Letter designations: F = Frontal, C = Central, T = Temporal, P = Parietal, O = Occipital.

EEG Brain Wave Frequencies

  • Alpha (8–12 Hz): Posterior dominant rhythm in relaxed, awake adult with eyes closed. Reactive to eye opening.
  • Theta (4–7 Hz): Drowsiness, N1 sleep, or light encephalopathy.
  • Delta (1–3 Hz): Deep sleep (N3) or severe structural/metabolic brain lesions.
  • Beta (>13 Hz): Alert, active thinking or sedative drug effect.
TABLE 3.1: EEG Clinical Applications & Characteristic Waveforms
Clinical Condition Key EEG Findings / Waveforms Clinical Significance
Epilepsy Spikes, sharp waves, spike-and-wave complexes Confirms epileptogenic focus; documents response to AED therapy.
Hepatic Encephalopathy Generalized Triphasic Waves Classic metabolic encephalopathy wave pattern.
Dementia Generalized slowing of background alpha rhythm Differs from expected age-matched baseline slowing.
Encephalitis Lethargica / Seizures Nonconvulsive status epilepticus discharges Explains unexplained altered mental status / coma.
💡
Clinical Pearl: Activation Procedures Standard activation procedures used during EEG to provoke latent epileptiform discharges include: Hyperventilation, Photic Stimulation (flashing lights), and Sleep Deprivation.

2. Electromyography & Nerve Conduction Velocity (EMG-NCV)

Distinguishes neurogenic (nerve/radicle) from myopathic (muscle) or neuromuscular junction disorders.

Electromyography (EMG) - Muscle Needle Test

Evaluates LMN integrity at rest and during contraction:

  • Insertional Activity: Increased in denervated muscle.
  • Abnormal Spontaneous Activity: Fibrillation potentials and Fasciculation potentials (indicates active LMN denervation).
  • Single-Fiber EMG: Highly sensitive for Neuromuscular Junction failure (Myasthenia Gravis jitter).

Nerve Conduction Studies (NCV)

Evaluates motor and sensory nerve conduction:

  • CMAP (Compound Muscle Action Potential): Reduced amplitude = Axonal loss.
  • Distal Latency & Velocity: Prolonged latency / slow velocity = Demyelination.
  • SNAP: Sensory Nerve Action Potential amplitude.
  • H-Reflex & F-Wave: Assesses proximal nerve roots and radiculopathies.
  • Repetitive Nerve Stimulation (RNS): Decremental response in Myasthenia Gravis.
🧠
Exam Tip: EMG-NCV Findings Fibrillations + Fasciculations = Lower Motor Neuron (LMN) pathology. Axonal injury causes low CMAP/SNAP amplitude with normal velocity; Demyelination causes prolonged latency and slow conduction velocity!

3. Evoked Potentials & Other Special Tests

  • Visual Evoked Potentials (VEP): Measures P100 latency along optic pathways. Prolongation confirms demyelination in Multiple Sclerosis.
  • Brainstem Auditory Evoked Responses (BAER): Evaluates interwave latencies I–III and III–V. Used for acoustic neuromas and brainstem tumors.
  • Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SSEP): Assesses dorsal column spinal pathways.
  • Video Nystagmography (VNG) / Vestibular Testing: Differentiates central from peripheral vertigo (e.g., BPPV).

💉 Core Topic: Lumbar Puncture & CSF Analysis

1. Lumbar Puncture (LP) Technique & Traversed Layers

LP is both a diagnostic tool for CNS infections/demyelination and a therapeutic procedure to reduce intracranial pressure in Cryptococcal or TB meningitis.

Landmarks & Patient Positioning

  • Anatomical Site: Intervertebral space between L4 and L5 (or L3-L4).
  • Landmark: Intersecting line connecting the superior borders of the posterior iliac crests (Tuffier's line) crosses L4 spinous process.
  • Positioning: Lateral decubitus position with maximum back flexion ("fetal position") and vertical alignment of shoulders and pelvis.

Sequential Layers Traversed (Superficial to Deep)

  1. Skin
  2. Subcutaneous Tissue
  3. Back Muscles / Fascia
  4. Supraspinous Ligament
  5. Interspinous Ligament
  6. Ligamentum Flavum ("Pop" #1)
  7. Epidural Space
  8. Dura Mater & Arachnoid Mater ("Pop" #2)
  9. Subarachnoid Space (CSF Collection Site)
⚠️
ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: Intracranial Mass Effect Performing LP in the presence of a focal brain mass or obstructive hydrocephalus creates a pressure gradient that can cause fatal transtentorial or tonsillar brain herniation! Always check for papilledema or perform CT scan first if focal deficits exist.

2. Comprehensive CSF Diagnostic Formulas

Normal Opening Pressure: 100–180 mmH2O. Normal Glucose: 2/3 of blood glucose (40–80 mg/dL). Normal Protein: 15–45 mg/dL.

Condition Cellularity (cells/mm³) Protein (mg/dL) Glucose (mg/dL) Opening Pressure & Key Features
Bacterial Meningitis > 500–10,000 (PMNs) Markedly High (>100–500) Markedly Low (<40%) Elevated pressure; Gram stain positive; cloudy fluid.
Viral Meningitis 10–500 (Lymphocytes) Normal to Slightly High (50–100) Normal Normal/Slightly elevated pressure; clear fluid.
Tuberculous (TB) Meningitis 25–500 (Lymphocytes) Extremely High (100–1000+) Markedly Low (<30%) Cobweb clot/pellicle formation; Froin syndrome risk.

Queckenstedt Maneuver

Compression of internal jugular veins normally causes a rapid rise in CSF opening pressure, which drops upon release. Positive test (failure of pressure to rise): Indicates spinal subarachnoid block or compression.

Froin Syndrome

Xanthochromic, highly proteinaceous CSF that coagulates spontaneously in the tube. Results from severe CSF stasis below a complete spinal cord block.

🌙 Core Topic: Sleep Physiology & Sleep-Wake Mechanisms

1. Circadian Rhythm & Sleep Architecture

Sleep is a reversible state of decreased sensorium modulated by the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus (SCN) of the anterior hypothalamus ("Master Clock"), entrained by light/dark environmental cues (zeitgebers) via the retinohypothalamic tract.

Age-Related Changes

  • Newborns: 16–20 hrs sleep; 50% spent in REM (60-min cycles).
  • Age 4–5: Consolidation into single nocturnal period.
  • Young Adults: 7–7.5 hrs sleep; 20–25% REM, 50–60% N2, 10–20% N3.
  • Elderly: ~6.5 hrs sleep; marked loss of N3 (deep slow-wave sleep) and sleep fragmentation.

Homeostatic vs Circadian Drive

Homeostatic Sleep Drive: Adenosine accumulates during wakefulness, increasing sleep pressure.

Circadian Drive: SCN-driven signal maintaining alertness during daylight hours.

Hormonal Dynamics During Sleep

  • Growth Hormone (GH): Sharp secretion surge during first 2 hours of sleep (Stage N3).
  • Cortisol & TSH: Peak at sleep onset and early morning awakening.
  • Melatonin: Secreted by Pineal Gland in darkness.

2. AASM Sleep Stages & EEG Features

TABLE 5.1: AASM Sleep Stages, EEG Landmarks, & Physiology
Stage Name Characteristic EEG Waveforms Motor & Ocular Physiology
W Wakefulness Posterior dominant Alpha (9–11 Hz) (eyes closed) High muscle tone; blinking artifacts.
N1 Drowsiness / Light Sleep Low-voltage, mixed frequency Theta (4–7 Hz) Slow rolling eye movements; decreased muscle tone.
N2 Light Sleep Sleep Spindles (12–14 Hz) & K-Complexes / Vertex Waves No eye movements; moderate tone. Accounts for ~50% total sleep.
N3 Deep Slow-Wave Sleep High-amplitude, slow Delta waves (1–3 Hz) (>20% epoch) Exotropic eyes, reactive small pupils. Hardest stage to arouse. GH surge.
R (REM) Rapid Eye Movement Desynchronized, low-voltage, mixed-frequency ("Sawtooth" waves) Skeletal Muscle Atonia, Rapid conjugate eye movements, Vivid dreaming.

Phasic vs. Tonic REM Sleep

  • Phasic REM: Sympathetic nervous system (SNS) bursts, fluctuating HR, BP, and respiration, pupil dilation/constriction, transient muscle twitches.
  • Tonic REM: Parasympathetic (PNS) predominance, profound skeletal muscle flaccidity/atonia, quiet background REM.

The Neurochemistry of Dreaming

Dreaming is mediated by basal forebrain dopaminergic pathways and activation of extrastriate visual cortices & limbic regions, with concurrent attenuation of primary visual and frontal association cortices (explaining bizarre, uncritical dream acceptance).

3. The Flip-Flop Switch Model

😴 Core Topic: Sleep Deprivation, Insomnia & Movement Disorders

1. Sleep Deprivation

Clinical Manifestations

  • Common: Fatigue, daytime sleepiness, irritability, headaches, cognitive deficits, slowed reaction times.
  • Severe (>60–200 hrs): Lapses of attention, microsleeps (seconds to minutes), hand tremors, slurred speech, visual and tactile hallucinations.

Neurological & EEG Findings

  • Inconstant nystagmus, impaired saccadic eye movements, loss of accommodation, ptosis, exophoria, expressionless face.
  • EEG: Decrement of alpha waves; alpha rhythm no longer reacts to eye closure. Appearance of theta rhythms.
  • Reduced Seizure Threshold: Provokes epileptiform spikes in predisposed patients.

2. Insomnia Disorder

Chronic inability to initiate or maintain sleep despite adequate opportunity and circumstances for sleep, causing daytime dysfunction.

ICSD-3 & DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

  • Short-term Insomnia: ≥ 3 nights per week for < 3 months.
  • Chronic Insomnia Disorder: ≥ 3 nights per week for ≥ 3 months.
  • Note: DSM-5 removed the distinction between primary and secondary insomnia, unifying under Insomnia Disorder.

Management Hierarchy

  1. First-Line: CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia): Sleep hygiene, stimulus control, sleep restriction, relaxation.
  2. Short-term Hypnotics:
    • Z-drugs: Zolpidem, Zaleplon, Eszopiclone (α1 GABA-A agonists).
    • Benzodiazepines: Temazepam (intermediate half-life).
    • Orexin Antagonists: Suvorexant.

3. Restless Leg Syndrome (RLS) & Periodic Limb Movement Disorder (PLMD)

Restless Leg Syndrome (RLS) Diagnostic Mnemonic

Mnemonic: U-R-G-E

Urge to move legs (unpleasant aching/creepy-crawly sensation)

Rest induced (provoked during inactivity/drowsiness)

Gets better with voluntary movement/activity

Evening and night worsening

Etiology & Risk Factors

  • Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) / Low Ferritin levels (<50 ng/mL).
  • Uremia / Chronic Kidney Disease.
  • Pregnancy, Thyroid disease.
  • Drugs: Antidepressants, sedating antihistamines.

Pharmacotherapy

Dopamine Agonists: Pramipexole, Ropinirole, Levodopa-Carbidopa. Alternatively: Gabapentin, Pregabalin.

Periodic Limb Movements of Sleep (PLMD)

Slower, repetitive involuntary leg movements (similar to triple flexion) occurring at 20–90 second intervals during NREM sleep. ~80% of RLS patients have co-existing PLMD.

🎭 Core Topic: Parasomnias & Neurologic Sleep Disorders

1. Parasomnias Classification

Abnormal behavioral, motor, or experiential events occurring during sleep onset, within sleep, or during arousal.

TABLE 7.1: Comprehensive Parasomnia & Differential Diagnosis Matrix
Condition Sleep Stage Clinical Features Dream Recall & Autonomic Features Treatment & Associations
Somnolescent Starts Sleep Onset (N1) Sudden myoclonic body jerks upon falling asleep None; benign physiological phenomenon Reassurance; avoid sleep deprivation/caffeine.
Sleep Paralysis Sleep Onset / Waking Inability to move muscles despite full consciousness 75% experience hallucinations (Intruder, V-M, Chest Pressure) Persistence of REM motor atonia. Associated with Narcolepsy.
Night Terrors N3 (Slow-Wave) Child awakens screaming, intense terror, autonomic surge (tachycardia) NO dream recall; Amnesia for event Disorder of arousal. Self-limiting, outgrown with age.
REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD) REM Sleep Older male acting out violent, action-packed dreams (punching, kicking) Vivid dream recall upon waking α-synucleinopathy prodrome (PD, DLB, MSA). Tx: Melatonin, Clonazepam.

2. Sleep Disorders Related to Specific Neurologic Lesions

Upper Pontine Lesions

Lesions near the locus coeruleus cause profound disruption and dysregulation of sleep architecture.

Bilateral Paramedian Thalamic Infarcts

Disrupts thalamocortical radiations from the ARAS, causing severe, intractable hypersomnia.

Medullary Lesions

Causes loss of automatic respiratory control during sleep: Central Sleep Apnea / Ondine's Curse.

Von Economo Encephalitis (Encephalitis Lethargica)

Etiology: Post-infectious viral/streptococcal. Features two distinct presentation types based on lesion localization:

  • Hypersomnia + Parkinsonism: Destruction of dopamine-rich neurons in the dorsal hypothalamus & subthalamus.
  • Persistent Insomnia: Lesion in the anterior hypothalamus & basal frontal lobes.

🫁 Core Topic: Sleep Apnea, Narcolepsy & Clinical Case Study

1. Sleep Apnea Syndromes

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

  • Pathophysiology: Repeated upper airway obstruction due to posterior pharyngeal soft tissue collapse during sleep.
  • Clinical Clues: Loud, irregular snoring, witnessed apneic pauses, daytime somnolence, morning headaches.
  • Complications: Systemic hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, pulmonary hypertension, sudden cardiac death.
  • Treatment: Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP), weight loss.

Central Sleep Apnea (CSA)

  • Pathophysiology: Loss of central respiratory drive from lower brainstem/medullary centers.
  • Clinical Clues: Mild/intermittent snoring; total cessation of breathing effort without pharyngeal obstruction (Ondine's Curse).
  • Treatment: Treat underlying cause; low-flow oxygen, nocturnal ventilation support.

2. Narcolepsy & Cataplexy

Pathogenesis: Selective autoimmune destruction of orexinergic (hypocretin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus. Strong genetic association with HLA-DQB1*0602.

3. Comprehensive Clinical Case Study: Patient EDC (68M)

Subjective Data & History

  • Patient Profile: EDC, 68-year-old male, baseline independent.
  • Chief Complaint: Violent sleep behaviors for 2 years.
  • HPI: 2-year history of progressive punching and kicking while asleep in the latter half of the night. Awakens immediately with vivid recall of action-packed, frightening fighting dreams. Wife sustained bruises. Denies daytime sleepiness, RLS, or sleepwalking.
  • ROS: Denies motor/sensory deficits, tremors. Pertinent Positives: Hyposmia (decreased smell over past year) and occasional constipation.

Objective Data & Neuro Examination

  • Vital Signs: BP 130/70, HR 87, RR 18, Temp 36.7°C, SpO2 98%.
  • Mental Status: Alert, oriented, intact memory. Spells "MUNDO" forwards and backwards.
  • Cranial Nerves: CN I-XII intact. Full EOMs, no facial asymmetry.
  • Motor & Reflexes: 5/5 strength throughout; normal bulk/tone. Babinski negative bilaterally. Cerebellar & tandem gait normal.

Polysomnography (v-PSG) Diagnostic Results

Normal sleep latency and efficiency. No significant OSA (AHI < 5). PLM index < 15/hr. Video/EMG Findings: Captured complex motor behaviors (punching, kicking, yelling) during REM sleep directly corresponding to sustained loss of normal REM muscle atonia (increased chin/limb EMG tone).

Anatomical Localization of REM Atonia

Normal REM atonia originates in the pontine tegmentum (sublaterodorsal / subcoeruleus nucleus) projecting to the ventromedial reticular formation in the ventral medulla. Inhibitory GABAergic and glycinergic spinal interneurons inhibit spinal motor neurons. Lesion in this circuit causes REM Sleep Without Atonia (RSWA).

Final Diagnosis & Neurodegenerative Prognosis

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Idiopathic REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD).

Clinical Significance: Strongest known prodromal marker for α-synucleinopathies (Parkinson's Disease, Dementia with Lewy Bodies, Multiple System Atrophy). Phenoconversion rate is >80% over 10–15 years!
