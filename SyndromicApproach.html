Course Overview & Syndromic Principles LECTURE MODULE 01

💡 Core Instructional Principle
Infectious disease board exams test your ability to synthesize clinical presentation, exposure history, anatomic depth, and localized resistance patterns. Always identify: (1) Anatomic site & depth, (2) Most likely pathogen based on exposure/host, (3) Need for immediate surgical source control vs medical management, and (4) Proper narrow-spectrum vs broad-spectrum empiric regimen per national/IDSA guidelines.
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Analyze different manifestations of clinical infectious syndromes across organ systems.
  • Identify the most common etiology based on patient age, comorbidities, host factors, and specific animal/environmental exposures.
  • Formulate appropriate, evidence-based empiric antimicrobial therapy adhering strictly to DOH National Antibiotic Guidelines 2018.
  • Differentiate surgical emergencies (Necrotizing Fasciitis, Secondary Peritonitis/Pneumoperitoneum, Left Lobe Liver Abscess) from medical conditions (Cellulitis, Uncomplicated SBP).
🔑 Core Diagnostic Paradigms
  • Anatomic Depth Rule: The deeper the soft tissue infection, the longer the treatment duration, the higher the risk of microvascular thrombosis, and the more severe the systemic toxicity.
  • Pus vs Non-Pus: Staphylococcus aureus = purulent/pus-forming; Streptococcus pyogenes (GAS) = non-purulent/spreading.
  • SBP Anaerobic Rule: SBP is monomicrobial and DOES NOT require anaerobic coverage because ascitic fluid is naturally bacteriostatic against B. fragilis and has high $PO_2$.
  • Infective Endocarditis Rule: S. aureus is the ONLY organism capable of infecting intact, undamaged heart valves.

⚡ Necrotizing Fasciitis & Myonecrosis

Pathophysiology: Breach in epidermis → exotoxin production (Panton-Valentine Leukocidin, Streptococcal Pyogenic Exotoxin, Streptolysin, TSST, Hyaluronidase) → local tissue damage & dermal permeability → intense hypercoagulable state → microvascular thrombosis → tissue ischemia & necrosis → rapid fascial spread.

⭐ MUST KNOW CLINICAL TRIAD
1. Pain out of proportion to physical findings.
2. Rapid evolution of skin discoloration: Erythema → Violaceous → Dark bronze/green-black patches of necrosis.
3. Systemic toxicity: High fever, tachycardia, hypotension, confusion/obtundation due to cytokine storm.

Laboratory Clues: Elevated WBC (leukocytosis), decreased Hemoglobin, Hyponatremia, elevated Creatinine (sepsis-induced AKI).

Diagnosis: A surgical diagnosis. Prompt surgical exploration and aggressive debridement is mandatory.

🔴 Cellulitis Etiologic Spectrum
  • Streptococcus pyogenes (GAS): Non-purulent cellulitis, poorly demarcated, accompanied by lymphangitis (venous cording) and lymphadenitis. Recurrent cellulitis.
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Purulent cellulitis with purulent drainage.
  • Group B Streptococcus (S. agalactiae): Common in elderly, Type 2 DM, and Peripheral Vascular Disease (PVD). Note: Cover GAS + GBS in insulin-requiring diabetics!
  • Haemophilus influenzae: Pediatric buccal or periorbital cellulitis (rare in adults due to Hib vaccine).
  • Aeromonas hydrophila: Aggressive necrotizing cellulitis following freshwater trauma.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Neutropenic patients (ANC <500/µL), hot-tub folliculitis, penetrating trauma ("napako" or motor vehicle accidents).
  • Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae: Occupational exposure in fishing/poultry handlers. Intrinsically resistant to Vancomycin, Cotrimoxazole, Chloramphenicol!
⚠️ COMMON PITFALL: Fresh Water Soft Tissue Infections
When a patient presents with skin infection following fresh water exposure, suspect Aeromonas hydrophila, Vibrio vulnificus, or Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae. NEVER give Vancomycin monotherapy—it is ineffective against all three (Aeromonas and Vibrio are Gram-negatives, while Erysipelothrix is intrinsically resistant to Vancomycin)!

Comprehensive DOH/IDSA Antibiotic Regimens for SSTIs

Type 1 (Polymicrobial) vs Type 2 (GAS) Necrotizing Fasciitis Regimens

Type 1 (Polymicrobial - Aerobic + Anaerobic):

  • 1st Line: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h (Target trough: 15–20 µg/mL).
  • 2nd Line: Meropenem 1 g IV q8h PLUS Vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h.
  • If Penicillin Allergy (Rash only): Cefepime 1–2 g q8–12h + Metronidazole 500 mg IV q6h + Vancomycin.
  • If Severe Anaphylaxis: Levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily (or Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h) + Metronidazole + Vancomycin.

Type 2 (Group A Streptococcus / Monomicrobial):

  • 1st Line: Penicillin G 4 MU IV q4h PLUS Clindamycin 600–900 mg IV q8h.
  • WHY CLINDAMYCIN? Penicillin G kills the organism, but Clindamycin suppresses exotoxin production by binding to 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibiting protein synthesis (Eagle effect).
  • 2nd Line (PCN Allergy): Vancomycin 1 g IV q12h.
Erysipelas & Cellulitis DOH Guidelines

Non-Purulent Cellulitis & Erysipelas:

  • Mild/Outpatient: Clindamycin 300–450 mg PO TID x 5–10 days OR Cephalexin 500 mg PO QID OR Penicillin VK 500 mg PO QID.
  • Inpatient Parenteral: Penicillin G 1–2 MU IV q6h OR Cefazolin 1 g IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily.
  • If Severe IgE Anaphylaxis: Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q12h OR Linezolid 600 mg IV/PO BID.

Purulent Cellulitis (MRSA dominant):

  • Outpatient: Cotrimoxazole 160/800 mg (1-2 DS tabs) PO BID OR Doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h OR Clindamycin 300–450 mg PO TID.
  • Inpatient: Vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q12h (Target trough 15-20 µg/mL) OR Clindamycin 600 mg IV q8h (if no bacteremia).

Acute Infectious Diarrheal Diseases & Bacterial Food Poisoning

⏱️ Incubation Period Diagnostic Algorithm

1 – 6 Hours (Emetic/Nausea Dominant): Pre-formed heat-stable toxins.

  • Staphylococcus aureus: Ham, poultry, potato/egg salad, mayo, cream pastries.
  • Bacillus cereus (Emetic): Reheated fried rice.
  • NO ANTIBIOTICS! Toxin-induced; antibiotics cannot neutralize pre-formed toxins. Rehydrate supportively.

8 – 16 Hours (Abdominal Cramps & Diarrhea): In-vivo toxin production.

  • Clostridium perfringens: Meats, poultry, legumes, gravies.
  • Bacillus cereus (Diarrheal): Meats, vegetables, dried beans, cereals.

>16 Hours (Watery or Inflammatory Diarrhea): Bacterial infection/invasion.

  • Non-inflammatory (No WBCs): Vibrio cholerae, ETEC, Norovirus.
  • Inflammatory (+ Fecal PMNs, Fever, Tenesmus): Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni, V. parahaemolyticus.
✈️ Traveler's Diarrhea & Special Pathogens
  • ETEC (Enterotoxigenic E. coli): #1 single most common cause of Traveler's Diarrhea worldwide (10-45%). Watery non-inflammatory diarrhea.
  • Campylobacter jejuni: Dominant cause in Southeast Asia (Thailand, Philippines). Associated with FQ-resistance! Preferred treatment: Azithromycin 500mg PO daily x 3 days.
  • EHEC (E. coli O157:H7): Produces Shiga toxin. Causes bloody diarrhea WITHOUT fecal leukocytes. Associated with Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS). Eating raw/undercooked ground beef.
  • Giardia lamblia: Hikers/campers drinking fresh stream water. Malabsorptive, greasy, foul-smelling, floating stools + bloating. DOC: Metronidazole.
  • Cryptosporidium: Voluminous watery diarrhea in PLHIV / immunocompromised (low CD4). Resistant to chlorine water treatment.

💡 DOH EMPIRIC TREATMENT GUIDELINES FOR DIARRHEA
• Mild (<3 unformed stools/day): Oral rehydration therapy (ORS).
• Moderate (3–4 stools/day, mild fever): ORS or IV hydration.
• Severe / Inflammatory (Fever, blood, tenesmus, fecal WBCs):
➔ Shigella / General Dysentery: Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO q12h x 3–5 days.
➔ Campylobacter jejuni / SE Asia Travel / ETEC: Azithromycin 500 mg PO q24h x 3 days (preferred in Asia due to high fluoroquinolone resistance).

Intra-Abdominal Infections: SBP vs Secondary Peritonitis

🧪 Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP / PBP)

Pathophysiology: Bacterial translocation from intestines into pre-existing ascitic fluid in cirrhotic patients with portal hypertension. Monomicrobial (E. coli 40%, K. pneumoniae 30%, Streptococci).

⭐ SBP DIAGNOSTIC CUT-OFF
Paracentesis Ascitic Fluid Analysis:
Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) > 250 cells/µL
Formula: ANC = Total Ascitic WBC/µL × % Neutrophils
(e.g., 1,000 WBCs with 90% neutrophils = 900 ANC ➔ SBP Positive!)
⚠️ THE SBP ANAEROBIC EXCLUSION RULE
EMPIRIC COVERAGE FOR ANAEROBES IS NOT NECESSARY IN SBP!
Why? Ascitic fluid possesses intrinsic bacteriostatic activity against Bacteroides fragilis, and the high $PO_2$ in ascitic fluid creates an inhospitable environment for anaerobes.

First-Line Treatment: Cefotaxime 2g IV q8h OR Ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h x 5 days. (Caution: Ceftriaxone can cause biliary sludge/cholestasis in severe liver dysfunction). If PCN allergic: Ampicillin-sulbactam.

🚨 Secondary Peritonitis

Pathophysiology: Perforation of a hollow intra-abdominal viscus (gastric ulcer, appendicitis, diverticulitis, trauma, surgical anastomotic leak) contaminating the sterile peritoneal cavity. Polymicrobial (Facultative GNB + Bacteroides fragilis anaerobes).

Clinical Signs: Rigid board-like abdomen, marked involuntary guarding, direct and rebound tenderness. Patient lies motionless with knees drawn up.

🚨 PATHOGNOMONIC RADIOGRAPHIC SIGN
"Air Under the Diaphragm" (Pneumoperitoneum) on Upright Chest X-ray / Abdominal Film.
This is an absolute SURGICAL EMERGENCY. Antibiotics alone without surgical source control will lead to fatal septic shock.

Empiric Treatment: MUST cover both aerobic GNB and B. fragilis!
• Mild-Moderate: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5g IV q8h OR (Ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h + Metronidazole 500mg IV q6h).
• Severe/Critically Ill: Meropenem 1g IV q8h OR Imipenem 500mg IV q6h.

SBP Variants & Management Rules

SBP Variant Ascitic Fluid Culture Ascitic ANC (PMNs) Clinical Action
Classic SBP Positive (Monomicrobial) > 250 PMNs/µL TREAT IMMEDIATELY (5-day IV Ceftriaxone)
Monomicrobial Non-Neutrocytic Bacterascites Positive (Single isolate) < 250 PMNs/µL TREAT if symptomatic (fever/pain). If asymptomatic: observe & repeat paracentesis.

Polymicrobial Bacterascites Positive (Multiple organisms) Variable Suspect traumatic paracentesis or early Secondary Peritonitis. Workup for viscus leak!

Indications for Long-Term SBP Prophylaxis

⭐ SBP PROPHYLAXIS INDICATIONS (EXAM HIGH YIELD)
Give long-term oral daily prophylaxis with Norfloxacin 400 mg PO daily, Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO daily, or Co-trimoxazole 160/800 mg PO daily if:
  1. Previous documented episode of SBP (Secondary Prophylaxis).
  2. Low protein ascites (Ascitic fluid total protein < 15 g/L or < 1.5 g/dL).
  3. Advanced liver disease (Child-Pugh score ≥ 9 with serum Bilirubin ≥ 3 mg/dL).
  4. Impaired renal function (Serum Creatinine ≥ 1.2 mg/dL, BUN ≥ 25 mg/dL, or Hyponatremia Na ≤ 130 mEq/L).
  5. Acute Upper GI / Variceal Bleeding (Give IV Ceftriaxone 1g/day x 7 days during acute bleed).

Intra-Abdominal Abscesses (Visceral & Space Abscesses)

💡 THE "PERSISTENT FEVER CLUE"
When a post-operative patient or a patient receiving appropriate systemic antimicrobial therapy continues to have persistent spikes of fever without an obvious primary source ➔ ALWAYS SUSPECT AN UNDRAINED INTRA-ABDOMINAL ABSCESS! Order contrast-enhanced Abdominal CT imaging immediately.
🧫 Pathophysiology & Medical Failure

Peritoneal breach → bacterial contamination → acute inflammation → coagulation cascade activation → massive fibrin deposition encapsulating purulent material → central liquefaction necrosis.

Why Antibiotics Alone Fail:

  • Poor antibiotic penetration across dense avascular fibrin capsule.
  • Extremely high bacterial burden and low pH inactivating drugs.
  • Presence of devitalized necrotic debris shielding bacteria.
  • Mandatory Management Rule: Antimicrobials + Source Control (Percutaneous Image-Guided Drainage or Surgical Drainage).
🦠 Microbiology Breakdown
  • Standard Polymicrobial: Enterobacterales (E. coli, Klebsiella) + Anaerobes (Bacteroides fragilis).
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Suggests hematogenous seeding! Primary psoas abscess, splenic abscess, renal cortical abscess.
  • Candida spp.: Suspect in high-risk post-op, recurrent GI perforation, anastomotic leak, necrotizing pancreatitis, or prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Enterococcus spp.: Healthcare-associated infections, severe post-op intra-abdominal sepsis.

Infective Endocarditis (IE)

🫀 Pathophysiology & Pathogen Rules

Endothelial injury (turbulent flow, RHD, VSD, degenerative valve) → exposure of tissue factor → platelet-fibrin deposition → Non-Bacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE) → transient bacteremia → bacterial adherence → mature vegetations.

⭐ THE S. AUREUS ENDOCARDITIS RULE
Staphylococcus aureus is the ONLY bacterial species capable of causing endocarditis on NORMAL, intact, undamaged heart valves!
It produces large, friable vegetations (≥1 cm) with extremely high risk of peripheral, cerebral, and splenic embolization.
  • Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis): Strongly associated with underlying Colonic Malignancy / Polyps. Order colonoscopy immediately!
  • Viridans Streptococci: Indolent course, low virulence, strongly tied to preceding dental procedures/extraction.
  • Enterococcus faecalis: Tied to GI or GU procedures (TURP, cystoscopy, colonoscopy).
🔎 Clinical Manifestations & Stigmata

Common Symptoms: Fever (80-90%), new or changing regurgitant murmur (20-50%), elevated CRP (>90%), anemia (70-90%).

Classic Physical Exam Stigmata:

  • Janeway Lesions: Painless, erythematous macules on palms and soles (Vascular phenomenon / microemboli).
  • Osler Nodes: Painful, tender subcutaneous nodules on pads of fingers and toes (Immunologic phenomenon).
  • Roth Spots: Retinal hemorrhages with pale central centers (Immunologic phenomenon).
  • Splinter Hemorrhages: Linear dark red streaks under nail beds (Vascular phenomenon).

Modified Duke Criteria (2000) for Diagnosis of IE

MAJOR CRITERIA

1. Blood Culture Positive for Typical IE Organisms:

  • Typical microorganisms from ≥2 separate blood cultures: Viridans strep, S. gallolyticus, HACEK group, S. aureus, or community-acquired Enterococci without primary focus.
  • Persistently positive blood culture (≥2 cultures drawn >12h apart, or all of 3, or majority of ≥4).

2. Evidence of Endocardial Involvement:

  • Positive Echocardiogram (TEE preferred): Oscillating intracardiac mass, abscess, new partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve, or new valvular regurgitation.
  • Abnormal PET/CT or Cardiac CT paravalvular lesion.
MINOR CRITERIA
  1. Predisposition: Predisposing heart condition (RHD, prosthetic valve, congenital heart disease) or IV drug use.
  2. Fever: Temperature ≥ 38.0°C (100.4°F).
  3. Vascular Phenomena: Major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial hemorrhage, Janeway lesions.
  4. Immunologic Phenomena: Glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, Rheumatoid Factor (RF).
  5. Microbiologic Evidence: Positive blood culture not meeting major criterion.
💡 DUKE DIAGNOSTIC CATEGORIES
• Definitive IE: 2 Major OR 1 Major + 3 Minor OR 5 Minor Criteria.
• Possible IE: 1 Major + 1 Minor OR 3 Minor Criteria.

⭐ WHEN TO THINK SURGERY IN NATIVE VALVE IE (THE 3 H'S)
1. HEART FAILURE: Refractory pulmonary edema, severe acute aortic/mitral regurgitation.
2. INFECTION CONTROL: Perivalvular abscess, persistent bacteremia >5–7 days despite maximal IV antibiotics, fungal endocarditis.
3. EMBOLIZATION PREVENTION: Recurrent embolic events despite therapy, large persistent vegetation > 10 mm (> 1 cm).

• Penetrating foot injury ("napako") or Hot-tub rash ➔ Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
• Fishing / poultry worker with Vancomycin-resistant cellulitis ➔ Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae.
• Nausea/vomiting 1–6 hours after eating fried rice ➔ Bacillus cereus pre-formed emetic toxin.
• Nausea/vomiting 1–6 hours after eating potato/egg salad or mayo ➔ Staphylococcus aureus enterotoxin.
• Traveler to Thailand/SE Asia with inflammatory diarrhea ➔ Campylobacter jejuni (Treat with Azithromycin due to FQ resistance!).
• Bloody diarrhea WITHOUT fecal leukocytes + burger intake + kidney failure ➔ EHEC (O157:H7) Shiga toxin ➔ Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS).
• Hiker with greasy floating stools + bloating after drinking stream water ➔ Giardia lamblia (DOC Metronidazole).
• Cirrhotic with ascites, fever, confusion, and ascitic ANC >250 PMNs/µL ➔ Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (Treat with IV Ceftriaxone; NO anaerobic coverage needed!).
• "Air under the diaphragm" (Pneumoperitoneum) on X-ray ➔ Perforated hollow viscus / Secondary Peritonitis (Emergency Laparotomy!).
• Persistent post-op fever spikes despite IV antibiotics ➔ Undrained Intra-Abdominal Abscess (Order CT scan!).
• Pyogenic Liver Abscess most common lab abnormality ➔ Elevated Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP).
• Liver Abscess requiring mandatory drainage regardless of size ➔ LEFT LOBE Liver Abscess (Pericardial rupture risk!).
• Male with fever, RUQ pain, and solitary right lobe liver lesion ➔ Amebic Liver Abscess (Metronidazole x 10 days).
• Triad of Fever + LUQ pain + Splenomegaly ➔ Splenic Abscess (Associated with Infective Endocarditis).
• Triad of Fever + Back/Flank pain + Limp/painful hip extension ➔ Psoas Abscess (+ Psoas Sign).
• Endocarditis with positive blood culture for S. gallolyticus (S. bovis) ➔ Underlying Colonic Cancer / Polyps (Order Colonoscopy!).
• Infective Endocarditis in IV Drug Users ➔ Tricuspid valve involvement; S. aureus septic pulmonary emboli.
• Painless erythematous macules on palms/soles ➔ Janeway Lesions (Embolic phenomenon).
• Painful tender nodules on pads of fingers/toes ➔ Osler Nodes (Immunologic phenomenon).

🧫 SSTI Rapid Summary

  • Erysipelas: GAS, superficial dermis, well-demarcated raised margins.
  • Cellulitis: GAS (non-purulent) or S. aureus (purulent); poorly demarcated.
  • Type 1 NF: Polymicrobial ➔ Pip-Tazo + Vancomycin.
  • Type 2 NF: GAS ➔ Pen G + Clindamycin (inhibits toxin!).

🍲 Diarrhea Incubation Summary

  • 1–6h: S. aureus, B. cereus (Emetic) ➔ Pre-formed toxin (No Abx).
  • 8–16h: C. perfringens, B. cereus (Diarrheal) ➔ In-vivo toxin.
  • >16h: V. cholerae, ETEC, Salmonella, Shigella, C. jejuni.

🧪 Peritonitis Rules

  • SBP: Ascitic ANC >250 PMNs/µL. Monomicrobial. NO anaerobic coverage needed!
  • Secondary: Viscus leak. Polymicrobial. Pneumoperitoneum. MUST cover anaerobes!
  • SBP Prophylaxis: Ascitic protein <15 g/L, prior SBP, Child-Pugh ≥9 ➔ Norfloxacin or Cipro.

🫀 Endocarditis Rapid Rules

  • Only organism infecting undamaged valves: S. aureus.
  • Duke Major: 2 (+) blood cultures typical organisms OR (+) Echo/TEE.
  • High risk initial patient (New murmur, AV block, HF) ➔ Go straight to TEE!
  • Native Valve Strep: Pen G or Ceftriaxone x 4 Wks.
  • Enterococcus IE: Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone x 6 Wks.
