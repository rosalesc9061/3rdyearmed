⭐ Must Know

90% of migraine diagnoses are established purely through history taking. Recognize ICHD-3 duration limits: Migraine without aura lasts 4–72 hours. Chronic TTH is defined as ≥15 days/month (Amitriptyline is the only proven drug).

⚠️ Common Pitfalls

Brain parenchyma is PAIN-INSENSITIVE. Do not confuse neurogenic claudication (relieved by sitting/forward bending) with vascular claudication (relieved immediately by standing still).

💡 Clinical Pearl

Presence of bilateral hand/leg symptoms with neck pain indicates Cervical Myelopathy (cord compression) requiring urgent surgical evaluation, whereas unilateral dermatomal symptoms represent Radiculopathy.

🧠 Exam Tip

Temporal Arteritis in >50 yo requires immediate high-dose Prednisone (40-60 mg/day) BEFORE biopsy results to prevent irreversible unilateral blindness due to ophthalmic artery occlusion.

📌 Module Learning Objectives

  • Master obtaining a comprehensive yet concise history (CLITAA) and accurately analyzing symptom profiles for cranial and spinal pain.
  • Understand the neuroanatomical structures responsible for pain transmission in the head and spine.
  • Differentiate primary headache syndromes (Migraine, TTH, TACs) from life-threatening secondary headaches using the SNNOOP10 red/orange flag criteria.

  • Accurately localize nerve root compressions (C5–C8, L2–S1) via physical exam, reflexes, motor testing, and special provocative maneuvers.

🔍 Mechanisms of Cranial Pain & CLITAA History Framework

Cranial pain arises through distinct physiological mechanisms:

  • Traction / Displacement: Mass lesions, tumors, or hematomas pulling on dural structures and blood vessels.
  • Arterial Dilatation: Excessive pulsation and neurogenic inflammation (e.g., migraine, febrile states, nitrites, hangover).
  • Vascular Dissection: Vertebral or carotid artery dissection causing severe neck or peri-orbital pain.
  • Intracranial Pressure Changes: CSF leak (post-LP hypotension) or raised ICP (mass, pseudotumor, venous sinus thrombosis).
  • Meningeal Irritation: Meningitis or Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH) causing diffuse chemical/bacterial stimulation.

⚡ Migraine Overview & Pathophysiology

Migraine is a neurovascular headache disorder with strong genetic polygenic risk. Estrogen plays a major regulatory role (higher female prevalence during reproductive years; abates during 2nd/3rd trimester of pregnancy and post-menopause).

🔬 Pathophysiologic Theories: From Vascular to Trigeminovascular ▼
  • Vascular Theory (Historical): Proposed headache was purely due to extracranial vasodilatation. Now recognized as incomplete.
  • Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD - Leão): A wave of neuronal and glial depolarization that moves across the cerebral cortex at ~2-3 mm/min. Responsible for the visual aura (fortification spectra, scintillating scotomas).
  • Trigeminovascular Theory (Prevailing Model): Activation of small unmyelinated trigeminal C-fibers innervating cranial vessels. Causes release of vasoactive neuropeptides: Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) and Substance P, leading to neurogenic inflammation, vasodilation, and central pain sensitization.
  • Biochemical Mediators:
    • CGRP: Key driver of migraine pain. Target for gepants & monoclonal antibodies.
    • Serotonin (5-HT): Drops during attack onset. Agonism of 5-HT1B/1D receptors (Triptans) causes cranial vasoconstriction and inhibits neuropeptide release.
    • Dopamine: Mediates premonitory symptoms (yawning, nausea, vomiting). Targeted by Metoclopramide.

🌀 Distinct Migraine Variants & Special Entities

Migraine Variant Clinical Presentation & Key Characteristics Genetics / Pathophysiology / Notes
Basilar / Brainstem Aura Visual aura occupying both visual fields + vertigo, dysarthria, tinnitus, ataxia, bilateral paresthesias, altered consciousness/coma. No motor weakness. Ischemia in vertebrobasilar territory. Predominantly in adolescents/children.
Familial Hemiplegic Migraine (FHM) Migraine with aura including motor weakness / hemiparesis that outlasts the headache. Autosomal dominant ion channelopathies:
• FHM1: CACNA1A (P/Q-type Ca2+ channel)
• FHM2: ATP1A2 (Na+/K+ ATPase)
• FHM3: SCN1A (Na+ channel)
Status Migrainosus Debilitating, continuous migraine attack lasting >72 hours. Requires IV hydration, IV Metoclopramide, NSAIDs, IV DHE or steroids. Discontinue overused oral analgesics.
Retinal Migraine Transient, fully reversible monocular visual disturbance (scintillations, scotoma, or blindness) in one eye. Transient ischemia of retinal or ciliary artery. Must rule out carotid dissection & GCA.
Ophthalmoplegic Migraine Recurrent headache + oculomotor nerve palsy (CN III with ptosis, dilated pupil). Now classified as Recurrent Painful Ophthalmoplegic Neuropathy. Seen in children. MRI shows CN III contrast enhancement.
HaNDL Syndrome Headache with Neurologic Deficits and CSF Lymphocytosis (pleocytosis up to 760 cells/mm³). Self-limiting (resolves in 7 weeks). Mimics encephalitis/meningitis. Responsive to corticosteroids.
Childhood Periodic Syndromes Cyclic vomiting, abdominal migraine, paroxysmal vertigo in young children. Precursor to classical migraine in adult life. Normal diagnostic workups.

🎗️ Tension-Type Headache (TTH)

The most common primary headache overall. Characterized as a "featureless" headache with bilateral, band-like tightness involving pericranial muscle tension. Lacks autonomic signs, nausea, or aggravation by routine physical activity.

  • Episodic TTH: <15 days per month. Treated with simple analgesics (Aspirin, Paracetamol, NSAIDs) or Paracetamol + Orphenadrine (muscle relaxant).
  • Chronic TTH: ≥15 days per month for >3 months.
  • ⭐ Board High-Yield: Amitriptyline is the only proven, established treatment for Chronic Tension-Type Headache.

🔥 Trigeminal Autonomic Cephalalgias (TACs) Overview

A group of severe unilateral headaches characterized by prominent cranial parasympathetic autonomic features (lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, ptosis, miosis). Mandatory Workup: Pituitary neuroimaging (MRI) and Pituitary function testing.

🩺 Secondary Headaches: Giant Cell Arteritis & Post-Traumatic Pain

🔍 Giant Cell (Temporal) Arteritis (GCA) ▼
  • Demographics: Almost exclusively in patients >50 years old.
  • Pathology: Granulomatous panarteritis of medium and large extracranial carotid arteries (superficial temporal artery).
  • Clinical Manifestations:
    • Scalp tenderness, palpable, nodular, tender, hardened superficial temporal artery.
    • Jaw claudication (high specificity for ischemia of maxillary artery).
    • Systemic signs: Fever, weight loss, night sweats. 50% associated with Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR) (aching of proximal limb girdle muscles).
  • Dreaded Complication: Ischemic optic neuropathy via ophthalmic artery occlusion ➔ Sudden irreversible blindness.
  • Diagnostics: Markedly elevated ESR (>50 mm/hr) and CRP. Temporal artery biopsy is gold standard.
  • Management: Immediate High-Dose Prednisone (40–60 mg/day PO). NEVER wait for biopsy results before starting steroids!
🚗 Post-Traumatic & Post-Infectious Headaches ▼

Post-Concussive Syndrome: Headache, dizziness, vertigo, fatigue, concentration impairment following head trauma. Neurologic exam and imaging are normal.

⚠️ Clinical Warning: In elderly patients with post-traumatic headache, always rule out Subdural Hematoma (SDH)! Age-related brain atrophy stretches bridging veins, predisposing them to rupture even after trivial trauma.

⚡ Trigeminal Neuralgia ("Tic Douloureux")

Characterized by paroxysmal, excruciating, stabbing, lancinating, or electric-shock-like pain confined to the distribution of one or more branches of the trigeminal nerve (CN V2 and CN V3 > CN V1).

  • Triggers: Minimal mechanical stimuli to facial trigger zones: chewing, talking, brushing teeth, washing face, exposure to cold wind. Lacks refractory period.
  • Etiology:
    • Classic / Idiopathic: Microvascular compression of CN V root entry zone by an aberrant vascular loop (most commonly Superior Cerebellar Artery).
    • Symptomatic: Multiple Sclerosis plaque, cerebellopontine angle tumor, basilar artery aneurysm.
  • First-Line Pharmacotherapy: Carbamazepine (600–1200 mg/day) — Effective in 80% of cases.
  • ⚠️ Pharmacogenomic Pitfall: Screen for HLA-B*1502 allele prior to starting Carbamazepine in Asian populations due to high risk of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN).
  • Surgical Management: Microvascular Decompression (Jannetta Procedure) or Gamma Knife Radiosurgery.

🦴 Spinal Anatomy & Pathophysiology of Axial Pain

Back pain affects 80% of adults during their lifetime, typically beginning around 30 years of age. Highly mobile spinal segments suffer the greatest wear and degenerative changes:

Mobility Order: Lumbar > Lumbosacral > Cervical

Disc Herniation Frequency: Lumbar (L5–S1 > L4–L5 > L3–L4) | Cervical (C5–C6 > C6–C7)

👴 Aging Changes in Spinal Structure ▼

Progressive dehydration and avascularity of the nucleus pulposus and fibrous annulus lead to disc height loss, osteophyte formation (spondylosis), facet joint arthropathy, and nerve root/spinal cord compression.

🏷️ Three Types of Spinal Pain ▼
  • Local Pain: Caused by irritation of nerve endings in dural membranes, periosteum, ligaments, or muscles. Aching, steady pain near affected vertebrae.
  • Referred Pain: Projected from spine to pelvic or abdominal visceral territories, or from visceral organs (pancreatitis, AAA) to the back.
  • Radicular Pain: Sharp, shooting, lancinating pain propagating down a specific dermatome due to compression or inflammation of a spinal nerve root. Increased by coughing, sneezing, or straining.

🩺 Physical Exam & Provocative Nerve Root Signs

Provocative Sign Technique / Maneuver Clinical Interpretation & Significance
Straight Leg Raising (SLR / Lasègue) With knee fully extended, flex hip until pain/resistance is elicited. Stretches L5/S1 sciatic nerve roots and hamstrings. Positive if pain radiates below knee at 30°–70°.
Crossed SLR (Fajersztajn Sign) Perform SLR on the UNAFFECTED leg. Highly Specific for lumbar disc herniation on the contralateral side.
Bragard Sign Perform SLR to threshold, lower leg slightly, then dorsiflex foot. Differentiates true nerve root pain from hamstring tightness.
Sicard Sign Perform SLR to threshold, then dorsiflex the great toe. Aggravates sciatic root tension.
Neri Sign Patient bends forward at waist. Flexion of the knee on the affected side to relieve sciatic tension.
Naffziger Sign Bilateral jugular vein compression or forced coughing/straining. Increases intraspinal CSF pressure, reproducing radicular pain.
Spurling Maneuver Neck extension, lateral rotation to affected side + axial compression. Narrows cervical intervertebral foramen; highly specific for Cervical Radiculopathy.

🚨 Urgent Surgical Emergencies & Distinct Spine Syndromes

🚨 Cauda Equina Syndrome (NEUROLOGIC EMERGENCY) ▼

Massive central disc herniation or lesion compressing the lumbosacral nerve roots of the cauda equina.

  • Classic Triad / Clinical Features: Low back pain + Bilateral leg weakness/areflexia + Saddle Anesthesia (numbness in perineal/perianal area) + Loss of bladder/bowel sphincter control (urinary retention / overflow incontinence).
  • Management: Emergency MRI and immediate surgical decompressive laminectomy within 24–48 hours to prevent permanent incontinence and paraplegia.
🛒 Lumbar Spinal Stenosis & Neurogenic Claudication ▼

Narrowing of spinal canal causing compression of cauda equina roots during standing or walking.

Hallmark Symptom: Neurogenic Claudication: Bilateral leg pain, numbness, or heaviness provoked by walking or standing erect. Relieved by sitting, stooping, or forward spinal flexion ("Shopping Cart Sign").

🦴 Congenital & Inflammatory Spine Conditions ▼
  • Spondylolysis: Bony defect or stress fracture in the vertebral pars interarticularis. Common in adolescent athletes. "Scotty dog with collar" on oblique X-ray.
  • Spondylolisthesis: Anterior slippage of one vertebral body over another.
  • Ankylosing Spondylitis: Inflammatory arthritis in young males (<40 yo). Low back pain improved by exercise, elevated ESR, HLA-B27 positive. Radiograph shows "Bamboo Spine".
  • Pott's Disease (Tuberculous Spondylitis): Hematogenous TB infection of vertebral bodies. Local destruction, gibbus deformity, cold abscess, elevated ESR, fever, night sweats.

📝 Case Presentation & Analysis (Dr. Guillermo Syllabus Case)

Patient Profile: 45/Female, Filipino, Right-handed

Chief Complaint: Recurrent headaches & neck/arm pain.

Headache Profile: 3-week history of recurrent unilateral (right frontotemporal) throbbing headache, 2-3x/week, lasting 4-24 hours, severity 7/10. Preceded 20 minutes prior by visual aura (shimmering zigzag lines). Associated with nausea, photophobia, phonophobia. Aggravated by routine physical activity; relieved by resting in a dark, quiet room.

Neck/Arm Profile: Posterior neck pain radiating to right shoulder, lateral arm, thumb, and index finger. Sharp shooting pain provoked by neck extension and right rotation. Associated with tingling/numbness.

Physical Exam Findings: Decreased sensation over right C6 dermatome (80% intact). Decreased right biceps and brachioradialis reflexes (1+). (+) Spurling sign. Normal strength, gait, Babinski (-), Hoffman (-).

🧠 Expert Diagnostic Breakdown:

  1. Diagnosis 1: Migraine with Aura — Fulfills ICHD-3 criteria (recurrent unilateral pulsating pain, moderate-severe, aggravated by routine activity, nausea, photo/phonophobia, preceded by typical visual aura).
  2. Diagnosis 2: Right C6 Cervical Radiculopathy — Secondary to cervical disc herniation or spondylosis. Supported by right C6 dermatomal sensory loss, diminished C6 reflexes (biceps/brachioradialis), and positive Spurling sign. No signs of myelopathy.

❓ Syllabus Review Questions Decoded

Q1: Which of the following is NOT considered a pain-sensitive structure of the head? ▼

A. Intracranial venous sinuses
B. Middle meningeal artery
C. Brain parenchyma

Correct Answer: C. Brain parenchyma

Rationale: Brain parenchyma, pia-arachnoid, and ependyma lack pain receptors entirely.

Q2: A patient presents with headache aggravated by walking upstairs and relieved by rest. Most likely diagnosis? ▼

A. Cluster headache
B. Migraine
C. Tension headache

Correct Answer: B. Migraine

Rationale: Aggravation by routine physical activity is a key diagnostic criterion for migraine.

Q3: Which medication is the ONLY proven treatment for chronic tension-type headache? ▼

A. Amitriptyline
B. Ibuprofen
C. Topiramate

Correct Answer: A. Amitriptyline

Rationale: Amitriptyline is the gold-standard proven prophylactic drug for Chronic TTH.

Q4: Which peptide is primarily targeted by newer migraine preventive medications? ▼

A. Substance P
B. Dopamine
C. CGRP

Correct Answer: C. CGRP

Rationale: Monoclonal antibodies (Erenumab, etc.) and Gepants selectively block CGRP ligands or receptors.

Q5: According to ICHD-3 criteria, migraine without aura lasts for? ▼

A. 30 minutes to 2 hours
B. 4 to 72 hours
C. 2 to 6 hours

Correct Answer: B. 4 to 72 hours

Rationale: Standard diagnostic duration for untreated or unsuccessfully treated migraine attacks.

  • Trigeminal Neuralgia: Carbamazepine (Check HLA-B*1502 in Asians).
  • SUNCT: Lamotrigine.
  • Temporal Arteritis: Immediate High-dose Prednisone (40-60 mg/day).

🚨 Red Flag High-Yield Highlights

  • Sudden Thunderclap Onset: Order non-contrast Cranial CT immediately to rule out Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH). If CT normal, perform Lumbar Puncture for Xanthochromia.
  • New Headache in >50 yo: Check ESR/CRP immediately (Temporal Arteritis).
  • Headache Worse When Supine / Early Morning: Think Elevated ICP or Intracranial Neoplasm.
  • Headache Worse Standing / Relieved Lying: Think Low CSF Pressure (Post-LP leak).
