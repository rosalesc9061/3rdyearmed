📖 Learning Objectives & Global Burden of Invasive Fungal Diseases

Lancet Infect Dis 2024 Data

Medical Mycology represents a critical component of internal medicine and infectious disease training for 3rd-year Philippine medical students and Philippine Physician Licensure Examination (PLE) examinees. Fungal infections range from benign superficial skin manifestations to life-threatening invasive organ diseases.

⭐ Core Learning Objectives
  • Define the etiology, classification (location, epidemiology, morphology) of fungal infections.
  • Characterize key clinical syndromes, risk factors, and diagnostic modalities for opportunistic, endemic, and deep organ fungal pathogens.
  • Master antifungal pharmacology: mechanisms of action, spectrum, indications, adverse reactions, and contraindications.
  • Distinguish high-yield differential diagnoses (e.g., Candida vs. Aspergillus vs. Cryptococcus vs. Mucorales).

🌳 Classification System Breakdown

📍 By Anatomic Location

  • Mucocutaneous: Involves skin, mucosa, nails. Causes high morbidity and temporary physical disability, but rarely fatal (prognosis good if treated).
  • Deep Organ: Affects internal organs (lungs, brain, heart, kidneys). Highly life-threatening with high mortality in immunocompromised hosts.

🌐 By Epidemiology

  • Endemic Mycoses: Environmentally acquired, non-commensal fungi (e.g., Histoplasma, Coccidioides). Self-limited in healthy hosts; severe/disseminated in immunosuppressed.
  • Opportunistic Mycoses: Body commensals (e.g., Candida) or ubiquitous environmental organisms (e.g., Aspergillus) that invade when immune defenses fail.

🔬 By Morphology

  • Yeasts: Round, single cells reproducing by budding (e.g., Cryptococcus, Candida).
  • Molds: Filamentous hyphae growth at both room temp and tissue (e.g., Aspergillus, Mucorales).
  • Dimorphic: Yeasts/spheres in tissue (37°C), molds/hyphae at room temp (25°C).

⚖️ Figure 1: Yeast vs. Mold Structural Comparison

Plain-language note: Yeasts grow as single rounded budding cells, whereas molds grow as branching filamentous strands (hyphae). Candida albicans can exist as both, EXCEPT C. glabrata and C. auris which exist strictly as yeasts in tissue.

🍄 Mold (Filamentous) Fungal Infections

High Mortality Pathogens

1. Aspergillosis (Aspergillus fumigatus, A. flavus, A. terreus, A. niger, A. nidulans)

Aspergillus species are ubiquitous molds found in soil and decaying vegetation. Inhalation of conidia leads to a spectrum of illnesses depending on host immune status.

Clinical Syndromes & Disease Types

  • Invasive Pulmonary Aspergillosis: Angioinvasive, necrotizing pneumonia in severely immunocompromised hosts. High untreated mortality (100%).
  • Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis (CPA): Cavitary lesions or fungal balls (aspergilloma) in pre-existing lung cavities (e.g., old TB).
  • Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis (ABPA): Hypersensitivity response in asthmatics and cystic fibrosis patients ("fungal asthma").
  • Fungal Keratitis: Direct corneal inoculation causing ulcerative keratitis.

Risk Factors & Diagnostics

  • Risk Factors: Profound neutropenia (ANC < 200), prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, post-HSCT, post-influenza, severe COVID-19 ("black fungus" co-infection), BTK inhibitors.
  • Tissue Histology: Septate hyphae with acute-angle (45°) branching.
  • Biomarkers: Serum/BAL Galactomannan antigen; Beta-D-Glucan (BDG) (available in PH, non-specific); IgE elevated in ABPA.
  • Blood Culture: Almost ALWAYS negative!
💡 Diagnostic Imaging & Clinical Pearl
Chest CT in Invasive Aspergillosis shows the classic "Halo Sign" (a nodule surrounded by ground-glass opacity representing alveolar hemorrhage/inflammation around an ischemic infarct) followed later by the "Air Crescent Sign" during neutrophil recovery.

2. Mucormycosis (Rhizopus, Rhizomucor, Mucor, Cunninghamella, Lichtheimia)

A group of rapidly progressive, life-threatening angioinvasive infections caused by fungi of the order Mucorales. Causes relentless tissue necrosis and vascular thrombosis.

Clinical Syndromes

  • Rhinocerebral / Rhino-orbital-cerebral: Classic triad of facial pain, bloody nasal discharge, and black necrotic eschar on the hard palate or nasal mucosa. Rapidly invades orbit and brain.
  • Pulmonary & Cutaneous: Pneumonia in neutropenic patients; necrotizing skin infections after trauma/direct inoculation (e.g., tornado victims).

Key Risk Factors & Histology

  • Pathognomonic Risk Factor: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) and uncontrolled Diabetes Mellitus (acidic pH frees iron, enhancing fungal growth). Also neutropenia and HSCT.
  • Histopathology: Broad, ribbon-like, NON-SEPTATE (aseptate) hyphae with wide-angle (90°) right-angle branching.
  • Biomarkers: NEGATIVE! Beta-D-Glucan and Galactomannan do NOT detect Mucorales!
⚠️ Diagnostic Pitfall: Aspergillus vs. Mucorales
Never confuse tissue morphology! Aspergillus has narrow, septate hyphae branching at acute angles (45°). Mucorales has broad, aseptate (ribbon-like) hyphae branching at wide right angles (90°). Voriconazole works against Aspergillus but has NO activity against Mucorales!

3. Other Molds: Fusariosis, Scedosporiosis, Phaeohyphomycosis & Dermatophytosis

Organism Clinical Presentation Unique Key Feature Treatment Options
Fusariosis
(Fusarium solani)		 Pneumonia, keratitis, disseminated disease with painful necrotic skin lesions. One of the few molds recovered from blood culture! Septate hyphae. BDG (+), GM (+). Highly resistant! Lipid AmB, Voriconazole, Posaconazole. Investigational: Fosmanogepix, Olorofilm.
Scedosporiosis
(S. apiospermum)		 Pneumonia or disseminated infection in neutropenic/HSCT patients. Acute-angle septate hyphae. BDG positive. Resistant to Amphotericin B! Voriconazole is preferred agent.
Phaeohyphomycosis Sinopulmonary, cutaneous, or CNS infection/meningitis in healthy or immunocompromised. Dematiaceous (pigmented) fungi. Cell walls appear golden/brown on H&E; Fontana-Masson stains melanin. Itraconazole, Voriconazole, or Posaconazole + surgical debridement.
Dermatophytosis Tinea corporis, tinea pedis, tinea capitis, onychomycosis. Skin scrapings show chains of arthrospores on KOH preparation. Topical azoles, Terbinafine, Griseofulvin.
Eumycetoma
(Madurella mycetomatis)		 Chronic subcutaneous nodular lesions with draining sinus tracts. Discharges visible colored grains/granules composed of fungal hyphae. Long-term Itraconazole + surgical excision.

🍞 Yeast Fungal Infections

Candida & Cryptococcus

1. Candidiasis (Candida albicans, C. glabrata, C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis, C. auris)

Candida species are ubiquitous normal human commensals (GI tract, skin, mucosa). Disease occurs when host mucosal barriers or immunity are breached.

👄 Mucosal Candidiasis

  • Oropharyngeal (Thrush): Painful white plaque-like lesions on erythematous mucosa, easily scraped off revealing bleeding base. Common in AIDS, inhaled steroids, denture wearers.
  • Esophageal Candidiasis: Dysphagia, odynophagia, chest pain. AIDS-defining illness. Requires systemic therapy!
  • Vulvovaginal (VVC): Thick white "cottage-cheese" discharge, intense pruritus. Associated with antibiotic use, diabetes, pregnancy. Not an STI!

🩸 Invasive Candidiasis & Candidemia

  • Candidemia: Presence of Candida in blood. Most common syndrome in critically ill ICU patients. Does not require immunocompromise!
  • Predisposing Factors: Central venous/IV catheters, TPN (hyperalimentation), broad-spectrum antibiotics, abdominal surgery/peritonitis, severe burns, CARD9 or MPO deficiency.
  • Disseminated End-Organ Infection: Hepatosplenic candidiasis, renal microabscesses, Candida Endophthalmitis (requires fundoscopy: off-white chorioretinal lesions with vitreous haze).
⭐ CHROMagar Identification & Yeast-Only Species
  • CHROMagar Isolate Colors: C. glabrata = Purple; C. tropicalis = Navy Blue; C. auris = White.
  • Morphology Rule: C. albicans forms both yeasts and pseudohyphae/hyphae in tissue. EXCEPTIONS: C. glabrata and C. auris form ONLY YEASTS in tissue!
  • Multidrug-Resistant Alert: Candida auris is an emerging global healthcare threat resistant to multiple antifungal classes!
⚠️ Clinical Pitfall: Specimen Colonization vs. Infection
Isolation of Candida from sputum, urine, or peritoneal drains frequently represents mere colonization rather than true invasive disease. Do NOT initiate systemic antifungal therapy based solely on sputum or un-catheterized urine cultures without clinical evidence of invasion!

2. Cryptococcosis (Cryptococcus neoformans, C. gattii)

In encapsulated yeast acquired via inhalation of dried pigeon droppings (C. neoformans) or eucalyptus trees (C. gattii). Declared a Critical Priority Pathogen by WHO in 2022.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Subacute Meningoencephalitis: Headache, fever, lethargy, neck rigidity, altered mental status. Most common complication = increased intracranial pressure (ICP) and hydrocephalus.
  • Pulmonary Cryptococcosis: Asymptomatic nodule to acute respiratory distress.
  • Target Population: AIDS patients with CD4 count < 100 cells/µL; long-term steroid users, sarcoidosis, BTK or JAK inhibitor therapy.

Diagnostic Modalities

  • CSF India Ink Stain: Shows round yeast cells with a wide, clear polysaccharide capsule halo.
  • Cryptococcal Antigen (CrAg): Highly sensitive (>95%) and specific test in serum and CSF.
  • Histopathology Stains: Mucicarmine stain (stains capsule red), GMS, PAS.

🌿 Endemic Dimorphic Fungi

Thermal Dimorphism

Endemic dimorphic fungi exist in nature as filamentous molds at room temperature (25°C) and convert to unicellular yeasts or spherules in host tissue at body temperature (37°C). They are environmentally acquired and can cause self-limited disease in healthy individuals or severe disseminated disease in immunocompromised hosts.

Disease & Pathogen Geographic / Environmental Niche Key Clinical Presentation Histopathology & Diagnostic Clues
Histoplasmosis
(Histoplasma capsulatum)		 Soil enriched with bird/bat guano (caves, chicken coops). Found in PH. Self-limited flu-like pneumonia in healthy. Disseminated in AIDS (fever, hepatosplenomegaly, mucocutaneous ulcers, pancytopenia). Small intracellular yeasts with NARROW-BASED BUDDING inside macrophages. Urine/Serum Antigen (+).
Blastomycosis
(Blastomyces dermatitidis)		 Moist soil, decaying wood in river basins (North America, Africa, Asia). Pneumonia (69-93%). Disseminated disease (15-48%) to skin (verrucous lesions), bone, genitourinary tract. Large single yeast with BROAD-BASED BUDDING (mother-daughter cell connection).
Coccidioidomycosis
(Coccidioides immitis)		 Arid desert soil ("Valley Fever", SW USA, Mexico). Acute pneumonia, erythema nodosum. Disseminated to meninges, bone, skin in immunocompromised. Large thick-walled SPHERULES containing ENDOSPORES (no budding yeasts!).
Paracoccidioidomycosis
(P. brasiliensis)		 Latin American rain forests / rural agricultural regions. Chronic pulmonary disease, painful ulcerative oral/mucosal lesions, lymphadenopathy. Multiple budding yeast around a central mother cell forming a "Steering-Wheel" or "Mariner's Wheel" pattern.
Sporotrichosis
(Sporothrix schenckii)		 Environmentally acquired (rose bushes, sphagnum moss - "Rose gardener's disease"). Direct inoculation causes Lymphocutaneous infection with ASCENDING LYMPHANGITIS (linear subcutaneous nodules). Cigar-shaped yeasts surrounded by eosinophilic radiation (asteroid body).
Talaromycosis
(Talaromyces marneffei)		 Southeast Asia (associated with bamboo rats). Severe in AIDS. Fever, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, skin lesions with central umbilication. Unicellular yeast-like cells that divide by TRANSVERSE SEPTATION (fission) rather than budding.
🧠 Exam Tip: General Management Rule for Endemic Mycoses
  • Mild to Moderate Disease in Immunocompetent: Oral Itraconazole (200 mg/day for 6–12 months).
  • Severe, Disseminated, or CNS Infection (or Immunocompromised): Induction with Lipid Amphotericin B for 2–6 weeks, followed by consolidation with oral Itraconazole for at least 12 months.

🔬 Pneumocystosis & Antifungal Host Defense

Immunology & Opportunistic PCP

1. Pneumocystis Pneumonia (PCP) (Pneumocystis jirovecii)

An atypical opportunistic fungal pathogen that lacks ergosterol in its cell membrane and cannot be cultured on standard laboratory media.

Clinical Features & Risk Factors

  • Classic Triad: Subacute onset (1–2 weeks) of fever, nonproductive cough, and progressive dyspnea/hypoxemia.
  • High-Risk Patient Groups: HIV/AIDS patients with CD4 count < 200 cells/µL (accounts for 80% of cases; >90% if CD4 < 100); patients on high-dose prolonged steroids or chemotherapy.
  • Imaging (Chest X-ray / CT): Symmetric, bilateral perihilar interstitial or ground-glass alveolar infiltrates ("butterfly pattern"). Can mimic ARDS.

Diagnosis & Treatment Choice

  • Gold Standard Diagnosis: Histopathologic examination of BAL fluid or induced sputum using GMS stain (shows darkly stained round/cup-shaped cysts) or immunofluorescence. Unculturable!
  • FIRST-LINE TREATMENT: High-dose Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) (TMP 5 mg/kg + SMX 25 mg/kg q6-8h PO/IV). The ONLY fungal infection treated primarily with an antibiotic!
  • Adjunctive Therapy: Corticosteroids (Prednisone) indicated if PaO₂ < 70 mmHg or A-a gradient ≥ 35 mmHg to reduce pulmonary inflammation and mortality.

2. Host Defense Mechanisms Against Fungal Pathogens

The human immune system employs specialized lineage-specific cellular and molecular pathways depending on the fungal morphology and anatomic site of entry.

🛡️ Th17 Cell Pathway

Primary Function: Protection against Mucosal Candida Invasion.

  • Differentiating Factors: STAT3 promotes differentiation via RORγt.
  • Cytokines Released: IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22.
  • Mechanism: Bind IL-17RA/RC on epithelial cells signaling via ACT1 to secrete antimicrobial peptides. IL-22 stimulates epithelial proliferation & repair.

⚡ CXCR2+ Neutrophil Pathway

Primary Function: Defense against Invasive Molds (Aspergillus) in Lungs.

  • Recruitment: CXCR2+ neutrophils recruited from blood to pulmonary tissue.
  • Mechanism: Assembly of the 6-subunit NADPH Oxidase complex generating superoxide anion radicals that mediate direct extracellular and intracellular fungal killing.

🧫 Th1 Cell & Macrophage Pathway

Primary Function: Defense against Intramacrophagic Pathogens (Histoplasma, Cryptococcus, PCP).

  • Signal Cascade: Macrophages release IL-12 → activates STAT4 on T-cells → secretion of IFN-γ.
  • Effector Action: IFN-γ binds macrophages → activates STAT1 → enables intramacrophagic fungal killing. TNF-α and GM-CSF are essential co-factors.

🔬 Histopathologic Stains Summary Table

Stain Name Target Component / Reaction Diagnostic Appearance & Target Fungi
Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS) Stains fungal cell wall carbohydrates black/dark brown. Darkly outlined fungal elements; best for Pneumocystis cysts, Aspergillus, Mucorales.
Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) Oxidizes cell wall polysaccharides to stain pink/bright magenta. Bright magenta hyphae and yeasts against background tissue.
India Ink Stain Negative staining technique (particles excluded by capsule). Clear halo surrounding round encapsulated yeast in CSF; diagnostic for Cryptococcus neoformans.
Gram Stain Crystal violet retention by fungal cell wall. Candida species stain Gram-positive (dark blue/purple oval budding yeast/pseudohyphae).
Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) General tissue stain; highlights host inflammation. Demonstrates tissue granulomas, angioinvasion, necrosis. Mucorales hyphae appear red/pink.
Mucicarmine Stain Stains acidic mucopolysaccharides red/pink. Specifically stains the mucopolysaccharide capsule of Cryptococcus bright rose red.
Calcofluor White Fluorescent dye binding chitin and cellulose in fungal walls. Apple-green fluorescence under UV light microscopy in fluid samples.

💊 Antifungal Pharmacology

Mechanisms & High-Yield Specs

🎯 Figure 2: Antifungal Drug Sites of Action

Drug Class & Agent Mechanism of Action Clinical Indications Key Adverse Effects & Pearls
Amphotericin B
(Deoxycholate, L-AmB, ABLC)		 Binds directly to ergosterol in fungal membrane, forming pores → ion leakage & osmotic cell lysis (Fungicidal). Broadest spectrum! Preferred for Mucormycosis, Cryptococcal meningitis induction, severe dimorphic mycoses. Nephrotoxicity, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, infusion reactions (fever, chills). Liposomal AmB (L-AmB) is less nephrotoxic with better CNS penetration.
Fluconazole Inhibits lanosterol 14α-demethylase, blocking ergosterol synthesis (Fungistatic). Mucosal candidiasis, Cryptococcal maintenance, Coccidioidal meningitis. Excellent CSF penetration. CYP450 inhibitor. Pregnancy Risk: associated with miscarriage and stillbirth in 1st/2nd trimester. Ineffective vs. Aspergillus & Mucorales!
Voriconazole Inhibits 14α-demethylase (broader spectrum azole). FIRST-LINE DRUG FOR INVASIVE ASPERGILLOSIS! Active vs. Scedosporium, Fusarium, Candida. Transient visual disturbances (photopsia/hallucinations), rash, hepatotoxicity. NO activity against Mucorales!
Posaconazole & Isavuconazole Inhibits 14α-demethylase with extended binding site. Active against BOTH Aspergillus AND Mucorales! Posaconazole used for prophylaxis in neutropenic leukemia/HSCT. Isavuconazole has lower toxicity, fewer drug interactions, and shorter QTc interval compared to Voriconazole.
Itraconazole Inhibits 14α-demethylase. First-line for mild-moderate Endemic Dimorphic Mycoses (Histoplasmosis, Blastomycosis, Sporotrichosis), ABPA. Poor CNS penetration. Absorption requires acidic gastric pH (impaired by PPIs/H2 blockers). Strong CYP3A4 inhibitor.
Echinocandins
(Caspofungin, Micafungin, Anidulafungin, Rezafungin)		 Inhibits β-1,3-glucan synthase, disrupting fungal cell wall synthesis (Fungicidal vs. Candida, static vs. Aspergillus). First-line for invasive Candidiasis & Candidemia in critically ill/neutropenic patients. Salvage for Aspergillosis. Safest antifungal class! Minimal drug interactions and no renal toxicity. Ineffective against Cryptococcus & Mucorales!
Flucytosine (5-FC) Intrafungal conversion to 5-FU by cytosine deaminase → inhibits fungal DNA & RNA synthesis. Combination therapy with AmB for Cryptococcal Meningitis induction. High CSF penetration. NEVER USE AS MONOTHERAPY (rapid resistance!). Requires CBC monitoring due to bone marrow suppression (pancytopenia) and hepatotoxicity.
Terbinafine & Griseofulvin Terbinafine inhibits squalene epoxidase. Griseofulvin disrupts mitotic spindle microtubule function. Dermatophytosis, tinea capitis, onychomycosis. Terbinafine is more effective than Griseofulvin. Monitor LFTs (risk of hepatotoxicity with oral Terbinafine).
Ibrexafungerp Novel oral triterpenoid glucan synthase inhibitor. Approved for Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC) and treatment of recurrent VVC. Oral alternative to echinocandins for Candida species.

⚡ Ultra-Condensed PLE Cram Sheet

10-Minute Rapid Review

🎯 "If You See X → Think Y" Rapid Associations

  • Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) + Black facial eschar → Mucormycosis (Rhizopus)
  • 45° acute-angle septate hyphae + Neutropenia → Aspergillus fumigatus
  • 90° broad ribbon-like non-septate hyphae → Mucorales
  • Pigeon droppings + CSF India ink positive yeast halo → Cryptococcus neoformans
  • CD4 < 200 + Bilateral perihilar interstitial infiltrates → Pneumocystis jirovecii (PCP)
  • Caves / Bat guano + intracellular yeast in macrophages → Histoplasma capsulatum
  • Rose gardener + ascending nodular lymphangitis → Sporothrix schenckii
  • Southwest US Desert + spherule containing endospores → Coccidioides immitis
  • Broad-based budding yeast + verrucous skin lesions → Blastomyces dermatitidis
  • Mariner's / Steering-wheel budding pattern → Paracoccidioides brasiliensis
  • Sputum or Urine isolate of Candida → Colonization (Do NOT treat!)
  • Mold recovered directly from Blood Culture → Fusarium solani

💊 Antifungal Drug Emergency Quick-Ref

  • First-line Invasive Aspergillosis: Voriconazole
  • First-line Mucormycosis: Lipid Amphotericin B + Surgical Debridement
  • First-line PCP Pneumonia: High-dose TMP-SMX (+ Prednisone if hypoxic)
  • First-line Cryptococcal Meningitis Induction: Amphotericin B + Flucytosine
  • First-line Candidemia in ICU: Echinocandin (Caspofungin / Micafungin)
  • First-line Mild Endemic Mycoses: Oral Itraconazole
  • First-line Vulvovaginal Candidiasis single-dose: Oral Fluconazole 150 mg
  • Drug requiring CBC monitoring: Flucytosine (bone marrow suppression)
  • Safest class with no renal toxicity: Echinocandins
  • Azole covering BOTH Aspergillus & Mucorales: Isavuconazole / Posaconazole
⭐ High-Yield Biomarker & Diagnostic Summary
  • Beta-D-Glucan (BDG): Detects Candida, Aspergillus, Fusarium, PCP, Histoplasma. NEGATIVE in Mucorales and Cryptococcus!
  • Galactomannan (GM): Specifically detects Aspergillus (BAL > Serum) and Fusarium.
  • Cryptococcal Antigen (CrAg): Highly sensitive and specific for Cryptococcus in serum and CSF.
