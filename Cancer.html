📋 Core Objectives & High-Yield Summary FOUNDATIONAL

Cancer therapy relies on three pillars: Surgery (local), Radiotherapy (local), and Chemotherapy / Systemic Therapy (systemic)[cite: 1]. Systemic treatments target cells body-wide and are broadly divided into cytotoxic agents, targeted biologicals, and immunotherapies[cite: 1]. Management hinges on balancing tumor destruction against host toxicity, guided heavily by functional baseline metrics like the ECOG Performance Status[cite: 1].

💡 Learning Objectives
  • Be familiar with the different types of systemic treatments (cytotoxic, targeted, biologic/immunotherapy)[cite: 1].
  • Recognize general and organ-specific adverse effects of systemic treatment[cite: 1].
  • Master the clinical approach, prevention, and management of acute and long-term toxicities[cite: 1].

🎯 Goals of Cancer Treatment
  • Potentially Cure: Feasible ONLY in early-stage or locally advanced disease[cite: 1].
  • Prolong Survival: Main goal in metastatic setting where cure is unrealistic, while preserving Quality of Life (QoL) and functional status[cite: 1].
  • Relieve Symptoms (Palliation): Primary objective when chances of prolonging survival are low[cite: 1].
⏳ Bone Marrow & Recovery Kinetics

Short-term toxicities are usually manageable at home, but severe complications require hospitalization[cite: 1].

⭐ Recovery Timeline

Patients receiving 1st-line therapy recover faster than heavily pretreated patients (≥2 lines of chemo)[cite: 1]. Bone marrow reserve changes permanently over multiple lines[cite: 1]. In clinical practice, full marrow recovery takes 3 to 6 months after completing therapy[cite: 1].

⚙️ Treatment Modalities Breakdown

🔪 Surgery

LOCAL TREATMENT

Removal of tumor & surrounding normal tissue[cite: 1]. Oldest & most invasive modality[cite: 1]. Provides the best chance of cure for most solid tumors[cite: 1].

⚡ Radiotherapy

LOCAL TREATMENT

Uses high-energy particles/waves (X-rays, gamma rays, electrons, protons)[cite: 1]. Mechanisms: induces double & single-strand DNA breaks[cite: 1].

💊 Chemotherapy

SYSTEMIC TREATMENT

Cytotoxic drugs targeting fast-dividing cells[cite: 1]. Prevents angiogenesis, stops division, causes apoptosis, creates non-dividing cells[cite: 1].

Table 2: Clinical Settings of Chemotherapy Administration
Setting Timing relative to Surgery Primary Therapeutic Goal Target Clinical Population
Adjuvant Given AFTER complete surgical resection. Eradicate microscopic residual disease; improve Disease-Free Survival (DFS) & Overall Survival (OS); prevent recurrence. Resected solid tumors (e.g., post-op Breast, Colorectal cancer).
Neoadjuvant Given BEFORE surgical intervention. Downsize/downstage tumor to enable less extensive surgery; treat micrometastases; achieve Complete Pathologic Response (pCR). Locally advanced unresectable/large tumors (e.g., Breast, Head & Neck).
Palliative No surgery planned (metastatic setting). Symptom management & control, quality of life maintenance without expectation of cure. Metastatic disease (1st-line, 2nd-line, etc. provided ECOG ≤ 2).
Induction Initial primary treatment regimen. Induce rapid hematologic/clinical remission; shrink massive bulky tumor burdens. Acute Leukemias, massive Head & Neck cancers.
Consolidation / Intensification Given after remission is achieved. Sustain remission, eliminate lingering undetectable malignant clones. Acute leukemias, hematologic malignancies (adjuvant setting).
Maintenance Given long-term after consolidation. Lower-dose therapy to assist in prolonging remission duration. Certain cancers (e.g., Acute Leukemias).
🧪 Standard Combination Chemotherapy Protocols & Biomodulation

Chemotherapy is frequently administered in multi-drug protocols (regimens) rather than single agents to maximize cell kill and prevent resistance.

FAC Protocol (Breast)

  • F: 5-Fluorouracil
  • A: Adriamycin (Doxorubicin)
  • C: Cyclophosphamide

AIM Protocol (Sarcoma)

  • A: Adriamycin (Doxorubicin)
  • I: Ifosfamide
  • M: Mesna (uroprotective)

FOLFOX Protocol (Colorectal)

  • FOL: Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)
  • F: 5-Fluorouracil
  • OX: Oxaliplatin
💡 High-Yield Biomodulator Concept: Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)

Folinic acid is NOT a cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agent. It functions as a biomodulator that stabilizes the binding of 5-FU to thymidylate synthase, enhancing its efficacy while reducing toxic side effects.

🧠 Routes of Administration

Beyond standard IV and Oral routes: Intrathecal chemo is used for CNS tumors/prophylaxis. Intraperitoneal chemo is used for carcinomatosis originating from gynecological, colorectal, and hepatobiliary malignancies.

🔄 The Cell Cycle & Chemotherapy Targets

Cytotoxic chemotherapy works primarily by destroying cells actively progressing through the cell cycle[cite: 1].

Figure 1: Cell Cycle Specificity of Cytotoxic Antineoplastic Agents.
Clinical Significance: Cell cycle-specific drugs are schedule-dependent (given in small, repetitive doses to catch cells as they enter the sensitive phase). Cell cycle non-specific drugs are dose-dependent (given in large bolus doses).

🩸 1. Myelosuppression & Hematologic Toxicity

Myelosuppression is the first known and most frequent side effect of cytotoxic chemotherapy. Neutropenia and Thrombocytopenia dominate clinical management.

⭐ High-Yield Neutropenia Rules & Kinetics
  • ANC Cutoff to Proceed: Chemo proceeds ONLY if ANC > 1500 /µL. If ANC ≤ 1500 /µL, DEFER chemo!
  • Nadir Neutrophil Count: Lowest point occurs around Day 10 post-chemo; recovery begins ~Day 14 and completes by Day 21. (Reason for 21–28 day chemo cycles!)
  • Heavily Pretreated Patients: Nadir occurs earlier (<7–10 days) and recovery takes longer (>21 days). Repeat CBC at 1 week post-chemo.
  • Neutropenic Infection: Assumed for ANY fever when ANC < 1000 /µL. Severe risk when ANC < 500 /µL. Gram-negative & S. aureus common; Fungal (Candida, Aspergillus, Mucor) in severe nadir.
💡 Neutropenia Management & Hematopoietic Growth Factors
  • Recombinant G-CSF:
    • Short-acting: Filgrastim, Lenograstim (given q24h; Filgrastim available in PH).
    • Long-acting: Pegfilgrastim, Lipegfilgrastim (covers 1 week; Pegfilgrastim available in PH).
  • Target ANC: Discontinue G-CSF once ANC > 3,000 /µL. Proceed with chemo when ANC > 1,500 /µL.
  • GM-CSF: Sargramostim, Molgramostim, Regramostim (none available in PH).
  • Prophylactic Antibiotics: Controversial; not routinely advocated in afebrile neutropenia.
🩸 Thrombocytopenia Thresholds & Management

Defined as Platelet Count < 100,000 /µL. Nadir observed at 10–14 days, recovery completed by 21 days.

  • Hemorrhage Risk: Significantly increases when Platelets ≤ 50,000 /µL.
  • Spontaneous / Intracranial Bleeding Risk: High risk when Platelets drop to < 10,000 /µL.
  • Strict Indications for Prophylactic Platelet Transfusion:
    1. All patients with Platelets ≤ 10,000 /µL (prevents spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage).
    2. Patients with Platelets < 50,000 /µL scheduled for a surgical procedure.
  • Delayed/Cumulative Thrombocytopenia Agents: Nitrosoureas, Mitomycin C, Busulfan, Melphalan, Lenalidomide.
  • Thrombopoietin Mimetics: Romiplostim & Eltrombopag (Eltrombopag available in PH as injection).
🤮 2. Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea and Vomiting (CINV)

CINV originates from two key anatomic centers: the Chemoreceptor Trigger Zone (CTZ) in the area postrema (caudal 4th ventricle, outside BBB) and the Vomiting Center in the lateral reticular formation of the medulla.

Table 4: Three Clinical Syndromes of CINV
Syndrome Onset & Timing Pathophysiology & Characteristics Primary Management / Prophylaxis
Acute CINV Develops within 24 hours post-chemotherapy. Direct emetogenic drug activation of CTZ & gut serotonin release. Pre-medication 30 mins prior to chemo (5-HT3 ant + Steroid ± NK1 ant).
Delayed CINV Develops > 24 hours post-chemotherapy. Substance P & central neurokinin-1 pathway activation. Home outpatient antiemetic regimens (e.g., Dexamethasone Days 2–4).
Anticipatory CINV Occurs PRIOR to chemotherapy administration. Conditioned response triggered by sights, smells, or presence of hospital staff. Systematic desensitization & hypnosis; Anxiolytics.

💡 High-Yield 5-HT3 Antagonist Pearl: Palonosetron

Palonosetron is the longest-acting 5-HT3 receptor antagonist (half-life ~40 hours) with high binding affinity. It is specifically indicated for patients receiving multi-day chemotherapy regimens (>2 days). Short-acting agents include Ondansetron, Granisetron, and Tropisetron.

💩 Diarrhea & Constipation

Chemo-Induced Diarrhea: >3 unformed stools/24h due to acute intestinal mucosal damage. Common causes: 5-FU, Capecitabine (oral 5-FU prodrug), and Irinotecan. Management: Oral hydration, diet modification, Loperamide. Hospitalize if severe dehydration/electrolyte imbalance occurs.

Chemo-Induced Constipation: Caused by Opioids (morphine) and Vinca Alkaloids (Vincristine, Vinblastine). Management: Non-pharmacologic first (fiber, fluids, exercise), followed by laxatives, enemas, suppositories.

💇 Alopecia & Cancer-Related Fatigue (CRF)

Alopecia: Begins 2–4 weeks post-treatment; hair regrows 3–6 months post-chemo. Most implicated class: Anthracyclines (Doxorubicin). Prevention: Scalp cooling / hypothermia (reduces scalp blood flow & drug uptake). Treatment: Topical minoxidil & α-tocopherol.

Cancer-Related Fatigue (CRF): Multifactorial (anemia, mood, altered energy metabolism, anorexia). Management: Exercise, yoga, and counseling are strongly effective.

🫀 1. Cardiovascular Toxicity

Cardiovascular toxicity includes dysrhythmias, ischemia, cardiomyopathic CHF, pericardial disease, and peripheral vascular disease.

Anthracyclines (Doxorubicin)

Most frequent cardiotoxic chemotherapeutic agent. Mechanism: Chemical free-radical damage causing dose-dependent, irreversible myocardial damage. Pathognomonic biopsy feature: Myofibrillar dropout.

⭐ Cumulative Dose Limit

5% of patients develop CHF at a cumulative Doxorubicin dose of >450–550 mg/m². Standard protocols (e.g., FAC) keep cumulative doses below 300 mg/m² to prevent toxicity. Cofactors (age, pre-existing heart disease, HTN, DM, thoracic radiation) lower this threshold.

Cardioprotection & Monitoring

Dexrazoxane: Intracellular iron chelator that limits anthracycline toxicity (not available in PH). Less toxic formulations: Liposomal Doxorubicin, Epirubicin, Idarubicin.

💡 Cardiac Surveillance

Baseline 2D Echocardiography or MUGA scan prior to initiating therapy, repeated after completion or every 3–4 cycles. Management of toxicity: Discontinue drug, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, diuretics.

🫁 2. Pulmonary Toxicity

Presents as interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis, hypersensitivity, or non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

Bleomycin-Induced Pneumonitis

Bleomycin generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) causing alveolar epithelial and endothelial cell injury. Toxicity is dose-related and dose-limiting.

  • Early Sign: Non-productive cough with or without fever.
  • Radiograph: Diffuse interstitial ground-glass appearance throughout both lobes (worse in lower lobes).
  • Monitoring: Pulmonary Function Test (PFT) with DLCO (Diffusion capacity of lungs for carbon monoxide) is the most sensitive metric.

Management of Chemo Pneumonitis

💡 Corticosteroid Therapy

1. Discontinue offending agent immediately.
2. High-dose oral Prednisone 1 mg/kg with slow taper.
3. Require support: PPI for GI protection, glucose monitoring, infection monitoring, bone protection.

🧠 3. Neurological Dysfunction

Vinca Alkaloids (Vincristine, Vinblastine)

Produces characteristic dose-related "stocking-glove" peripheral neuropathy. Sensorimotor polyneuropathy with loss of deep tendon reflexes (DTRs), initial loss of pain/temperature, jaw claudication, paralytic ileus, cranial nerve palsies.

Cisplatin (Ototoxicity & Neuropathy)

Enters cochlea and generates ROS, destroying outer hair cells & causing permanent hearing loss and sensorimotor neuropathy (doses >400 mg/m²). Requires baseline & post-treatment audiometry! Substitute Carboplatin if renal failure or hearing loss occurs.

5-FU & Capecitabine

Causes Palmar-Plantar Erythrodysesthesia (Hand-Foot Syndrome): painful erythema, swelling, dysesthesias, and desquamation of palms and soles. Abates upon drug cessation.

Methotrexate & "Chemo Brain"

High-dose Methotrexate (>3 g/m²) causes acute reversible/irreversible leukoencephalopathy (somnolence, confusion). "Chemo Brain" refers to long-term neurocognitive decline (memory, attention, speed) post-chemo.

🧪 Organ Toxicities: Renal, Bladder & Hepatic
  • Cisplatin Nephrotoxicity: Tubular toxicity; requires aggressive pre- and post-hydration.
  • Cyclophosphamide & Ifosfamide Hemorrhagic Cystitis: Hepatic cleavage yields acrolein, causing severe bladder inflammation. Prevented using Mesna (free-radical scavenger) and hydration. (Ifosfamide requires mandatory Mesna!) Hemorrhagic cystitis predisposes to bladder cancer.
  • Hepatic Veno-Occlusive Disease (SOS): Caused by high-dose chemo or long-term Methotrexate. Damage to liver sinusoidal capillaries presents with anicteric ascites, elevated ALP, and hepatosplenomegaly.
🦷 Oral, Endocrine & Second Malignancies
  • Bisphosphonates & Osteonecrosis of the Jaw (ONJ): Used for bone metastases. Requires mandatory dental evaluation prior to treatment!
  • Reproductive Toxicity: Alkylators cause premature ovarian failure, premature menopause, and long-term azoospermia (Cyclophosphamide > 9 g/m²).
  • Raynaud's Phenomenon: Occurs in 40% of patients treated with Bleomycin.
  • Second Malignancies: Chemotherapy is linked to two fatal hematologic malignancies: Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) and Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS) (associated with alkylating agents & topoisomerase inhibitors).

🎯 2. Targeted Biological Agents

Trastuzumab (Anti-HER2/neu)

Adjuvant therapy for HER2+ Breast Cancer. 2nd most common cardiotoxic agent.

💡 Trastuzumab vs. Doxorubicin Cardiotoxicity

Unlike anthracyclines, Trastuzumab cardiotoxicity is NOT dose-related, REVERSIBLE, and lacks myofibrillar dropout on biopsy. Monitor LVEF every 3–4 cycles via 2D Echo/MUGA. DEFER drug if EF drops >10%.

Bevacizumab (Anti-VEGF-A)

Recombinant humanized anti-VEGF mAb for Colorectal, Gynecologic, and Lung cancers.

⭐ Adverse Effects & Surgical Warning

Causes HTN, proteinuria (check urinalysis!), bleeding, and impaired wound healing. Hold drug at least 4 weeks before surgery! Life-threatening risks: GI perforation, arterial thrombosis, hemoptysis.

🛡️ 3. Immunotherapy & Checkpoint Inhibitors

Trains the host immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells by blocking inhibitory checkpoint pathways.

Pembrolizumab (Anti-PD-1)

First FDA-approved immunotherapy in the Philippines. Blocks PD-L1 binding to PD-1 receptor on T-cells.

  • 1st-Line Indication: Metastatic Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC) with high PD-L1 expression.
  • 2nd-Line Indication: Recurrent/metastatic Head & Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSCC); Advanced Melanoma.

Immune-Mediated Adverse Events (irAEs)

Autoimmune attack on normal host tissues. Presents as pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis, and nephritis.

⭐ Management of irAEs

Systemic Corticosteroids are the mainstay of management. Additional immunosuppressants (e.g., Infliximab) considered if steroid-refractory.

⚡ 10-Minute Last-Minute PLE Cram Sheet

Ultra-condensed high-yield rapid review. Read this in under 10 minutes before stepping into the examination hall!

💡 "IF YOU SEE X → THINK Y" CLINICAL CLUES
  • Afebrile neutropenia 1 week post-chemo: Observe & repeat CBC (do NOT give antibiotics if afebrile).
  • Fever + ANC < 1000 /µL: Immediate medical emergency! Assume neutropenic sepsis.
  • Endomyocardial biopsy with myofibrillar dropout: Doxorubicin (Anthracycline) toxicity.
  • Reversible drop in LVEF without myofibrillar dropout: Trastuzumab cardiotoxicity.
  • Diffuse interstitial ground-glass on CXR + non-productive cough: Bleomycin pneumonitis.
  • Stocking-glove neuropathy + lost DTRs: Vincristine / Vinblastine.
  • Cochlear hair cell damage / Hearing loss + High Creatinine: Cisplatin ototoxicity & nephrotoxicity.
  • Hemorrhagic cystitis: Cyclophosphamide / Ifosfamide (caused by acrolein; prevent with Mesna).
  • Palmar-Plantar Erythrodysesthesia (Hand-Foot Syndrome): 5-Fluorouracil or Capecitabine.
  • Osteonecrosis of the Jaw (ONJ): Bisphosphonate therapy (do dental exam first!).
  • Arthralgias & Myalgias in postmenopausal breast cancer: Exemestane / Anastrozole (Aromatase Inhibitors).
  • Endometrial thickening / bleeding on Tamoxifen: Endometrial carcinoma risk (do TVUS!).
  • Hypertension + Proteinuria + Bleeding: Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF).
  • Immune-mediated pneumonitis / colitis: Pembrolizumab (treat with Corticosteroids!).
🎯 HIGH-YIELD BUZZWORDS & CUTOFF NUMBERS
  • ANC Cutoff to proceed with Chemo: > 1500 /µL.
  • ANC Cutoff to stop G-CSF: > 3000 /µL.
  • Nadir Day: Day 10 post-chemo (recovery Day 14–21).
  • Spontaneous intracranial bleed threshold: Platelets < 10,000 /µL.
  • Surgical platelet transfusion threshold: Platelets < 50,000 /µL.
  • Doxorubicin CHF Cumulative Dose Limit: > 450–550 mg/m².
  • High Emetic Risk Cyclophosphamide Dose: ≥ 1,500 mg/m².
  • Bevacizumab Pre-op Hold Time: At least 4 weeks before surgery.
  • Longest acting 5-HT3 Antagonist: Palonosetron (half-life ~40 hrs).
  • ONLY Platinum Analog in High Emetic Risk: Cisplatin.
  • ONLY mAb in Moderate Emetic Risk: Alemtuzumab.
  • Folinic Acid (Leucovorin) Role: Biomodulator of 5-FU (NOT chemotherapy).
