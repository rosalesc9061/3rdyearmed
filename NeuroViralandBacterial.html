CNS INFECTIONS: VIRAL & BACTERIAL

Module Overview & High-Yield Learning Objectives

Target Learning Objectives
  • Syndrome Localization: Identify the major clinical syndromes of CNS infection (Meningitis, Encephalitis, Meningoencephalitis, Brain Abscess, Chronic Meningitis, Myelitis) and localize the precise anatomical compartment involved.
  • Pathophysiology & Dynamics: Explain how microbial invasion and host inflammation disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB), alter CSF hydrodynamics, impair cerebral perfusion pressure ($CPP = MAP - ICP$), and trigger cerebral edema.
  • CSF Pattern Recognition: Interpret classic and atypical CSF patterns in acute bacterial, viral/aseptic, tuberculous, and fungal CNS infections while identifying critical diagnostic pitfalls.
  • Emergency Empiric Management: Formulate immediate diagnostic and empiric antimicrobial/steroid management plans without causing harmful delays for lumbar puncture (LP) or neuroimaging.
  • Atypical & Special Entities: Master diagnostic criteria and clinical phenotypes of Brain Abscess, Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis, Tuberculous Meningitis, Cryptococcal Disease, Neurocysticercosis, and Prion Diseases (sCJD).

⭐ Fundamental Rule of CNS Emergencies
Acute bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis are medical and neurological emergencies. High clinical suspicion requires immediate administration of empiric antimicrobial therapy. Do NOT allow neuroimaging or lumbar puncture delays to postpone antibiotic or antiviral initiation!

Topic 1: CNS Infection Fundamentals & Pathophysiology

1. Anatomic Barriers & Vulnerability

The brain and spinal cord are enclosed within rigid bony compartments (skull and vertebral canal). Modest inflammation, vasogenic edema, or focal purulent collections rapidly raise intracranial pressure (ICP), compromising cerebral perfusion.

4 Broad Routes of Microbial Entry:

  • Hematogenous Spread (Most Common): Pathogens enter via bloodstream bacteremia, interact with cerebral microvascular endothelial cells, and cross the BBB. Typical for S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, Mycobacteria, fungi, and septic emboli (e.g., Infective Endocarditis).
  • Contiguous Spread: Direct extension from adjacent infected structures: paranasal sinuses, mastoid air cells, middle ear (otitis media), dental abscesses, or cranial osteomyelitis. Crucial in brain abscess development.
  • Direct Inoculation: Pathogens introduced via penetrating head trauma, neurosurgical procedures, CSF shunts, external ventricular drains, or basilar skull fracture defects with CSF leaks.
  • Neural Spread: Retrograde axonal transport along peripheral or cranial nerves. Classically exploited by Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1) along trigeminal (CN V) or olfactory pathways to temporal/limbic structures, as well as Rabies virus.
💡 Clinical Pearl: BBB Breakdown
The Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) is formed by microvascular endothelial cells with tight junctions, basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocytic end-feet. Inflammatory cytokines ($TNF-\alpha$, $IL-1\beta$) disrupt these tight junctions, increasing permeability. Plasma proteins enter CSF (elevating CSF protein) and leukocytes migrate across (pleocytosis), driving vasogenic cerebral edema.
2. Intracranial Pressure (ICP) & Cerebral Perfusion Dynamics

Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP) is defined by the mathematical relationship:

$$CPP = MAP - ICP$$

Where MAP is Mean Arterial Pressure and ICP is Intracranial Pressure. Normal ICP in adults is $100-180\text{ mmH}_2\text{O}$ ($8-14\text{ mmHg}$). Elevated ICP reduces CPP, leading to global ischemia, drowsiness, herniation, and death.

Bacterial Lysis / Pathogen Component Release → Cytokine Release ($TNF-\alpha$, $IL-1$) → BBB Disruption & Leukocyte Recruitment

↓
Cerebral Edema (Vasogenic, Cytotoxic, Interstitial) → Impaired CSF Absorption at Arachnoid Granulations → Marked ICP Rise & Decreased CPP

Clinical Indications for Cranial CT/MRI Prior to Lumbar Puncture

Performing an LP in the presence of a focal space-occupying lesion or severe pressure gradient creates a caudally directed pressure differential, triggering transtentorial or tonsillar brain herniation.

⭐ 5 Key Indications to Obtain Neuroimaging BEFORE LP
  1. Focal Neurologic Deficits (e.g., hemiparesis, cranial nerve palsy, aphasia).
  2. Papilledema on fundoscopic exam.
  3. New-onset Seizures (within 1 week of presentation).
  4. Markedly Depressed Sensorium / Encephalopathy (GCS $<10$ or severe confusion).
  5. Severely Immunocompromised State (e.g., advanced HIV, post-transplant, chemotherapy).
⚠️ Mandatory Protocol If CT Is Delayed
If CT scanning is required before LP, DO NOT WAIT FOR THE CT SCAN TO ADMINISTER ANTIBIOTICS!
Correct Order: 1. Draw Blood Cultures → 2. Immediately start Empiric IV Antibiotics + Dexamethasone → 3. Send for Urgent Neuroimaging → 4. Perform LP if imaging rules out mass effect.
Critical CSF Diagnostic Pitfalls
  • Early Bacterial Meningitis: Very early in course, WBC count may be only modestly elevated (<100/µL). Do not exclude bacterial meningitis if clinical suspicion is high!
  • Partially Treated Bacterial Meningitis: Self-medication or prior antibiotic administration (2-3 days prior) shifts the WBC differential toward lymphocytic predominance and sterilizes CSF cultures. However, low glucose and high protein persist! Use multiplex PCR.
  • Early Viral Meningitis: Within the first 24 hours, viral meningitis can transiently show a neutrophilic predominance before shifting to lymphocytic.
  • Non-specificity of Hypoglycorrhachia: Low CSF glucose is NOT specific for bacterial meningitis; it also occurs in TB meningitis, fungal meningitis, neurosarcoidosis, and carcinomatous meningitis.

Topic 3: Acute Bacterial Meningitis (Leptomeningitis)

1. Clinical Presentation & The Classic Triad

Acute bacterial meningitis is inflammation of the leptomeninges (pia mater, arachnoid mater) and subarachnoid CSF. It evolves rapidly (over hours to <7 days).

⭐ The Classic Clinical Triad
1. Fever
2. Nuchal Rigidity
3. Altered Mental Status

Clinical Reality: All 3 elements are present in only ~44-50% of patients. However, almost all (>95%) present with at least two of: headache, fever, neck stiffness, or altered sensorium.

Meningeal Irritation Signs:

  • Nuchal Rigidity: Inability to passively flex the neck forward due to severe muscle spasm.
  • Kernig Sign: Resistance or painful extension of the leg at the knee when the hip is flexed at 90 degrees.
  • Brudzinski Sign: Involuntary flexion of the hips and knees upon passive neck flexion.
  • Petechial/Purpuric Rash: Indicates invasive Neisseria meningitidis (Meningococcemia) with DIC and endotoxemia.
2. Age & Setting-Based Etiology & Empiric Therapy

Reconciled from Dr. Mesina and Dr. Guillermo (Adams & Victor 12th Ed.):

Age Group / Setting Major Etiologic Pathogens Empiric Antibiotic Regimen
Neonates (0 - 4 weeks) Group B Streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes Ampicillin + Cefotaxime (or Ampicillin + Gentamicin)
Infants (4 - 12 weeks) S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae, GBS, Listeria Ampicillin + 3rd-Gen Cephalosporin (± Dexamethasone)
Children & Adults (3 mos - 50 yrs) Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis 3rd-Gen Cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone / Cefotaxime) + Vancomycin
Older Adults (>50 yrs), Pregnancy, Alcoholism S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, Listeria monocytogenes, Gram-neg bacilli 3rd-Gen Cephalosporin + Vancomycin + AMPICILLIN (for Listeria)
Immunocompromised State S. pneumoniae, L. monocytogenes, Gram-neg bacilli (Pseudomonas) Vancomycin + Ampicillin + Ceftazidime (or Meropenem)

3. Dexamethasone Corticosteroid Therapy: Detailed Nuance

The administration of adjunctive corticosteroids in acute bacterial meningitis has historical and modern clinical debate detailed across both lectures:

  • Pathophysiologic Rationale: Bactericidal antibiotics induce rapid bacterial lysis, releasing cell wall endotoxins and teichoic acid. This amplifies subarachnoid space cytokine production ($TNF$, $IL-1$), worsening cerebral edema and hearing loss.
  • WHO & Modern Guidelines (Dr. Mesina): Dexamethasone ($10\text{ mg IV}$ q6h for 4 days) is indicated in suspected/proven Pneumococcal meningitis. Timing is vital: Must be given 10-20 minutes before or WITH the first antibiotic dose. Giving it after antibiotics confers no benefit.
  • Historical / Adams & Victor Perspective (Dr. Guillermo): Notes that early clinical trials failed to show consistent overall mortality benefits in all adult bacterial meningitides, and routinely avoids it in non-pneumococcal cases or HIV co-infection. Continuances depend on microbiologic confirmation.

Topic 4: Acute Viral Meningitis & Encephalitis

Anatomic Focus Meninges & subarachnoid CSF only Brain parenchyma (± meninges = meningoencephalitis)

2. Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1) Encephalitis
⭐ Classic Phenotype & Localization of HSV-1
HSV-1 gains access to the brain via retrograde neural spread along trigeminal or olfactory nerves. It displays a tropism for inferior/medial temporal lobes and limbic structures.

Clinical Clues: Rapid onset fever, headache, acute memory loss/amnesia (hippocampus), personality/behavioral changes (limbic system), aphasia (dominant temporal cortex), focal temporal seizures, and olfactory/gustatory hallucinations.

Diagnostic & Imaging Findings:

  • MRI (Gold Standard): T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and restricted diffusion (DWI) in asymmetric medial temporal lobes, insular cortices, and orbitofrontal lobes. Basal ganglia are spared (distinguishes HSV from MCA infarction!).
  • CSF PCR: High sensitivity (>95%) and specificity for HSV-1 DNA. CSF may show lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and red blood cells (RBCs) due to necrotizing hemorrhagic lesions.
  • Treatment: Immediate Empiric IV Acyclovir ($10\text{ mg/kg}$ IV q8h or $30\text{ mg/kg/day}$ for 14-21 days). DO NOT WAIT FOR PCR RESULTS TO START ACYCLOVIR!
💡 Japanese Encephalitis B (Flavivirus) Clue
JE virus is an endemic mosquito-borne arbovirus in Asia. Unlike HSV, JE specifically targets deep gray matter structures (thalamus, basal ganglia, brainstem), producing encephalitis accompanied by movement disorders (parkinsonism, dystonia, myarrhythmia) or flaccid paralysis.

Topic 5: Chronic & Opportunistic CNS Infections

1. Tuberculous Meningitis (TBM)

Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Highly prevalent in the Philippines. It presents subacutely over 1 to 4 weeks with low-grade fever, weight loss, night sweats, and progressive headache.

Pathophysiology & Anatomical Complications:

TBM produces thick gelatinous inflammatory exudates concentrated at the basal meninges (basal cisterns and Sylvian fissures). This accounts for its 3 primary complications:

  1. Cranial Neuropathies (Craniopathies): Entrapment of cranial nerves at the base of the skull, most commonly CN III, VI (ocular motor palsies) and CN VII.
  2. Communicating Hydrocephalus: Impaired CSF resorption through arachnoid granulations due to basal exudates blocking subarachnoid spaces.
  3. Basal Ganglia / Internal Capsule Infarctions: Inflammatory endarteritis/vasculitis involving deep perforating vessels (lenticulostriate arteries) passing through the Circle of Willis.

Treatment Protocol:

Antituberculous Regimen: 2 months Intensive Phase (Isoniazid + Rifampin + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol) followed by 7-10 months Continuation Phase (Isoniazid + Rifampin).
Adjunctive Dexamethasone: Tapered over 8 weeks ($0.3-0.4\text{ mg/kg/day}$ initial). Shown to significantly reduce mortality in HIV-negative TBM patients!

2. Cryptococcal Meningitis & Fungal Infections

Common opportunistic fungal infection in advanced HIV infection ($CD4 < 100/\text{µL}$) or organ transplant recipients receiving immunosuppression.

  • Dominant Pathophysiologic Problem: Markedly elevated Intracranial Pressure (ICP) due to fungal capsular polysaccharide clogging arachnoid villi.
  • Classic MRI Finding: "Soap-bubble" gelatinous pseudocysts located in the basal ganglia and Virchow-Robin perivascular spaces.
  • Diagnosis: Cryptococcal Antigen Latex Agglutination System (CALAS) (>95% sensitive), India Ink preparation (shows budding encapsulated yeast), and fungal culture.
  • Management: Induction with Amphotericin B + Flucytosine, followed by Fluconazole. Active management of high ICP with repeated therapeutic LPs.
3. Neurocysticercosis (NCC)

Caused by the encysted larval stage of the pork tapeworm, Taenia solium. Most common cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic developing regions (including the Philippines).

Staging Imaging Features (CT / MRI) Clinical & Inflammatory Status
Vesicular Stage Cystic lesion with visible eccentric dot (the scolex). No edema. Viable parasite. Asymptomatic or mild symptoms. Immune tolerance.
Colloidal Stage Ring-enhancing lesion with prominent surrounding vasogenic edema. Degenerating parasite releasing antigens. Provokes severe inflammation & seizures.
Granular-Nodular Thickening cyst wall, involuting lesion, declining edema. Healing stage. Parasite dying.
Calcified Stage Small calcified punctate nodule best seen on non-contrast CT. Inactive, dead parasite. Acts as a chronic epileptogenic focus.
⚠️ Treatment Pitfall: Antiparasitic Administration
Administering antiparasitic drugs (Praziquantel / Albendazole) causes massive parasite lysis, dramatically increasing local edema and seizures. Always co-administer high-dose corticosteroids (Dexamethasone) and evaluate for hydrocephalus before initiating anti-helminthics!

Topic 6: Focal CNS Infections & Brain Abscess

1. Etiology & Anatomic Pathways

A brain abscess is a focal, encapsulated parenchymal collection of pus. Etiology depends on entry route:

  • Otogenic (Middle ear / Mastoiditis): Localizes to Temporal Lobe (causing visual field deficits or aphasia) or Cerebellum (causing ataxia, nystagmus, 4th ventricle obstruction).
  • Sinusitis (Frontal / Ethmoid sinuses): Localizes to Frontal Lobe (causing executive dysfunction, personality changes).
  • Hematogenous (Endocarditis, Pulmonary AV fistulas, IVDU): Characterized by multiple lesions distributed at the gray-white matter junction in the MCA distribution.
  • Most Common Pathogens: Virulent Streptococci (anaerobic or microaerophilic, 30-50%), often mixed with oral anaerobes (Bacteroides, Prevotella) or Staphylococcus aureus (post-trauma/endocarditis).
2. Stages of Abscess Formation (Parenchymal Evolution)
Stage Timeline Pathologic Features Imaging Characteristics
Early Cerebritis Days 1 - 3 Unconfined inflammatory response, localized edema, vascular congestion, early necrosis. Non-enhancing or patchy enhancement; patchy high FLAIR signal.
Late Cerebritis Days 4 - 7 Progressive central liquefactive necrosis; early peripheral fibroblasts. Irregular thin ring enhancement; central hyperintensity on T2.
Early Capsule Days 8 - 14 Development of neovascularized collagenous capsule; central pus formation. Well-defined rim enhancement on T1+C; ring lesion.
Late Capsule > 14 Days Dense mature collagenous wall with dense gliosis surrounding the lesion. Thick, smooth, uniformly hyper-enhancing rim; persistent central necrosis.
⭐ MRI Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) Hallmark
On brain MRI, a pyogenic brain abscess shows Restricted Diffusion (Hyperintense on DWI, Hypointense on ADC) within its necrotic center due to viscous, hypercellular pus. This key finding distinguishes an abscess from a necrotic cystic tumor (metastosis or glioblastoma, which shows free diffusion)!
⚠️ LP Contraindication & Preferred Management
Lumbar puncture is strictly contraindicated in brain abscess due to high herniation risk and low diagnostic yield. Preferred initial definitive management is CT/MRI-guided Stereotactic Needle Aspiration (for decompression and pathogen isolation) plus 6-8 weeks of targeted IV antibiotic therapy.

Topic 7: Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis & Prion Disease

1. Autoimmune Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis

An autoimmune encephalitis that closely mimics viral encephalitis. Frequently affects young females and children, and is strongly associated with Ovarian Teratomas (paraneoplastic mechanism).

Multistage Clinical Progression:

  1. Prodromal Phase: Nonspecific fever, headache, upper respiratory symptoms.
  2. Psychiatric Phase: Severe anxiety, insomnia, mania, paranoia, hallucinations, catatonia.
  3. Neurologic / Movement Phase: Memory loss, seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, choreoathetosis, limb myarrhythmias.
  4. Autonomic Instability Phase: Hypoventilation, cardiac arrhythmias, blood pressure fluctuations, coma.
⭐ Confirmatory Laboratory Diagnosis
Definite diagnosis is confirmed by detecting IgG antibodies against the GluN1 (NR1) subunit of the NMDA receptor in CSF or serum. EEG may show "Extreme Delta Brush".

Management: 1. First-line Immunotherapy (IV Methylprednisolone, IVIG, or Plasma Exchange) → 2. Tumor Screening (Pelvic Ultrasound/MRI for ovarian teratoma) & Surgical Excision → 3. Second-line Immunotherapy (Rituximab or Cyclophosphamide) if refractory.
2. Prion Diseases & Sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (sCJD)

Prion diseases are transmissible, fatal neurodegenerative disorders caused by conformational misfolding of normal cellular prion protein ($PrP^C$) into an abnormal scrapie isoform ($PrP^{Sc}$). $PrP^{Sc}$ acts as a template, promoting progressive protein aggregation, spongiform vacuolation, and neuronal cell death without inducing a cellular inflammatory immune response.

Sporadic CJD Clinical Phenotype:

  • Rapidly Progressive Dementia: Cognitive decline evolving over weeks to months.
  • Startle Myoclonus: Involuntary muscle jerks elicited by auditory or tactile stimuli.
  • Cerebellar & Extrapyramidal Dysfunction: Ataxia, rigidity, akinetic mutism.

Diagnostic Biomarkers & Imaging:

  • MRI Brain: "Cortical Ribboning" (diffusion restriction on DWI in cerebral cortex) and hyperintensity in caudate nucleus and putamen.
  • CSF Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion (RT-QuIC): Highly specific assay detecting minute amounts of misfolded prion protein.
  • CSF 14-3-3 & Total Tau Proteins: Elevated surrogate markers reflecting rapid, severe neuronal lysis (nonspecific; elevated in acute stroke/encephalitis).
  • EEG: Periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWCs).
⚠️ Mandatory Exclusions in Suspected CJD
Never diagnose CJD without actively excluding treatable mimics of rapidly progressive dementia: Autoimmune encephalitis (Anti-LGI1, Anti-CASPR2), HSV encephalitis, Neurosyphilis, HIV encephalopathy, Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus, Toxic/Metabolic encephalopathy, and CNS Lymphoma!

Topic 11: Ultra-Condensed Rapid Cram Sheet (< 10 Min Read)

  • Petechial / Purpuric Rash in Febrile Patient: → Neisseria meningitidis (Meningococcemia).
  • Fever + Headache + Acute Memory Loss + Behavioral Changes + Focal Temporal Seizures: → HSV-1 Encephalitis.
  • Encephalitis + Parkinsonian Movement Disorder / Flaccid Paralysis: → Japanese Encephalitis B Virus.
