Autonomic Nervous System Overview

Welcome to the interactive master reviewer for Disorders of the Autonomic Nervous System. Designed specifically for 3rd-year medical students, this resource unifies lectures by Dr. Pia Teresa A. Camara-Chua and Dr. Marietta Cruz-Olaivar into a single, high-yield board review platform.

🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Describe the anatomical organization of the sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric nervous systems.
  • Differentiate central vs. peripheral autonomic control mechanisms.
  • Identify key autonomic neurotransmitters, receptor subtypes, and pharmacologic targets.
  • Recognize clinical presentations of dysautonomia across cardiovascular, GI, GU, sudomotor, and pupillary domains.
  • Interpret diagnostic tests: tilt-table, Valsalva ratio, deep breathing, and pupillary pharmacologic testing.
  • Master central respiratory/swallowing control and clinical syndromes (Ondine's Curse, Sympathetic Storm, LEMS, Riley-Day).
📐 Core Anatomical Axioms
  • Sympathetic Origin: Thoracolumbar spinal cord gray matter lateral horns (C8 to L2).
  • Parasympathetic Origin: Craniosacral (CN III, VII, IX, X and S2–S4 sacral spinal cord).
  • Sympathetic Ganglia: Paravertebral chain and prevertebral (collateral) ganglia; short preganglionic, long postganglionic.
  • Parasympathetic Ganglia: Terminal (intramural) ganglia within or near target organs; long preganglionic, short postganglionic.
⚡ Core Neurochemical Axioms
  • All Preganglionic Fibers: Release Acetylcholine (ACh) acting on Nicotinic receptors.
  • Parasympathetic Postganglionic: Release ACh acting on Muscarinic receptors (blocked by Atropine).
  • Sympathetic Postganglionic: Release Norepinephrine (NE) acting on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ receptors.
  • ⭐ CRITICAL EXCEPTION: Sympathetic postganglionic innervation to eccrine sweat glands uses ACh acting on Muscarinic receptors!

1. ANS Organization, Pathways & Neurotransmitters

The Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) is the involuntary branch of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) regulating internal visceral homeostasis, smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glandular secretions.

⭐ MUST KNOW: CNS vs. ANS Motor Architecture

The Somatic Nervous System (CNS) utilizes a single motor neuron bridging the CNS directly to the effector organ (skeletal muscle). In contrast, the Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) utilizes a two-neuron pathway consisting of a preganglionic neuron (cell body in CNS) synapsing in an autonomic ganglion with a postganglionic neuron that projects to effector tissues.

Cranial Parasympathetic Ganglia & Innervation
Cranial Division (CN III, VII, IX, X)
  • CN III (Oculomotor): Originates in Edinger-Westphal nucleus → Synapses at Ciliary Ganglion → Pupillary sphincter (miosis) & ciliary muscle (accommodation).
  • CN VII (Facial): Superior salivatory nucleus → Synapses at Pterygopalatine Ganglion (lacrimal & nasal glands) and Submandibular Ganglion (submandibular & sublingual salivary glands).
  • CN IX (Glossopharyngeal): Inferior salivatory nucleus → Synapses at Otic Ganglion → Parotid gland (profuse watery saliva).
  • CN X (Vagus): Dorsal motor nucleus of vagus & nucleus ambiguus → Innervates heart, lungs, GI tract down to splenic flexure.
Sacral Division & Sympathetic Distribution
  • Sacral Parasympathetic (S2–S4): Originates in lateral horn gray matter of S2–S4 → Preganglionic fibers travel via sacral nerves / cauda equina → Synapse in intramural ganglia of distal colon, rectum, bladder, and erectile tissue.
  • Sympathetic Distribution (C8–L2): 3 cervical ganglia (Superior, Middle, Stellate/Cervicothoracic), 11 thoracic, and 4–6 lumbar ganglia. Long postganglionic fibers allow widespread multi-organ activation.

⚠️ COMMON PITFALL: Atropine & Ganglionic Blockade

Do NOT confuse Nicotinic and Muscarinic blockade! Atropine blocks postganglionic muscarinic parasympathetic receptors and sweat glands. Atropine does NOT block autonomic ganglia or the neuromuscular junction. Nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia are counteracted by agents like tubocurarine or hexamethonium, not atropine.

2. Central Control of Autonomic Function & Homeostasis

The Central Autonomic Network (CAN) coordinates autonomic, endocrine, and behavioral responses to maintain internal milieu stability (thermoregulation, blood pressure, fluid balance, and stress responses).

Brainstem Integration & The NTS

The principal visceral sensory nucleus in the brainstem is the Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS) located in the dorsal medulla.

  • Visceral Afferents: Cardiovascular, respiratory, and GI afferents carried by CN IX (via petrosal ganglion) and CN X (via nodose ganglion) terminate directly on specific subnuclei of the NTS.
  • Projections: Caudal NTS integrates incoming signals and projects to the hypothalamus, amygdala, insular cortex, and medullary/pontine respiratory and vasopressor centers.
Cerebral Control & The "Visceral Brain"

The Hypothalamus is the master integrator of the ANS and limbic system, acting through direct descending pathways to the brainstem/spinal cord and via the pituitary gland.

  • Highest Integration Levels: Ventromedial prefrontal cortex and cingulate cortex.
  • Insular Cortex: Receives projections from NTS, parabrachial nucleus, and lateral hypothalamus. Direct electrical stimulation of the insula can trigger severe cardiac dysrhythmias and wall motion abnormalities.
  • Visceral Brain: Cingulate gyrus, hippocampal gyrus, amygdala, substantia innominata, septal, piriform, and habenular nuclei.
Baroreflex Physiology & Blood Pressure Homeostasis

The baroreflex provides moment-to-moment rapid blood pressure regulation. Baroreceptors are stretch receptors located in the carotid sinus (innervated by CN IX) and aortic arch (innervated by CN X).

💡 CLINICAL PEARL: Orthostatic Response Mechanism

When a healthy person stands up, gravity causes 300–800 mL of blood to pool in the lower extremities, reducing venous return and cardiac output. This transient drop in BP reduces baroreceptor firing, which immediately disinhibits sympathetic outflow and inhibits vagal tone, producing compensatory tachycardia and systemic vasoconstriction. Failure of this baroreflex arc leads to orthostatic hypotension!

3. Autonomic Regulation of Pelvic & Visceral Organ Systems

Regulation of Bladder Function (Micturition)

Micturition requires coordinated switching between the storage phase (sympathetic dominant) and the voiding phase (parasympathetic dominant), mediated by the Pontine Micturition Center (PMC / Barrington's nucleus).

1. Storage Phase (Sympathetic T11–L2 + Somatic)
  • Sympathetic (Hypogastric Nerve): Releases NE acting on $\beta_3$ / $\beta_2$ receptors to relax the detrusor muscle, and on $\alpha_1$ receptors to contract the bladder neck/internal sphincter.
  • Somatic Pudendal Nerve (S2–S4): Contracts the external urethral sphincter under voluntary control.
  • Result: Urine accumulates without leakage.
2. Voiding Phase (Parasympathetic S2–S4)
  • Parasympathetic (Pelvic Splanchnic Nerves): Releases ACh acting on $M_3$ receptors to cause powerful detrusor muscle contraction.
  • Sphincter Relaxation: Inhibition of sympathetic and somatic tone relaxes internal and external sphincters.
  • Pontine Micturition Center: Coordinates detrusor contraction with sphincter relaxation.
Regulation of Bowel Function & Defecation

Bowel regulation depends on the intrinsic Enteric Nervous System (ENS), modulated by extrinsic autonomic nerves:

  • Colonic Storage: Sympathetic activity inhibits intestinal motility and maintains tonic contraction of the internal anal sphincter.
  • Defecation Reflex: Rectal distension activates pelvic visceral afferents. Parasympathetic nerves (S2–S4) increase rectal and colonic peristaltic contractions and relax the internal anal sphincter. Voluntary relaxation of the external anal sphincter (pudendal nerve) permits evacuation.

4. Clinical Disorders of the Autonomic Nervous System

⭐ MUST KNOW: Diagnostic Definition of Orthostatic Hypotension (OH)

Orthostatic Hypotension is defined as a sustained reduction of Systolic Blood Pressure of $\ge 20 \text{ mmHg}$ OR a reduction of Diastolic Blood Pressure of $\ge 10 \text{ mmHg}$ within 3 minutes of standing up from a recumbent position (or on tilt-table testing). Note: Some criteria use $30/15 \text{ mmHg}$.

1. Acute Pandysautonomia (Acute Autonomic Ganglionopathy) ▼

Definition: Rare acute/subacute widespread sympathetic and parasympathetic failure with complete sparing of somatic motor and sensory nerve fibers.

Pathophysiology: Autoimmune antibody-mediated destruction of autonomic postganglionic fibers or autonomic ganglia (associated with ganglionic AChR antibodies; seen as a post-infectious Guillain-Barré variant).

Clinical Picture: Severe orthostatic hypotension, anhidrosis, fixed unreactive pupils, loss of lacrimation/salivation (dry eyes, dry mouth), erectile dysfunction, neurogenic bladder/bowel (urinary retention, severe postprandial bloating, paralytic ileus/constipation), and profound fatigue.

Treatment & Prognosis: IVIg or plasmapheresis (plasma exchange). Prognosis is generally good with gradual recovery.

2. Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) ▼

Definition: Adult-onset, sporadic, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative fatal synucleinopathy characterized by oligodendroglial cytoplasmic inclusions (alpha-synuclein).

Clinical Subtypes:

  • MSA-P (Parkinsonian type): Prominent parkinsonism poorly responsive to L-dopa.
  • MSA-C (Cerebellar type): Prominent cerebellar ataxia and dysarthria.
  • MSA-A (Shy-Drager Syndrome / Autonomic type): Predominant autonomic failure (severe orthostatic hypotension, urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction, vocal cord paresis/stridor).
3. Peripheral Neuropathies with Neurogenic OH & Diabetic Autonomic Neuropathy ▼

Etiologies: Diabetes mellitus (most common), amyloidosis, alcohol, nutritional deficiencies, toxic/heavy metals, porphyria.

Diabetic Polyneuropathy: Long-standing poorly controlled diabetes causes unmyelinated/small-fiber autonomic loss. Manifests as orthostatic hypotension, resting tachycardia (due to early vagal loss), gastroparesis (delayed gastric emptying, postprandial fullness, vomiting), diabetic cystopathy (neurogenic bladder), erectile dysfunction, and gustatory sweating.

4. Familial Autonomic Neuropathy (Riley-Day Syndrome / HSAN Type III) ▼

Genetics: Autosomal recessive disorder primarily affecting Ashkenazi Jewish infants and children (IKBKAP/ELP1 gene mutation).

Hallmark Features: Postural hypotension with marked BP lability, absence of fungiform papillae on tongue (taste loss), defective lacrimation (alacrima/dry eyes), insensitivity to pain and temperature, hyperhidrosis, blotchiness of skin, cyclic vomiting, and emotional lability.

5. Horner Syndrome (Bernard-Horner Syndrome) & 3-Neuron Pathway ▼

Definition: Interruption of Oculosympathetic sympathetic supply to the eye and face.

Classic Triad: Miosis (pupillary constrictor unopposed), Ptosis (paralysis of superior tarsal / Müller's muscle), and Anhidrosis (loss of sweating on ipsilateral face).

3-Order Oculosympathetic Pathway Breakdown
  • 1st-Order (Central) Neuron: Hypothalamus → travels down brainstem and cervical spinal cord → synapses in Ciliospinal Center of Budge (C8–T2). (Lesions: Wallenberg syndrome, stroke, syringomyelia).
  • 2nd-Order (Preganglionic) Neuron: Ciliospinal center → exits spinal cord → sympathetic chain → apex of lung → synapses in Superior Cervical Ganglion (SCG) at C2–C3 level. (Lesions: Pancoast apical lung tumor, subclavian aneurysm, neck trauma).
  • 3rd-Order (Postganglionic) Neuron: SCG → travels along internal carotid artery plexus → cavernous sinus → long ciliary nerves → dilator pupillae and Müller muscle. (⭐ Note: Facial sudomotor fibers branch at SCG along the external carotid artery! Thus, 3rd-order post-carotid lesions show ptosis/miosis WITHOUT facial anhidrosis!).
6. Acute Autonomic Crises (Sympathetic Storm / PSH) ▼

Etiologies: Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage, acute cocaine/methamphetamine/phenylpropanolamine toxicity.

⭐ THREE TEMPORAL MECHANISMS OF HYPERSYMPATHETIC STATE
  1. Acute Catecholamine Surge: Sudden massive adrenal release of epinephrine and NE at the time of ictus → acute extreme hypertension and tachycardia.
  2. Cushing's Response: Brainstem-mediated vasopressor reflex triggered by high ICP → Hypertension, Bradycardia, and Irregular respiration.
  3. Late Chronic Phenomenon (Diencephalic Autonomic Seizures / PSH): Recurrent paroxysmal episodes of severe hypertension, profuse diaphoresis, hyperthermia, mydriasis, and rigid extensor posturing.
7. Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS) ▼

Pathology: Autoantibodies directed against presynaptic P/Q-type Voltage-Gated Calcium Channels (VGCC), inhibiting ACh release at both nicotinic (NMJ) and muscarinic (autonomic) synapses. Highly associated with Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC).

Autonomic Symptoms: Dry mouth (xerostomia - most common), constipation, erectile dysfunction, urinary hesitancy/difficulty.

Key Clinical Distinction: Weakness in LEMS improves with exertion/repetitive muscle contraction (facilitation), unlike Myasthenia Gravis where weakness worsens with activity.

5. Nervous System Control of Respiration & Aberrant Breathing

Breathing is completely under neural control. Respiratory rhythm generation and pattern modulation occur in lower brainstem medullary and pontine nuclei.

Paired Respiratory Brainstem Nuclei Groups
1. Dorsal Respiratory Group (DRG)

Located dorsal medulla near Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS). Contains primarily inspiratory neurons. Connects directly to phrenic (C3–C5) and intercostal motor neurons.

2. Ventral Respiratory Group (VRG)

Extends along ventral medulla near nucleus retroambiguus/ambiguus.

  • Rostral VRG: Inspiratory neurons.
  • Caudal VRG: Expiratory neurons.
  • Pre-Bötzinger Complex: Rostral ventrolateral medulla; essential pacemaker for respiratory rhythm generation.
3. Pontine Respiratory Group (PRG)

Located in dorsolateral pons (parabrachial nucleus). Acts as "on-off" switches modulating transitions between inspiration and expiration (pneumotaxic center).

🧠 EXAM TIP: Neuromuscular Respiratory Failure Indicators

In acute neuromuscular respiratory failure (GBS, Myasthenia Gravis), watch for Paradoxical Respiration (inward abdominal retraction during inspiration) and Respiratory Alternans (diaphragm descends only on alternate breaths). These signs appear when Vital Capacity (VC) drops to ~10% of normal (~500 mL), signaling impending respiratory arrest requiring intubation!

6. Control of Swallowing, Vomiting & Dysphagia

Nervous System Control of Swallowing

Swallowing is a complex reflex-coordinated process occurring ~1 time per minute at rest. Neural control is centered in the Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS) in the medulla, coordinating with respiratory centers to enforce swallowing apnea (preventing aspiration).

1. Oral Phase (Voluntary)

Controlled by motor cortex and CN XII (Hypoglossal). Tongue propels food bolus into oropharynx. Lesions cause difficulty initiating swallow and lingual dysarthria.

2. Pharyngeal Phase (Reflexive)

Mediated by CN IX (Glossopharyngeal) and CN X (Vagus). Nasopharynx closes, larynx elevates, epiglottis folds, vocal cords adduct, and cricopharyngeus relaxes.

3. Esophageal Phase (Involuntary)

Driven by CN X (Vagus). Sequential peristaltic waves propel bolus down esophagus into stomach.

Dysphagia, Aspiration & Clinical Neurologic Causes
  • Stroke: Most common neurologic cause of swallowing discoordination. Usually improves over weeks, but up to 50% remain impaired at 6 months.
  • Parkinson's Disease: Reduced swallowing frequency + discoordination between swallowing and breathing → high risk of silent aspiration.
  • Velopalatine Closure Failure: Causes nasal regurgitation of liquids and hypernasal speech. Caused by MG, CN X lesions, or bulbar/pseudobulbar palsy.
Neural Control of Vomiting (Emesis)

Vomiting is coordinated by medullary networks receiving inputs from the Area Postrema (located in the floor of the 4th ventricle).

⭐ Area Postrema & Neurochemistry

The Area Postrema lacks a blood-brain barrier (BBB), serving as the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) detecting circulating emetogenic toxins and drugs. It expresses rich receptor density: Dopamine $D_2$, Serotonin $5\text{-HT}_3$, Opioid, Substance P / $NK_1$, and ACh receptors. Pharmacologic antiemetics like Ondansetron ($5\text{-HT}_3$ blocker) and Metoclopramide ($D_2$ antagonist) act directly here!

7. Clinical Approach & Autonomic Testing

Systematic clinical assessment of dysautonomia follows 4 steps: (1) Identify affected domains, (2) Determine pattern (central vs. peripheral, acute vs. chronic), (3) Search for etiology, and (4) Treat underlying disorder and manage symptoms.

💡 CLINICAL PEARL: Simplest Bedside Test for Vagal Dysfunction

Failure of the heart rate to rise in response to a drop in blood pressure upon standing (or loss of heart rate variability during deep breathing) is the simplest, most sensitive bedside indicator of vagal (parasympathetic) nerve dysfunction!

  • Ptosis + Miosis + Anhidrosis: → Horner Syndrome.
  • Horner Syndrome + pupil dilates with Apraclonidine: → Confirms Horner Syndrome.
  • Horner Syndrome + pupil fails to dilate with Hydroxyamphetamine: → 3rd-Order Postganglionic Lesion.
  • TBI patient + HTN + mydriasis + diaphoresis + extensor posturing: → Sympathetic Storm (Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity).
  • Proximal weakness + dry mouth + strength improves with effort: → Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS).
  • Loss of automatic breathing during sleep ONLY (awake normal): → Ondine's Curse.
  • Waxing-waning respiration with LOC mirroring cycle: → Cheyne-Stokes Breathing (CHF / hemispheric lesion).
  • Singultus (Hiccups) + wallenberg stroke: → Medullary circuit lesion (Rx: Baclofen).
  • Inward abdominal retraction during inspiration: → Paradoxical Respiration (impending diaphragm paralysis).
🎯 Buzzword Rapid Reference
  • NTS (Nucleus Tractus Solitarius): Principal brainstem visceral sensory nucleus (CN IX, X).
  • Area Postrema: Floor of 4th ventricle, lacks BBB, chemoreceptor trigger zone for vomiting.
  • Pre-Bötzinger Complex: Pacemaker for respiratory rhythm generation in ventrolateral medulla.
  • Ciliospinal Center of Budge: C8–T2 origin of 2nd-order preganglionic sympathetic fibers for Horner's.
  • Eccrine Sweat Glands: Anatomically sympathetic, BUT neurochemically CHOLINERGIC ($M_3$).
  • 30:15 Ratio $\ge 1.04$: Normal HR response to standing (tests vagal efferent).
  • Valsalva Phase IV Overshoot: Normal sympathetic vasoconstriction recovery; absent in autonomic failure.
  • Vital Capacity $\le 10\%$ (~500 mL): Threshold for acute neuromuscular respiratory failure.
  • Atropine: Blocks muscarinic postganglionic parasympathetic, does NOT block ganglia or NMJ.
  • Pudendal Nerve (S2–S4): Somatic control of external urethral and external anal sphincters.
📊 Rapid Review Table: Drug Reactions
  • 4% Cocaine: Dilates normal pupil; no dilation in Horner.
  • 0.5% Apraclonidine: Dilates Horner pupil, reverses ptosis.
  • 1% Hydroxyamphetamine: Dilates 1st/2nd order Horner; fails in 3rd order.
  • 2.5% Methacholine / 0.125% Pilocarpine: Constricts Adie's tonic pupil (denervation supersensitivity).
  • Baclofen: First-line pharmacotherapy for intractable hiccups (singultus).
