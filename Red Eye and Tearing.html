The Red Eye & Tearing

OPHTHALMOLOGY BOARD REVIEWER • PLE / LE1

Ophthalmology Core Module: Red Eye & Tearing

Based on the lecture by Verma / Efren R. Garcia, MD, DPBO | Prepared for 3rd Year Philippine Medical Students & PLE Preparation

🎯 High-Yield Summary

The red eye (ocular hyperemia) is one of the most common presenting ocular chief complaints in clinical practice. It stems from inflammation or infection causing vascular dilation of the conjunctival vessels (superficial) or branches of the anterior ciliary arteries (deep).


Primary triage requires distinguishing between Non-Serious (Non-Vision-Threatening) conditions (e.g., conjunctivitis, subconjunctival hemorrhage) and Serious (Vision-Threatening) emergencies (e.g., acute angle-closure glaucoma, microbial keratitis, anterior uveitis, scleritis).

📌 Core Learning Objectives

  • Master the step-by-step Clinical Decision Tree for red eye diagnosis based on Pain, Discharge, Photophobia, and Itchiness.
  • Differentiate Conjunctival Injection from Ciliary Flush/Injection.
  • Identify etiologies, presentations, and treatments for Bacterial, Viral, and Allergic Conjunctivitis.
  • Diagnose and manage emergency conditions: Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma, Microbial Keratitis, Anterior Uveitis, and Scleritis.
  • Evaluate tearing disorders: Differentiate Lacrimation (hypersecretion) from Epiphora (impaired drainage/obstruction).
⭐ MUST KNOW - DANGER SIGNS OF THE RED EYE
Presence of any of the following signs indicates a serious vision-threatening condition requiring urgent ophthalmology referral:
  • Blurring or Loss of Vision (indicates corneal, intraocular, or optic nerve involvement)
  • Moderate to Severe Ocular Pain (indicates corneal ulcer, uveitis, scleritis, or glaucoma)
  • Photophobia (spasm of inflamed ciliary body and iris)
  • Ciliary Flush / Injection (deep perilimbal hyperemia)
  • Corneal Opacity / Infiltrate / Ulcer or Edema
  • Pupillary Abnormalities (fixed mid-dilated, or irregular miotic pupil with synechiae)
  • Elevated Intraocular Pressure (IOP > 21 mmHg)

Anatomy & Physiology of the Eye and Lacrimal Apparatus

Structural foundations of ocular coats, anterior chamber dynamics, and tear film physiology.

1. Outer / External Coat

Cornea: Anterior transparent, avascular structure with rich sensory innervation via CN V1 (Ophthalmic division of Trigeminal Nerve).

Sclera: Posterior opaque, fibrous protective layer.

Conjunctiva: Covers the sclera anteriorly. Divided into bulbar and palpebral conjunctiva.

Episclera: Vascular layer sandwiched between conjunctiva and sclera.

Limbus: Corneoscleral junction; anatomically the weakest part of the outer coat.

2. Middle Coat (Uveal Tract)

Vascular middle layer of the eyeball consisting of 3 continuous structures:

  • Iris: Anterior pigmented sphincter controlling pupil size.
  • Ciliary Body: Produces aqueous humor and controls lens accommodation.
  • Choroid: Posterior vascular coat supplying the outer retina.

*Inflammation: Iris = Iritis; Iris + Ciliary body = Anterior Uveitis / Iridocyclitis; Choroid = Posterior Uveitis / Choroiditis; Entire uvea = Pan-uveitis.

3. Inner Coat & Chambers

Retina: Inner transparent neural coat.

Anterior Chamber (AC): Space between corneal endothelium and iris/pupil. Normal depth: 3 mm; normal angle: 45°.

Posterior Chamber (PC): Potential space between posterior iris surface and anterior lens capsule.

Anatomical Variation: Asian patients are frequently born with narrow angles (~20°), predisposing them to acute angle-closure glaucoma!

Figure 1: Normal Aqueous Humor Circulation Pathway. Obstruction at the pupillary aperture leads to pupillary block and acute angle closure glaucoma. Normal IOP = 10 - 21 mmHg.

💧 Anatomy of the Lacrimal Drainage System

Lacrimal Outflow Pathway

  1. Tears produced: Secreted by Main Lacrimal Gland & Accessory glands.
  2. Puncta: Superior and Inferior puncta on medial lid margins collect tear film.
  3. Canaliculi: Superior and Inferior canaliculi carry tears to common canaliculus.
  4. Lacrimal Sac: Resides in the lacrimal fossa behind medial palpebral ligament.
  5. Nasolacrimal Duct (NLD): Descends into the nose to drain into the Inferior Meatus under the inferior turbinate.
  6. Valve of Hasner: Mucosal fold valve at NLD entry to inferior meatus. Common site of congenital imperforation!

Lacrimation vs. Epiphora

💡 Key Physiological Distinction

Lacrimation: True hypersecretion / excessive tear production caused by reflex stimulation of lacrimal gland (e.g., corneal FB, inflammation, emotion).

Epiphora: Passive overflow of tears down the cheek due to mechanical blockage/obstruction of drainage system (e.g., NLD obstruction, punctal stenosis) or lid laxity failure.

Conjunctival Disorders & Diagnostic Triaging

Bacterial, Viral, Allergic Conjunctivitis, Dry Eye Syndrome, Subconjunctival Hemorrhage, Pterygium, and Pinguecula.

🧫 Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Non-Painful

Etiology by Age:

  • Newborns: Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae[cite: 1].
  • Children: Haemophilus influenzae (frequently associated with URTI)[cite: 1].
  • Adults: Staphylococcus aureus, Moraxella[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Acute onset, acute red eye, mucopurulent/purulent discharge causing eyelids to be matted shut upon waking, foreign body sensation, normal vision, NO eye pain[cite: 1]. Usually starts unilateral[cite: 1].

Physical Signs: Papillae (polygonal elevations <1mm with central fibrovascular core)[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Clinical; Conjunctival scrapings/gram stain showing Gram-negative coccobacilli (if H. influenzae)[cite: 1].

Treatment: Topical broad-spectrum fluoroquinolone eye drops (Levofloxacin, Moxifloxacin, Fucithalmic)[cite: 1].

🦠 Viral Conjunctivitis ("Sore Eyes")

Contagious

Etiology: Most common cause of acute red eye[cite: 1]. Caused by Adenovirus[cite: 1]. Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (EKC serotypes: 8, 19, 29, 37)[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Starts unilateral then becomes bilateral after a few days[cite: 1]. Profuse watery/serous discharge, foreign body sensation, lid edema, normal vision[cite: 1].

Pathognomonic Sign: Preauricular Lymphadenopathy (PAL)[cite: 1]. Conjunctival follicles (pebbly lymphoid germinal centers), subconjunctival hemorrhage, pseudomembranes[cite: 1].

Complication: Subepithelial corneal opacities leading to transient blurred vision[cite: 1].

Management: Supportive; cold compresses, artificial tears, goggle protection[cite: 1]. Strict 2-week quarantine (contagious for 10-12 days via eye-to-hand/fomites)[cite: 1].

🌸 Allergic Conjunctivitis

Type I Hypersensitivity

Etiology: Type I Hypersensitivity (IgE-mediated) reaction to environmental allergens (pollen, animal dander)[cite: 1]. Associated with rhinitis, asthma, eczema, food allergies[cite: 1].

Hallmark Symptom: SEVERE INTENSE ITCHINESS (PRURITUS)[cite: 1]. Always bilateral[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Watery to white stringy/ropy discharge, conjunctival chemosis (edema), eyelid swelling, pale papillary reaction on tarsal conjunctiva[cite: 1]. Vision normal, NO pain[cite: 1].

Diagnosis: Clinical; Conjunctival scrapings reveal Eosinophils[cite: 1].

Treatment: Allergen avoidance, cold compresses (causes vasoconstriction), topical antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, artificial tears; topical steroids reserved for severe refractory cases[cite: 1].

🏜️ Dry Eye Syndrome (K. Sicca)

Pathophysiology: Tear deficiency or tear film dysfunction common in elderly[cite: 1].

Symptoms: Gritty/sandy sensation, eye fatigue, mild redness, no pain, no discharge (or scant white stringy)[cite: 1]. Aggravated by wind, air conditioning, electric fans[cite: 1].

Diagnostic Tests:

  • Tear Break-Up Time (TBUT): <10 seconds indicates unstable tear film[cite: 1].
  • Schirmer's Test: Filter paper <5 mm wetting in 5 minutes confirms tear deficiency[cite: 1].

Treatment: Artificial tears/lubricants; Punctal occlusion for severe cases[cite: 1].

🩸 Subconjunctival Hemorrhage

Etiology: Rupture of small superficial conjunctival blood vessels[cite: 1]. Causes: Idiopathic, minor trauma, Valsalva maneuvers (severe coughing, sneezing, straining), uncontrolled HTN, or antiplatelet/anticoagulant drugs (Aspirin, Warfarin)[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Sudden onset of sharply demarcated, bright red, flat blood patch under bulbar conjunctiva[cite: 1]. Normal vision, completely painless, no discharge[cite: 1].

Treatment: Reassurance! Self-limiting; completely reabsorbs spontaneously within 10 to 14 days[cite: 1]. Artificial tears can be given to relieve anxiety[cite: 1].

☀️ Pterygium & Pinguecula

Pinguecula: Yellowish-white, amorphous subepithelial elastotic deposit in interpalpebral bulbar conjunctiva (nasal > temporal)[cite: 1]. Does NOT encroach on cornea[cite: 1].

Pterygium ("Pugita sa Mata"): Triangular, wing-shaped fibrovascular growth originating from limbal conjunctiva that ENCROACHES ONTO THE CORNEA[cite: 1].

Risk Factors: Chronic UV sunlight exposure, dust, wind, equatorial regions (common in farmers, fishermen)[cite: 1].

Treatment: Sunglasses, lubricants[cite: 1]. Surgical excision with conjunctival autograft + Mitomycin-C if visually obstructive[cite: 1].

⚠️ COMMON PITFALL - CONJUNCTIVITIS MANAGEMENT
Do NOT prescribe topical corticosteroids for undiagnosed conjunctivitis or red eye! If the cause is herpes simplex viral keratitis or a bacterial corneal ulcer, steroids can cause corneal perforation and permanent blindness![cite: 1]

Corneal Disorders & Keratitis

Corneal Abrasion, Foreign Body Injury, and Vision-Threatening Microbial Keratitis.

🔪 Corneal Abrasion & Foreign Body

Pathophysiology: Traumatic sloughing of corneal epithelium (fingernails, paper, tree branches, contact lenses) exposing free nerve endings of CN V1[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Sudden severe sharp ocular pain, tearing, marked foreign body sensation, photophobia, red eye[cite: 1]. Pain worsens dramatically with blinking[cite: 1].

Diagnostic Confirmation: Fluorescein Dye Staining[cite: 1]. Disrupted epithelial defects stain bright GREEN under cobalt blue light[cite: 1].

Upper Lid Eversion: Always evert the upper lid if a foreign body sensation persists without obvious lesion to rule out trapped tarsal foreign body[cite: 1].

⭐ ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS IN ABRASION
  1. NO Topical Anesthetic Drops for Home Use: Causes severe corneal epithelial toxicity, non-healing ulcers, and corneal melting/perforation![cite: 1]
  2. NO Topical Steroids: Delays epithelial wound healing and predisposes to secondary fungal/bacterial superinfections![cite: 1]

Treatment: Prophylactic topical antibiotics, lubricating drops/ointment, bandage contact lens for pain control. Heals within 24-48 hours[cite: 1]. Foreign bodies removed via 30G needle under slit-lamp[cite: 1].

🚨 Microbial Keratitis (Corneal Ulcer)

EMERGENCY

Etiologies:

  • Bacterial: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (classic in contact lens wearers!), Staphylococcus, Streptococcus[cite: 1].
  • Fungal: Aspergillus, Fusarium (trauma with vegetable matter/plants)[cite: 1].
  • Parasitic: Acanthamoeba (contact lenses washed in dirty/tap water)[cite: 1].
  • Viral: Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV - dendritic ulcer), VZV[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Moderate to severe pain, photophobia, blurred vision, mucopurulent discharge, ciliary flush[cite: 1].

Slit-Lamp Key Finding: Distinct white/yellow focal corneal opacity or infiltrate (ulcer) with surrounding corneal edema and frequent Hypopyon (layered pus in AC)[cite: 1].

Workup: Immediate corneal scrapings for Gram stain, KOH/Giemsa, and Culture (Blood agar, Sabouraud)[cite: 1].

Treatment: Intensive loading dose of topical 4th-generation fluoroquinolones (Moxifloxacin/Levofloxacin) or fortified antibiotics (q15min for 1-2 hours, then q1h)[cite: 1]. Fungal = Natamycin[cite: 1]. Viral = Acyclovir[cite: 1]. Late permanent scarring requires Penetrating Keratoplasty (corneal transplant)[cite: 1].

Scleral, Uveal & Intraocular Disorders

Episcleritis, Scleritis, Anterior Uveitis, and Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma.

🔥 Episcleritis vs. Scleritis

Episcleritis: Benign, superficial inflammation of episclera[cite: 1]. Sectoral redness, mild discomfort, normal vision, no discharge[cite: 1].

Scleritis: Severe, destructive inflammation of entire scleral wall[cite: 1]. Diffuse purple-tinged redness, severe deep boring pain radiating to forehead/head that wakes patient at night[cite: 1]. Vision mildly/moderately reduced[cite: 1]. Strongly linked to systemic autoimmune diseases (Rheumatoid Arthritis, SLE, GPA/Wegener's, PAN)[cite: 1].

💡 THE PHENYLEPHRINE BLANCH TEST
Instill 1 drop of 2.5% Phenylephrine:
  • Episcleritis: Vasoconstricts superficial vessels → BLANCHES (WHITENS)[cite: 1].
  • Scleritis: Deep vessels unaffected → DOES NOT BLANCH[cite: 1].

👁️ Anterior Uveitis (Iridocyclitis)

Pathophysiology: Inflammation of iris and ciliary body[cite: 1]. ~50% idiopathic; strongly associated with HLA-B27 Seronegative Arthropathies (Ankylosing Spondylitis, IBD, Psoriatic Arthritis, Reactive Arthritis)[cite: 1].

Clinical Features: Ciliary flush, dull aching pain, marked photophobia (ciliary muscle spasm), blurred vision[cite: 1].

Physical Examination Signs:

  • Keratic Precipitates (KPs): Inflammatory cell clumps deposited on corneal endothelium (fine vs mutton-fat)[cite: 1].
  • Aqueous Flare & Cells: Protein scattering light in AC (Tyndall effect)[cite: 1].
  • Posterior Synechiae: Adhesions between iris and anterior lens capsule leading to an irregular miotic pupil[cite: 1].
  • Hypopyon: Layered white blood cells/pus in AC[cite: 1].

Treatment: Topical corticosteroids (Prednisolone acetate) + Mydriatic/Cycloplegic agents (Atropine, Cyclogyl) to break/prevent posterior synechiae and relieve ciliary spasm pain[cite: 1].

💥 Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma (AACG)

OPHTHALMIC EMERGENCY

Pathophysiology: Pupillary block in predisposed shallow angles (<20°) → aqueous trapped in posterior chamber → iris bows forward (iris bombé) → acute peripheral angle closure → rapid spike in IOP (>40–50 mmHg; normal: 10–21 mmHg)[cite: 1].

Risk Factors: Asian ethnicity, females > males (4:1), narrow anterior chambers, hyperopia, darkness/dim light (causes pupillary dilation, e.g., watching movie in dark theater)[cite: 1].

Clinical Presentation: Sudden severe unilateral ocular pain radiating to ipsilateral frontal head, nausea & vomiting (vagal stimulation), blurred vision, iridescent colored halos around lights (secondary to corneal edema)[cite: 1].

Physical Findings: Deep ciliary flush, hazy/edematous "steamy" cornea, mid-dilated non-reactive pupil, shallow AC (temporal light illuminates temporal iris but leaves nasal shadow = Eclipse Sign), globe feels rock-hard on digital palpation[cite: 1].

Diagnostic Workup

  • Applanation / Schiotz Tonometry: Confirms sky-high IOP (>40-50 mmHg)[cite: 1].
  • Gonioscopy: Gold standard visual assessment showing 360° closed angle[cite: 1].

Immediate Medical Management

  1. Acetazolamide: Oral/IV Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor[cite: 1].
  2. IV Mannitol: Hyperosmotic agent to dehydrate vitreous[cite: 1].
  3. Topical Beta-Blocker / Alpha-Agonist: Timolol, Brimonidine[cite: 1].
  4. Pilocarpine 2%: Miotic agent (effective only when IOP drops <40 mmHg)[cite: 1].

Definitive Surgical Treatment: Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) (creates a full-thickness hole in peripheral iris to relieve pupillary block) or Surgical Iridotomy[cite: 1].

Eyelid, Orbital & Lacrimal Apparatus Disorders

Eyelid malpositions, Preseptal vs Orbital Cellulitis, Epiphora, and Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction.

1. Eyelid Infections / Malpositions

Hordeolum (Stye): Acute focal painful nodule[cite: 1]. Internal (Meibomian gland infection); External (Zeiss or Moll gland infection)[cite: 1].

Blepharitis: Chronic inflammation of lid margins (Staphylococcal)[cite: 1].

Entropion: Inward turning of lid margin → lashes rub cornea → ulceration[cite: 1].

Ectropion: Outward turning of lid margin → corneal exposure & epiphora[cite: 1].

Trichiasis: Misdirected lashes rubbing cornea[cite: 1].

2. Lagophthalmos

Definition: Inability to completely close the eyelids[cite: 1].

Etiologies:

  • CN VII Palsy (Bell's Palsy): Paralysis of orbicularis oculi muscle[cite: 1].
  • Thyroid Eye Disease (Graves'): Proptosis and lid retraction[cite: 1].

Complications: Exposure keratopathy, drying, corneal ulceration, and blindness[cite: 1].

3. Cellulitis: Preseptal vs. Orbital

Preseptal Cellulitis: Infection anterior to orbital septum[cite: 1]. Lid edema, redness. Normal vision, normal pupils, NO proptosis, NO pain with eye movements[cite: 1].

Orbital Cellulitis: Infection posterior to orbital septum (often from ethmoid sinusitis)[cite: 1]. Life/vision threat! Proptosis, painful & restricted EOM ("frozen eye"), fever, decreased vision[cite: 1].

💧 Congenital Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction (NLDO)

Etiology: Delayed recanalization / imperforate membrane at the Valve of Hasner at the distal NLD in newborns[cite: 1].

Clinical Presentation: Infant presents with chronic tearing (epiphora), sticky mucopurulent discharge, matting of eyelashes upon waking[cite: 1].

Physical Diagnostic Exam: Applying pressure over the medial canthus / lacrimal sac produces mucoid reflux from punctum[cite: 1].

Natural History & Management:

  • Spontaneous Resolution: 90-95% resolve spontaneously by age 12 months[cite: 1].
  • Conservative Tx: Crigler NLD massage (downward pressure over sac 4x/day) + topical antibiotic drops if infected[cite: 1].
  • Surgical Probing: Indicated if non-resolving by 12 to 18 months of age (90% cure on 1st probe)[cite: 1].

🧪 Diagnostic Investigations for Epiphora

  • Dye Disappearance Test (DDT): Instill fluorescein into cul-de-sac. Dye retention >5 minutes confirms lacrimal drainage blockage[cite: 1].
  • Probing & Irrigation: Saline flushed via canaliculus cannula. Fluid reflux from opposite punctum confirms obstruction[cite: 1].
  • Palpation of Lacrimal Sac: Direct pressure yields purulent reflux in Dacryocystitis[cite: 1].
  • Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR): Surgical bypass procedure connecting lacrimal sac directly to nasal cavity (External, Endoscopic transnasal [no facial scar], or Laser DCR)[cite: 1].

🎯 HIGH-YIELD BUZZWORDS

  • Ciliary Flush: Perilimbal deep redness; danger sign of keratitis/uveitis/glaucoma[cite: 1].
  • Valve of Hasner: Site of distal NLD imperforation in congenital tearing[cite: 1].
  • Mutton-fat KPs: Large granulomatous Keratic Precipitates on endothelium[cite: 1].
  • Hypopyon: Layered white blood cell pus in anterior chamber[cite: 1].
  • Eclipse Sign: Temporal light shadow on nasal iris indicating shallow AC[cite: 1].
  • Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI): Definitive cure for pupillary block glaucoma[cite: 1].
  • Schirmer Test <5mm: Diagnostic cutoff for tear deficiency dry eye[cite: 1].
  • Pugita sa Mata: Pterygium encroaching on cornea from UV exposure[cite: 1].

🚫 ABSOLUTE CLINICAL DON'TS

  • NEVER prescribe topical steroids for undiagnosed red eyes or keratitis![cite: 1]
  • NEVER give topical anesthetics to patients for home use (causes corneal melting)![cite: 1]
  • NEVER give Pilocarpine during acute glaucoma attack if IOP >40 mmHg (iris sphincter is ischemic)![cite: 1]
  • NEVER perform surgery on congenital NLDO in infants <12 months (90-95% resolve with massage)![cite: 1]
