1. High-Yield Overview & Epidemiology

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Master the physiological transition from intra- to extrauterine life.
  • Identify high-risk maternal/fetal factors causing neonatal sickness.
  • Execute step-by-step Neonatal Resuscitation according to standard algorithms.
  • Differentiate extracranial vs. intracranial cranial hemorrhages.
  • Understand pathophysiology, clinical staging, and management of HIE, PVL, and Neonatal Seizures.
  • Diagnose and manage major respiratory distress etiologies (RDS, BPD, MAS, TTN, Pneumonia, Pneumothorax, CDH, Apnea).
  • Recognize and treat cardiovascular emergencies (PPHN, Cyanotic CHDs, PDA).

📌 Core Neonatal Definitions

  • Neonatal Period: First 28 days of life after birth.
  • Early Neonatal Period: First 7 days of life (0–6 days).
  • Late Neonatal Period: 8th to 28th day of life (7–27 days).
  • Perinatal Period: 28th week AOG (28 weeks) to 7th postpartum day.
  • Sick Newborn: An infant presenting with lethargy, systemic illness, poor weight gain, or requiring extra-uterine support (O2, IV fluids, meds).

📊 Global Neonatal Mortality Statistics (WHO Data)

The neonatal period represents the most vulnerable stage for child survival. In 2021, 2.3 million children died in their first month of life (~6,400 daily deaths), accounting for 47% of all under-5 deaths worldwide.

⭐ MUST KNOW: Primary Causes of Death by Stage
  • Early Neonatal Period (0–6 days): Preterm birth complications and intrapartum-related events (Asphyxia) dominate.
  • Late Neonatal Period (7–27 days): Infections (Sepsis, Pneumonia, Meningitis) and Congenital anomalies predominate.
  • Rank Order of Global Neonatal Mortality: Prematurity > Intrapartum-related complications (Asphyxia) > Serious Infections (Sepsis) > Congenital Anomalies.

2. Neonatal Resuscitation

💡 CLINICAL PEARL: Adult vs. Neonatal Arrest Pathophysiology

Adult cardiac arrest is predominantly cardiac/circulatory in origin (arrhythmias, ischemia). Neonatal arrest is almost exclusively secondary to respiratory failure / inadequate ventilation leading to severe hypoxemia and bradycardia. Therefore, establishing effective ventilation is the single most vital step in neonatal resuscitation!

⏱️ The Golden Minute & Resuscitation Flow

Golden Minute Goal: Complete initial evaluation and establish effective positive pressure ventilation (PPV) within the first 60 seconds of life for non-breathing/apneic or bradycardic infants.

⭐ MUST KNOW: MR. SOPA Ventilation Corrective Steps

When PPV fails to produce chest movement or improve heart rate, execute MR. SOPA immediately:

  • M – Mask adjustment: Reapply mask for tight seal.
  • R – Reposition head: Place in "sniffing" position (slightly extended).
  • S – Suction mouth & nose: Clear thick secretions or mucus.
  • O – Open mouth: Lift jaw, open mouth slightly.
  • P – Pressure increase: Increase pressure in 5–10 cmH2O increments (max 40 cmH2O).
  • A – Alternate airway: Insert Endotracheal Tube (ETT) or Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA).

🫀 Chest Compressions Protocol

  • Indication: HR < 60 bpm despite 30 seconds of effective PPV with chest movement.
  • Compression-to-Ventilation Ratio: 3:1 (3 compressions : 1 breath).
  • Rate: 90 compressions + 30 breaths = 120 events/minute.
  • Oxygen Concentration: Increase FiO2 to 100% during compressions.
  • Technique: Two-thumb encircle technique over lower third of sternum.
  • Re-evaluation Duration: Continue compressions for 60 seconds before re-assessing HR.

💉 Epinephrine & Volume Expansion

  • Indication for Epinephrine: HR remains < 60 bpm after at least 60 seconds of coordinated compressions and 100% PPV.
  • Route: IV/IO preferred (via Emergency Umbilical Venous Catheter - UVC). ETT route while awaiting IV access.
  • Concentration: 1:10,000 (0.1 mg/mL).
  • Persistent Bradycardia: Consider underlying causes: Pneumothorax or Hypovolemia.
  • Volume Expander: Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) or O-negative PRBCs at 10 mL/kg over 5–10 mins.

⚠️ COMMON PITFALL: Resuscitation Errors
  • Assigning APGAR scores before initiating resuscitation in a limp, cyanotic, apneic infant. Resuscitation MUST start immediately!
  • Hyper-extending or flexing the neck (causes airway occlusion; maintain neutral sniffing position).
  • Suctioning deep pharynx vigorously (triggers vasovagal response causing severe bradycardia).
  • Using 100% O2 routinely at birth for preterms (increases oxidative stress and tissue damage).

B. Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH) & Periventricular Leukomalacia (PVL)

IVH Pathophysiology: Primarily affects preterm infants (<1,500g, <32 weeks AOG). Bleeding originates in the subependymal germinal matrix (GM), a highly vascular gelatinous region producing neuroblasts and oligodendrocytes. The blood vessels here have poor basement membrane support, lack structural tissue, and feature high fibrinolytic activity.

Pressure-Passive State: Preterm infants lack cerebral vascular autoregulation. Pathologic fluctuations in cerebral blood flow velocity (caused by RDS, pneumothorax, intubation, suctioning, hypercapnia, acidosis) trigger vessel rupture.

Table 3.2: Papile Grading System for Intraventricular Hemorrhage

Grade Ultrasound Findings / Description Incidence (%) Prognosis & Complications
Grade I Hemorrhage confined within Subependymal Germinal Matrix or <10% of lateral ventricle. ~35% Good prognosis; low risk of neurodevelopmental deficits.
Grade II Intraventricular blood filling 10% – 50% of lateral ventricle WITHOUT ventricular dilatation. ~40% Favorable prognosis; mild risk of neurodeficit.
Grade III Intraventricular blood filling >50% of lateral ventricle WITH ventricular dilatation. ~15% High risk of Post-Hemorrhagic Ventricular Dilatation (PHVD) & Hydrocephalus.
Grade IV Parenchymal extension of hemorrhage (Periventricular Hemorrhagic Infarction - PVHI). ~10% Worst prognosis (~70% neuroimpairment/CP); severe cognitive/motor deficits.
🧠 EXAM TIP: Post-Hemorrhagic Ventricular Dilatation (PHVD)

PHVD occurs in 30–50% of Grade III/IV IVH due to CSF flow obstruction by blood clots. Monitor with weekly head circumference measurements. A head growth of >1 cm/week with bulging fontanelles and separated sutures demands urgent cranial ultrasonography! Interventions include Ventriculoperitoneal Shunt (VPS) placement, Reservoir/EVD, or Ventriculosubgaleal shunt.

🧠 Periventricular Leukomalacia (PVL)

  • Definition: Focal necrotic lesions in periventricular white matter (the watershed area between arterial territories).
  • Pathogenesis: Ischemia and free-radical damage select oligodendrocyte precursors (which myelinate axons from 23–32 weeks AOG).
  • Motor Tract Affected: Descending Corticospinal Tracts pass through periventricular white matter.
  • Classic Sequela: Spastic Diplegia (Cerebral Palsy) — lower extremity motor involvement greater than upper extremity.
  • Ultrasound Appearance: Initial "flare/blush" echogenicity in 1st week → Cystic lesions superolateral to lateral ventricles within 3 weeks → Ventriculomegaly/ex-vacuo loss at term.

C. Perinatal Asphyxia & Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE)

Perinatal Asphyxia: Impaired oxygenation and perfusion to vital organs. Triggers the Diving Reflex (transiently shunts blood to brain, heart, and adrenals away from lungs, kidneys, gut, skin).

Table 3.3: Multiorgan Target Systemic Effects of Asphyxia

Organ System Frequency (%) Clinical Manifestations & Pathology
Renal System 50% (#1 Target) Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN), oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h), gross hematuria, proteinuria, elevated creatinine.
Central Nervous System 28% HIE, cerebral edema, seizures, intra-parenchymal infarction, hypotonia/hypertonia.
Cardiovascular System 25% Transient myocardial ischemia, poor contractility, heart failure, tricuspid regurgitation, hypotension.
Pulmonary System 23% Pulmonary edema, ARDS (plasma protein surfactant inactivation), PPHN, pulmonary hemorrhage.

🔥 Pathophysiology: Primary vs. Secondary Energy Failure

  • Primary Energy Failure (0–6 Hours): Hypoxia/Ischemia → Anaerobic metabolism → ATP depletion → Failure of Na+/K+-ATPase pumps → Cellular depolarization → Massive release of toxic extracellular Glutamate → Excessive Ca2+ & Na+ influx → Acute cellular swelling & Necrosis.
  • Latent Period (Up to 6 Hours): Brief recovery of cellular energy. Golden window for Therapeutic Hypothermia!
  • Secondary Energy Failure (6–48 Hours): Mitochondrial failure, neuroinflammation, free radical oxidative stress → Delayed neuronal Apoptosis.

💡 CLINICAL PEARL: Therapeutic Hypothermia

Therapeutic hypothermia (head cooling or whole-body cooling to 33.5°C for 72 hours) initiated within the first 6 hours of life reduces mortality and neurodevelopmental disability in moderate-to-severe HIE by decreasing apoptosis and oxidative stress!

D. Neonatal Seizures & Birth Injuries

⚡ Types of Neonatal Seizures

  • Subtle Seizures (Automatisms): Most common type in both term & preterm! Manifests as ocular deviations, blinking, lip smacking, pedaling, rowing, bicycling, apnea, or autonomic changes.
  • Clonic Seizures: Rhythmic jerking. Focal (one limb, focal brain lesion) or Multifocal (migrates limb to limb; mostly term infants).
  • Tonic Seizures: Sustained posture/stiffening of limbs. Most common in preterms with severe IVH.
  • Myoclonic Seizures: Single or multiple lightning-fast flexor jerks.
  • #1 Etiology: Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) causes >50% of neonatal seizures.

💪 Birth Nerve Injuries Summary

  • Erb-Duchenne Paralysis (C5–C6): Upper plexus injury. "Waiter's tip" posture (adduction, internal rotation, forearm pronation). Moro reflex absent; Hand grasp INTACT.
  • Klumpke Paralysis (C8–T1): Lower plexus injury. Claw hand posture. Hand grasp absent. Associated with Ipsilateral Horner Syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) due to T1 sympathetic chain involvement.
  • Phrenic Nerve Palsy (C3–C5): Causes diaphragmatic paralysis, breath sound reduction, elevated hemidiaphragm on CXR, mediastinal shift.
  • Facial Nerve Injury: Peripheral affects entire side of face (including forehead/eyelid; 90% resolve). Central spares forehead and eyelid.

⚠️ COMMON PITFALL: Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia Emergency

In a newborn presenting with respiratory distress and a scaphoid abdomen, NEVER perform bag-mask ventilation! Bag-mask ventilation inflates the stomach and bowel loops within the thoracic cavity, causing rapid lung compression and fatal tension pneumothorax. Intubate immediately and place an OGT to decompress the stomach!

5. Cardiovascular Diseases in the Sick Newborn

A. Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN)

PPHN is characterized by failure of the normal postnatal decrease in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), leading to persistent right-to-left shunting across the Patent Foramen Ovale (PFO) and Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA), resulting in severe, labile hypoxemia.

⚡ Etiological Mechanisms of PPHN

  • Maladaptation: Structurally normal vessels with severe vasoconstriction due to parenchymal lung disease (MAS, RDS, pneumonia) or sepsis/hypothermia.
  • Maldevelopment: Hypertrophied vascular smooth muscle bed due to chronic intrauterine hypoxia or maternal intake of SSRIs or NSAIDs/COX-inhibitors (causes premature ductal closure).
  • Underdevelopment: Hypoplastic pulmonary vascular bed secondary to Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH) or Potter sequence.
  • Obstruction: Vascular bed occlusion due to polycythemia or TAPVR.

🩺 Clinical Manifestations & Diagnosis

  • Term or post-term infant with severe, labile cyanosis within 24h of life.
  • Auscultation: Single or narrowly split S2 with a loud P2 component.
  • Differential Cyanosis: Preductal SpO2 (Right Hand) is >5% – 10% higher than Postductal SpO2 (Feet).
  • Echocardiogram (Gold Standard): Shows elevated RV pressures, flattening/leftward bowing of interventricular septum, tricuspid regurgitation, and right-to-left shunt across PDA/PFO.
  • Treatment: Gentle ventilation, Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO) (selective pulmonary vasodilator), Sildenafil, Bosentan, ECMO.

B. Congenital Heart Diseases (CHD) & CCHD Screening

⭐ MUST KNOW: High-Yield CHD Rankings
  • Most Common CHD Overall: Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD).
  • Most Common Cyanotic CHD Beyond Neonatal Period: Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF).
  • Most Common Cyanotic CHD in First Week of Life: Transposition of the Great Arteries (TGA).
  • Most Common Cardiac Cause of Mortality in First Week of Life: Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS) [2nd most common cyanotic CHD in 1st week].
  • The 5 T's of Cyanotic CHD: Truncus arteriosus, Transposition of Great Arteries, Tricuspid atresia, Tetralogy of Fallot, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR).

C. Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)

  • Normal Course: Physiologic closure within 12–15 hours post-birth; anatomic closure in weeks. Pathologic if open >72 hours.
  • Prevalence: Inversely proportional to GA (<30 weeks AOG ~ 50%).
  • Clinical Presentation: Bounding peripheral pulses, wide pulse pressure, continuous "machinery-like" murmur at left infraclavicular border.
  • Preterm Complications: Left-to-right shunt causes pulmonary overperfusion (pulmonary hemorrhage, BPD) and systemic steal (NEC, intraventricular hemorrhage, renal failure).
  • Medical Closure Management: Fluid restriction + IV Ibuprofen, Indomethacin, or Paracetamol (Acetaminophen). Surgical ligation if medical therapy fails.

🎯 High-Yield Fast Numbers

  • DO perform MR. SOPA if chest is not moving during PPV.
  • DO give Caffeine Citrate for Apnea of Prematurity.
  • DO perform weekly head circumference in infants with severe IVH.
