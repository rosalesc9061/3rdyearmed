1. Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs) & Transmission

Core Section I

Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs), formerly called nosocomial infections, refer to infections acquired during hospitalization or in other healthcare settings that were not present or incubating at the time of admission. Approximately 3% to 5% of pediatric patients acquire an HAI during their hospital stay.

A. Susceptibility Factors in Children

  • Host Factors: Anatomic abnormalities, abnormal skin or breaks in skin barrier, organ dysfunction, severe malnutrition, or underlying chronic comorbidities.
  • Recent Invasive Procedures: Breaches normal anatomic host protective barriers, allowing direct microbial access.
  • Catheters and Devices: Provide direct access to sterile anatomic sites for minimally pathogenic organisms (e.g., Staphylococcus epidermidis and other coagulase-negative Staphylococci). Devices like Nasogastric Tubes (NGT) disrupt normal protective mucus flow.
  • Prolonged Antibiotic Use: Alters normal protective bowel flora, encouraging overgrowth and multiplication of invasive pathogens present in small numbers in the gut (e.g., Clostridium difficile, Salmonella spp.).

Reconstructed Figure 1: Routes of Infection Transmission in Healthcare Settings

Close Contact

Direct inhalation or spray of airborne/droplet viruses during coughing, sneezing, talking.

Airborne Nuclei

Evaporation leaves small nuclei (≤5 μm) suspended in air currents over long distances/time.

Fomite / Contact

Surface deposition of pathogens → transferred via unwashed hands to nose/mouth touch.

Clinical Significance: The hands of healthcare workers represent the single most common and important route of hospital pathogen transmission.

⭐ Hand Hygiene & The 5 Moments (INICC Benchmark)

A 15-second friction scrub with soap and water removes the majority of transient surface flora. Friction is essential, especially when hands are visibly soiled with blood or bodily fluids. Alcohol hand rubs are acceptable when hands are not visibly soiled.

The 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene:

  1. Before patient contact
  2. Before a clean or aseptic procedure (e.g., administering IV medications, dressing changes)
  3. After body fluid exposure risk (e.g., handling urine bags, diapers, blood samples)
  4. After patient contact
  5. After contact with patient surroundings (e.g., bed rails, monitors, tables)

Note (INICC Data): Data from the International Nosocomial Infection Control Consortium shows that physicians are the least compliant healthcare discipline in performing hand hygiene!

🛡️ Isolation Precautions & Transmission-Based Bundles

STOP: Contact Precautions

Requirements: Hand hygiene before/after, Gown + Gloves before room entry. Discard before exit. Dedicated equipment.

Indications: C. difficile, MRSA, ESBL+, Varicella/Zoster lesions, RSV/respiratory viruses, infectious diarrhea in diapered patients, undrained abscesses, Enterovirus meningitis, Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers.

STOP: Droplet Precautions

Requirements: Large particles (>5 μm), travel <3 feet. Gown, gloves, mask, eye/face shield. Patient wears mask during transport. Cohort >6ft if single room unavailable.

Indications: Meningococcemia (N. meningitidis), Pertussis, Group A Streptococcus, Influenza, Adenovirus, Parainfluenza.

STOP: Airborne Precautions

Requirements: Droplet nuclei (≤5 μm). Fit-tested N95 or higher respirator, negative-pressure room (6–12 air changes/hr), door MUST remain closed.

Mnemonic (MTVS): Measles, Tuberculosis, Varicella, SARS-CoV-2 (also MERS-CoV, Avian Flu).

F. Prevention Measures for Neonatal Device-Associated Infections

Device-Associated Infection Anatomic / Pathophysiologic Context Evidence-Based Prevention Bundle
CLABSI
(Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection)		 Central catheters inserted via IJV, subclavian vein, or Umbilical Vein Catheter (UVC in neonates) leading to right atrium. Biofilm formation by S. epidermidis or GNB. • Hand hygiene & maximal sterile barriers during insertion.
• >0.5% Chlorhexidine with alcohol skin prep (term infants >2 wks; plain alcohol if <2 wks).
• Change transparent dressing every 7 days, gauze every 3 days (or when damp/soiled).
• Scrub needleless hub/cap with disinfectant before every entry.
• Daily review and prompt removal when line no longer needed.
VAP
(Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia)		 Endotracheal intubation bypasses upper airway defenses, allowing microaspiration of contaminated secretions into the lower respiratory tract. • Elevate head of bed 15° to 30°.
• Oral care with antiseptic solution or sterile water.
• Hand hygiene & gloves when manipulating ETT or secretions.
• Frequently drain condensate away from circuit; change circuit ONLY when visibly soiled or malfunctioning.
• Daily assessment for extubation readiness.
CA-UTI
(Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection)		 Indwelling Foley catheter provides intraluminal and extraluminal pathways for periurethral bacteria to ascend into the bladder. • Insert using strict sterile/aseptic technique.
• Maintain an uninterrupted closed drainage system.
• Keep drainage bag below bladder level without touching the floor.
• Daily review and prompt catheter removal.

2. Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) & Employee Health

Core Section II

Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) is the ability of microorganisms causing disease to withstand attack by antimicrobial agents. Antibiotic use among children in daycare/childcare settings is 2 to 4 times higher than those cared for at home, with a mean treatment duration 4 times longer, dramatically increasing resistant flora in respiratory and intestinal tracts (e.g., S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, E. coli O157:H7, Shigella).

🧠 Board Exam Definitions of Antimicrobial Resistance Categories
Category Term Standardized Definition Clinical Vignette Example
MDR
Multidrug-Resistant		 Acquired non-susceptibility to at least ONE agent in 3 or more antimicrobial categories. Pseudomonas aeruginosa resistant to Amikacin (aminoglycoside), Ceftazidime (cephalosporin), and Meropenem (carbapenem). OR E. coli resistant to Ampicillin, Cefuroxime, and Gentamicin.
XDR
Extensively Drug-Resistant		 Non-susceptibility to at least one agent in ALL BUT TWO OR FEWER antimicrobial categories (resistant to almost everything; only 1 or 2 drug classes remain active). Acinetobacter baumannii resistant to all antibiotic classes except Colistin and Tigecycline.
PDR
Pan-Drug Resistant		 Non-susceptibility to ALL agents in ALL antimicrobial categories. No viable antibiotic options remain. Isolate demonstrating in vitro resistance to every tested antibiotic class.

3. Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO): Definitions & Breakdown

Core Section III

Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO) is defined as fever (>38.0°C) lasting for more than 1 week (≥8 days), for which the cause could not be identified after 3 weeks of outpatient evaluation or 1 week of comprehensive evaluation in the hospital.

⚠️ Diagnostic Distinction: FUO vs. Fever Without Source (FWS)

Fever Without Source (FWS): Fever of recent onset (<1 week) with no adequate explanation and no apparent anatomic site of infection after thorough history and physical exam. The vast majority of FWS cases in children are self-limiting viral infections.

Key Distinction: FUO requires a prolonged duration (>1–3 weeks) and an extensive diagnostic workup, whereas FWS is acute and managed with risk-stratification algorithms.

Four Clinical Categories of FUO

FUO Category Diagnostic Criteria Most Common Etiologies
Classic FUO >38.0°C for >3 weeks outpatient or >1 week inpatient. Infections (most common, 59% bacterial), Connective Tissue Diseases, Malignancy.
Healthcare-Associated FUO ≥38.0°C for ≥1 week in hospitalized patient, not present or incubating on admission. HAIs (CLABSI, VAP, UTI), C. difficile enterocolitis, septic thrombophlebitis, drug fever (resolves 48–72h after drug cessation).
Immunodeficient FUO ≥38.0°C for >1 week with negative cultures after 48 hours. Infections (bacterial, fungal, viral); documented in only 40–60% of cases.
HIV-Related FUO >38.0°C for >3 weeks outpatient or >1 week inpatient in confirmed HIV infection. Primary HIV, opportunistic pathogens (Mycobacteria, P. jirovecii, CMV, Toxoplasmosis, Cryptococcus), Lymphomas, IRIS.

🦠 Detailed Analysis of Infectious Etiologies of FUO

🫁 1. Tuberculosis (TB) & Pediatric Diagnostic Criteria ▼

Tuberculosis remains a primary infectious cause of FUO in developing countries like the Philippines. Diagnostic criteria for children (<15 years old) are stratified into Presumptive vs. Active TB:

Presumptive TB (≥3 of 6 Clinical Criteria):
  • Coughing or wheezing of ≥2 weeks duration
  • Unexplained fever of ≥2 weeks duration
  • Weight loss, failure to gain weight, weight faltering, or loss of appetite
  • Failure to respond to 2 weeks of appropriate antibiotics for LRTI
  • Failure to regain previous state of health 2 weeks after viral infection/exanthema
  • Fatigue, reduced playfulness, or lethargy
Active TB (≥3 of 5 Master Domains):
  • Epidemiologic: Exposure to an adult/adolescent with active TB
  • Immunologic: Positive TST or IGRA
  • Clinical: Signs and symptoms consistent with TB
  • Radiologic: Suggestive CXR (hilar adenopathy, cavitary lesion, miliary seed, effusion)
  • Laboratory: Positive AFB smear, GeneXpert, culture, or suggestive histology
💡 Tuberculin Skin Test (TST) & IGRA Pearls

• Standard Positive Cutoff: Induration ≥10 mm regardless of BCG status.

• Positive Cutoff at ≥5 mm: Severely malnourished children, immunocompromised, HIV-infected, close contact with active TB source, or CXR consistent with prior untreated TB.

• Window Period: 2 to 12 weeks between exposure and skin test reactivity. Repeat TST/IGRA at 3 months if initial test is negative but suspicion remains high.

• IGRA vs. TST: IGRA measures IFN-γ generation by T-cells; does NOT cross-react with BCG vaccine. Preferred in BCG-vaccinated children ≥2 years. TST is preferred in children <2 years old.

• TB Meningitis Cranial CT Triad: 1) Tuberculoma, 2) Basal meningeal enhancement, 3) Ventricular dilatation (hydrocephalus).

🦠 2. Enteric (Typhoid) Fever & Cat-Scratch Disease ▼

Enteric / Typhoid Fever (Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi)

  • Clinical Features: Step-ladder fever, headache, abdominal pain, hepatosplenomegaly, leukopenia, and relative bradycardia (temperature-pulse dissociation / Faget sign).
  • Rose Spots: Transient, salmon-pink macules seen on the trunk.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Blood culture is the GOLD STANDARD (highest yield in 1st week). Stool and urine cultures become positive after the 1st week.
  • Widal Test Warning: Widal test is OBSOLETE due to low sensitivity, low specificity, and extremely high rate of false positives.

Cat-Scratch Disease (Bartonella henselae)

  • Transmission: Scratches or bites from cats (and occasionally dogs), or kitten flea contact.
  • Hallmark: Chronic regional lymphadenitis appearing 1 to 4 weeks after exposure.
  • FUO Presentation: Disseminated illness occurs in ~14% of patients, presenting with prolonged high fever lasting several weeks and hepato-splenic granulomas.
🔴 3. Viral Infections, Kawasaki Disease, & Malignancies ▼

Viral Pathogens: CMV (Cytomegalovirus) and EBV (Infectious Mononucleosis) are the primary viral etiologies of FUO. CMV can present as encephalitis, retinitis, pneumonia, or colitis, but may present solely as prolonged fever without organ symptoms.

⭐ Kawasaki Disease: Diagnostic Mnemonic (CRASH and BURN)

Kawasaki disease is a systemic vasculitis of medium-sized arteries with a strong predilection for the coronary arteries (risk of coronary aneurysms).

  • C – Conjunctivitis: Bilateral, non-exudative with limbal sparing.
  • R – Rash: Polymorphous, morbilliform, macular, or papular.
  • A – Adenopathy: Cervical, usually unilateral, >1.5 cm in diameter.
  • S – Strawberry Tongue / Mucosal: Erythematous, cracked, or peeling lips, strawberry tongue.
  • H – Hands & Feet: Extremity changes (edema, erythema of palms/soles, periungual desquamation).
  • BURN – Fever: High fever lasting for 5 or more days duration.

Pediatric Malignancies: Leukemia and Lymphoma represent the most common malignancies presenting as FUO in children. Other causes include Neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, Hepatoma, Sarcomas, and Atrial Myxoma.

⚠️ Principles of Antimicrobial Therapy in FUO

Avoid empirical trials of antibiotics! Empirical antibiotics alter bacterial culture yields, obscure clinical signs, delay definitive diagnosis, and promote drug resistance.

Strict Exceptions (Only 2 Indications for Empirical Antimicrobials):

  1. Culture-negative Infective Endocarditis with hemodynamic instability
  2. High clinical and epidemiological suspicion for Disseminated / Miliary Tuberculosis

📋 Clinical Case Analysis: R.J. (9-Month-Old Male)

Synchronous Case Study

History: R.J., a 9-month-old male, was brought to the ER due to a 4-week history of fever. Fever occurred mostly in the afternoon and night, temporarily relieved by paracetamol. 3 weeks PTA, CBC, urinalysis, stool exam, and CXR were normal. He was given a 7-day oral antibiotic course without improvement. He became increasingly irritable, with decreased oral intake, weight loss, and occasional cough.

ER Evaluation & Diagnostics:

  • CBC: Unremarkable | CRP: 10 mg/L | Urinalysis: Normal
  • Blood & Urine Cultures: Negative
  • Chest X-ray: Suspicious infiltrates in perihilar area with hilar lymphadenopathy
  • Tuberculin Skin Test (TST): 15 mm induration
💡 Case Rationale & Clinical Reasoning

1. Does R.J. meet FUO criteria? YES (>38.0°C for >1 week / ≥8 days, lasting 4 weeks).

2. Working Impression: Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB).

3. Criteria Met: Meets Active TB criteria (≥3 domains: Epidemiologic exposure, Immunologic [+TST 15mm], Clinical [fever, weight loss, cough], Radiologic [hilar lymphadenopathy]).

4. Unique Feature in Children: Pediatric TB presents with protean systemic manifestations (fever, weight loss, lethargy) and is paucibacillary (low bacterial load, non-infectious to others, but higher risk of rapid dissemination in the child).

⚡ Bullet High-Yield Rapid Review

  • Hand Hygiene Friction: 15-second friction scrub is the most important component of handwashing.
  • CLABSI Skin Antisepsis: >0.5% Chlorhexidine with alcohol for term infants >2 wks. Plain alcohol for <2 wks.
  • VAP Prevention: Elevate head of bed 15–30°. Change ventilator circuit ONLY when visibly soiled or malfunctioning.
  • CA-UTI Prevention: Maintain closed drainage system; daily review for prompt removal.
  • Drug Fever Resolution: Resolves within 48 to 72 hours following drug discontinuation.
  • Widal Test Status: OBSOLETE due to low specificity and high false positives.
  • Typhoid Gold Standard: Blood culture (1st week); Stool/Urine culture (>1st week).
  • Empirical Antibiotics in FUO: STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED except for culture-negative endocarditis or high suspicion of disseminated TB.
