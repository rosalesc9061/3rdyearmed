🧪 Leptospirosis: Comprehensive Review

Leptospirosis is a major global zoonosis caused by pathogenic spirochetes of the genus Leptospira. In tropical regions like the Philippines, heavy downpours, typhoons, and inadequate drainage lead to frequent epidemics.

🔬 Bacterial Architecture & Classification

  • Order: Spirochaetales | Family: Leptospiraceae.
  • Morphology: Thin, highly motile, coiled spirochete measuring 6–20 µm long and 0.1 µm in diameter with characteristic hooked ends used for tissue penetration.
  • Motility: Two periplasmic flagella with polar extrusions from the cytoplasmic membrane.
  • Staining & Visualization: Stains poorly on Gram stain. Visible via Dark-field microscopy and silver impregnation staining of tissues.
  • Phylogenetic Classification: 64 species identified: 17 pathogenic, 21 intermediate, 26 non-pathogenic. Replaces old 2-species system (L. interrogans vs L. biflexa).
  • Sensitivities: Remains highly sensitive to Penicillins despite evolutionary shifts.

🐀 Animal Reservoirs & Serovars

Leptospires colonize the renal tubules of reservoir hosts and are excreted in urine.

⚡ Pathophysiology & Multi-Organ Damage Patterns

Entry via intact mucous membranes (conjunctival, oral) or broken skin/cuts
↓
Rapid hematogenous dissemination (Leptospiremic Phase: 3–10 days)
↓
Immune Evasion & Tissue Attachment (Endothelium, Kidney, Liver, Lungs, Brain)
↓
Widespread Endothelial Gap Junction Widening → Capillary Leakage & Platelet Consumption
↓
Multi-Organ Failure: Weil's Syndrome (Jaundice + AKI + Hemorrhage) or Severe Pulmonary Hemorrhage

Organ-Specific Pathology:

  • Kidneys: Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and interstitial nephritis. Inhibits renal tubular Na+ reabsorption leading to early non-oliguric hypokalemic renal insufficiency with marked urinary potassium and magnesium wasting (HypoMg is uniquely pathognomonic).
  • Liver: Hepatocyte apoptosis, focal necrosis, and plugging of bile canaliculi leading to cholestatic jaundice with markedly elevated bilirubin but only mild-to-moderate transaminase elevation.
  • Vascular System: Endothelial cell swelling without overt necrotizing vasculitis. Widespread capillary leak, petechiae, platelet aggregation on activated endothelium, leading to consumptive thrombocytopenia and DIC.
  • Lungs: Endothelial disruption in pulmonary capillaries → alveolar hemorrhage. Immunoglobulin and complement deposition found on alveolar walls.
💡 Clinical Pearl: The Iceberg Effect

The vast majority of Leptospirosis infections are asymptomatic or mild self-limiting febrile illnesses. Only ~1% of cases progress to severe, life-threatening forms (Weil's Syndrome or Severe Pulmonary Hemorrhage Syndrome).

⚠️ Common Pitfall: Slaughterhouse Workers

Slaughterhouse employees and abattoir workers can contract severe leptospirosis via direct contact with infected animal tissues/blood without any history of wading in floodwaters or living in flood-prone areas!

🩺 Clinical Spectrum: Mild vs. Severe Leptospirosis

Feature Mild Leptospirosis (99% of cases) Severe Leptospirosis / Weil's Syndrome (1%)
Onset & Course Abrupt onset, biphasic course (Leptospiremic → Immune phase). Spontaneous resolution in 7–10 days. Rapidly progressive, often monophasic and fulminant. High mortality (up to 50%).
Pathognomonic Signs Conjunctival suffusion (redness without purulent exudate), severe calf & back myalgia. Triad: Jaundice, Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), and Hemorrhagic diathesis.
Pulmonary Mild cough, clear lungs. Severe Pulmonary Hemorrhage Syndrome (SPHS), hemoptysis (often seen only post-intubation), ARDS.
Renal Normal or mild transient non-oliguric hypokalemia. Severe AKI, oliguria/anuria, azotemia, severe hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia.
Neurologic Frontal/retro-orbital headache, photophobia. Aseptic meningitis, delirium, altered mental status. Long-term post-leptospirosis depression.
Mortality Risk Factors Mortality < 1%. Age > 40 y/o, obesity, AKI, ARDS/respiratory failure, shock, cardiac arrhythmias.

💊 Treatment Guidelines & Post-Exposure Chemoprophylaxis

Antimicrobial Therapy:

  • Mild Disease (Oral):
    • First-line: Doxycycline 100 mg PO BID for 7 days.
    • Alternatives: Azithromycin 500 mg PO OD or Amoxicillin 500 mg PO TID.
    • Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction: May occur within hours of antibiotic initiation. Manage with supportive care, short-course corticosteroids, and antihistamines.
  • Severe Disease (Parenteral):
    • First-line: IV Penicillin G (1.5 million units IV q6h) initiated immediately.
    • Alternatives: IV Ceftriaxone (1 g IV q24h), IV Cefotaxime, or IV Doxycycline.
  • Ineffective Antibiotics: Vancomycin, Rifampicin, Metronidazole, Chloramphenicol.

🛡️ DOH Post-Exposure Chemoprophylaxis (PEP) Algorithm

🦟 Malaria: Parasite Biology, Pathophysiology & Management

Malaria remains a public health threat in specific Philippine provinces (e.g., Palawan). Caused by intraerythrocytic protozoa of the genus Plasmodium and transmitted exclusively by female Anopheles mosquitoes.

🧬 Biological Culprits & Life Cycle Dynamics

Plasmodium Species Spectrum:

  • Plasmodium falciparum: Most deadly species; responsible for almost all malaria deaths. Invades RBCs of all ages, causing ultra-high parasitemia levels (>2% to >20%).
  • Plasmodium vivax: Preferentially invades reticulocytes / young RBCs. Produces dormant liver forms (hypnozoites) causing relapses months to years later.
  • Plasmodium ovale: Invades young RBCs; also produces dormant hypnozoites.
  • Plasmodium malariae: Preferentially invades older RBCs. Long 72-hour cycle; can persist silently for decades.
  • Plasmodium knowlesi: Zoonotic monkey parasite in Southeast Asia. 24-hour cycle causing rapid, severe parasitemia.

Detailed Life Cycle Stages:

  1. Exo-erythrocytic (Hepatic) Cycle: Female Anopheles injects motile sporozoites into bloodstream → travel to liver → invade hepatocytes → replicate into 10,000–30,000 daughter merozoites per cell (schizogony). P. vivax/ovale form hypnozoites here.
  2. Erythrocytic (Blood) Cycle: Liver cells rupture → merozoites invade RBCs → Ring Form → Trophozoite → Schizont → RBC ruptures, releasing 6–30 merozoites to invade new RBCs. Synchronized rupture releases toxic metabolites triggering fever spikes.
  3. Gametogony: Some parasites differentiate into sexual forms: female & male gametocytes (4:1 female-to-male ratio). Gametocytes are the infective stage for mosquitoes!
  4. Sporogonic Cycle: Mosquito ingests gametocytes → fertilization in mosquito gut → Zygote → Ookinete → Oocyst on gut wall → releases Sporozoites that migrate to salivary glands.

💥 Pathophysiology of Severe Falciparum Malaria

Infected RBCs degrade hemoglobin into toxic heme, which is detoxified into inert brown-black crystals called HEMOZOIN (malaria pigment).

PfEMP1 & Cytoadherence Mechanism:

12–15 hours post-invasion, P. falciparum forms electron-dense protrusions called "knobs" on the RBC membrane. These knobs express PfEMP1 (P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1), an antigenically variant adhesive protein.

Target Organ Endothelial Receptor Bound by PfEMP1 Pathologic Result
Brain ICAM-1 & EPCR (Endothelial Protein C Receptor) Cerebral malaria, microvascular obstruction, coma
Placenta Chondroitin Sulfate B Placental sequestration, low birth weight, fetal death
Systemic Organs CD36 Widespread sequestration in visceral capillary beds

Microvascular Sequestration & Rosetting:

  • Cytoadherence: Parasitized RBCs stick to vascular endothelium.
  • Rosetting: Infected RBCs adhere to uninfected RBCs, forming clumps.
  • Agglutination: Infected RBCs adhere to other infected RBCs.
  • Consequence: Sequestered parasites mature out of reach of splenic clearance! Microvascular compromise, tissue hypoxia, lactic acidosis, and organ failure.
🧠 Exam Tip: Peripheral Parasitemia Underestimation

Because mature trophozoites and schizonts of P. falciparum are sequestered in deep organ capillaries, a peripheral blood smear only reveals early ring forms and gametocytes. Peripheral parasitemia significantly underestimates the total body parasite burden!

🧬 Human Genetic Protection Against Malaria

Genetic Trait / Condition Mechanism of Protection Against P. falciparum
Sickle Cell Trait (HbAS) 6-fold reduction in severe malaria mortality. Impairs parasite growth at low O2 tension; reduces PfEMP1 expression & cytoadherence.
G6PD Deficiency Increased oxidative stress in RBCs inhibits intracellular parasite survival. Protects against severe falciparum and vivax malaria.
Alpha-Thalassemia (Melanesia) Protects against severe disease and high parasitemia despite frequent childhood infections.
Melanesian Ovalocytosis Rigid erythrocyte membrane resists merozoite attachment and invasion.
Hemoglobin E & C (HbE, HbC) HbE reduces parasite multiplication at high densities; HbC reduces knob formation and cytoadherence.

🔬 Laboratory Diagnosis & Microscopy Techniques

Blood Smear Examination (Gold Standard):

  • Thick Blood Smear: Concentrates parasites by 40- to 100-fold. RBCs are lysed during staining. Used for parasite detection and calculating parasite density (parasites counted per 200 WBCs, assuming 8,000 WBCs/µL).
  • Thin Blood Smear: Fixed in methanol. Preserves RBC architecture. Used for species identification and calculating % parasitemia (% infected RBCs per 1,000 total RBCs).

Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs):

  • PfHRP2 (Histidine-Rich Protein 2): Specific for P. falciparum. Note: PfHRP2 persists in blood for 1–5 weeks after parasite clearance (can cause false positives post-treatment).
  • Plasmodium Lactate Dehydrogenase (pLDH) & Aldolase: Pan-species antigen targets used to detect non-falciparum species.
⭐ Diagnostic Cutoffs & Criteria
  • Critical Parasitemia Threshold: >2% parasitemia (>100,000 parasites/µL) indicates severe malaria requiring parenteral therapy!
  • CSF Findings in Cerebral Malaria: Opening pressure ~160 mmH2O, normal to slightly elevated protein (<1.0 g/L), normal cell count (<20 cells/µL). Lumbar puncture is essential to rule out bacterial meningitis!
  • Normal Platelet Count Exception: Mild-to-moderate thrombocytopenia is nearly universal in acute malaria. A completely normal platelet count should make you question the diagnosis of malaria!

⚠️ Primaquine Contraindications

Primaquine is strictly contraindicated in pregnant women (risk of fatal hemolysis in G6PD-deficient fetus) and infants under 6 months of age!

🦟 Arboviruses: Dengue, Chikungunya & Zika Virus Diseases

Arthropod-borne viruses (Arboviruses) transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes represent an escalating public health crisis affecting over 3.9 billion people globally.

🔴 Dengue Virus (DENV)

Family: Flaviviridae (Orthoflavivirus dengue, serotypes 1–4).

Hallmark: Microvascular hyperpermeability, plasma leakage, severe thrombocytopenia, hemoconcentration, and shock.

Secondary Infection Risk: Infection with a different serotype triggers Antibody-Dependent Enhancement (ADE), drastically increasing severe dengue risk.

🟢 Chikungunya Virus (CHIKV)

Family: Togaviridae (Alphavirus chikungunya).

Hallmark: Abrupt high fever and severe, incapacitating polyarthralgia/arthritis causing patients to stoop or crawl. Pain can persist for months/years.

No Plasma Leakage: Does not cause significant capillary leakage or severe bleeding.

🔵 Zika Virus (ZIKV)

Family: Flaviviridae (Orthoflavivirus zikaense).

Hallmark: Low-grade fever, pruritic maculopapular rash, non-purulent conjunctivitis, and peri-articular edema.

Unique Routes: Sexual contact, transplacental, blood transfusion.

Teratogenicity: Congenital Zika Syndrome (Microcephaly, cerebral calcifications, GBS).

📈 Course of Dengue Illness & Critical Phase Management

Dengue progresses through 3 distinct phases: Febrile Phase (Days 1–3), Critical Phase (Days 4–5), and Recovery Phase (Days 6–10).

Phase Days of Illness Clinical Features & Lab Changes Management Priorities
Febrile Phase Days 1–3 (up to Day 5) Abrupt high fever (40°C), severe retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, leukopenia, positive tourniquet test. Viraemia is high. Oral rehydration solutions (ORS), Paracetamol for fever. Avoid NSAIDs/Aspirin! Daily monitoring of CBC & vitals.
Critical Phase Days 4–5 (around Defervescence) Fever drops, BUT plasma leakage peaks! Rising Hematocrit with rapidly falling platelets. Risk of hypovolemic shock, pleural effusion, ascites, mucosal bleeding. Hospital admission for Warning Signs. Judicious IV isotonic crystalloids titrated to hematocrit and urine output (≥0.5–1 mL/kg/hr).
Recovery Phase Days 6–10 Extravascular fluid reabsorption, stabilization of vitals, hematocrit normalizes, platelet count gradually recovers. Convalescent rash ("islands of white in a sea of red"). Stop IV fluids to prevent hypervolemia & pulmonary edema! Monitor for fluid overload.

💧 Fluid Management Algorithm for Dengue Compensated Shock

Compensated Shock (Systolic BP maintained, but narrow pulse pressure <20 mmHg, tachycardia, delayed CRT)
↓
Initial Resuscitation: IV Isotonic Crystalloid 5–10 mL/kg/hr over 1 hour
↓
Reassess Clinical Response & Hematocrit (Hct)

✅ IF CLINICAL IMPROVEMENT:

Reduce IV fluids stepwise:

  • 5–7 mL/kg/hr for 1–2 hours →
  • 3–5 mL/kg/hr for 2–4 hours →
  • 2–3 mL/kg/hr for 2–4 hours →
  • Further reduce based on urine output. STOP IV fluids at 48 hours!

❌ IF NO CLINICAL IMPROVEMENT (Check Hct):

  • High / Rising Hct (Plasma Leakage): Administer 2nd IV fluid bolus 10–20 mL/kg/hr over 1 hour.
  • Low / Falling Hct (Occult/Overt Bleeding): Initiate urgent blood transfusion with Fresh Whole Blood or Packed RBCs!
⚠️ WHO Prophylactic Platelet Transfusion Guidelines

Platelet transfusion is NOT recommended for low platelet count alone—even if count drops below 20,000/µL—in the absence of severe active bleeding! Prophylactic platelet transfusions do NOT reduce bleeding risk and carry severe risks of fluid overload, transfusion reactions, and alloimmunization.
