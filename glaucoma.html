Glaucoma: Disease Definition & High-Yield Summary Must Know

Glaucoma is a progressive, neurodegenerative eye disease that results in visual field disturbance and irreversible blindness. It is characteristically associated with the death of retinal ganglion cells, characteristic optic nerve cupping, and a corresponding visual field defect respecting the horizontal meridian.

❗
Diagnostic Triad Rule (2 out of 3 Criteria): To establish a formal diagnosis of glaucoma, a patient must satisfy at least 2 out of the following 3 criteria (with changes being gradual and progressive):
  1. Abnormal Eye Pressure: IOP elevated beyond normal limits (Normal: 10 – 21 mmHg).
  2. Optic Nerve Damage: Characteristic structural loss (e.g., vCDR > 0.5, rim notching, ISNT rule violation).
  3. Visual Field Damage: Functional visual loss matching optic nerve structural loss (e.g., Bjerrum arcuate scotoma).

Glaucoma Suspects & Diagnostic Criteria Exam Tip

Glaucoma Suspects are individuals with no confirmed glaucoma but present with suspicious clinical findings that put them at risk of converting to definitive glaucoma:

  • No definitive glaucoma established yet.
  • Pressure suspiciously high for the individual eye (>21 mmHg).
  • Suspicious optic nerve damage (e.g., asymmetric cupping, vCDR > 0.5).
  • Suspicious visual field damage (e.g., early localized scotoma).
⚠️
Board Exam Trap (Formative Quiz #3): A patient with Normal IOP, Normal CDR (0.2), but a Nasal Step on Visual Field exam satisfies ONLY 1 criterion. They do NOT meet the 2 out of 3 rule required for a definitive glaucoma diagnosis or classification as a high-probability suspect without structural correlation!
Risk Factors Matrix: Modifiable vs Non-Modifiable Ratio 1:6

Modifiable Risk Factor (Only 1)

  • Intraocular Pressure (IOP): The single primary modifiable risk factor. Lowering IOP slows or halts progressive optic nerve degeneration.

Non-Modifiable Risk Factors (6+)

  • Family History: Blindness from glaucoma in first-degree relatives.
  • Refractive Status: High Myopia (POAG) vs High Hyperopia (PACG).
  • Corneal Thickness: Thin central corneas (<555 μm).
  • Steroid Intake: Oral, inhalational, eyedrop, or extensive topical steroids.
  • Systemic/Trauma: Diabetes, prior eye trauma (e.g., shuttlecock injury), blood transfusions, smoking, metastatic tumors.
Focused Medical History Taking Protocol in Glaucoma

Patients rarely present stating "I have glaucoma." Doctors must use pattern recognition during consultation:

Key Historical Enquiries:
  • Mobility/Function: Bumping into doorframes, missing side objects, difficulty driving.
  • Visual Symptoms: Intermittent blurring, halos around lights, eye pain, headaches.
  • Medication Review: Steroids, anti-Kochs (Ethambutol → toxic optic neuropathy), antivirals, anticonvulsants.
Observational Cues:
  • Observe mobility while patient walks into the clinic and sits down.
  • Check eyeglass prescription (Myopic glasses = POAG risk; Hyperopic = PACG risk).
  • Family history of blindness or ocular surgeries.
1. Tonometry & Pachymetry Clinical Pearl

Digital Tonometry (Crude Estimation)

Examiner asks patient to look down without closing eyes. Gently alternate pressure with two index fingers on upper sclera.

  • Normal Pressure: Feels firm, like the tip of the nose.
  • High Pressure: Hard, rigid like the forehead.
  • Low Pressure: Soft like the lips.
⚠️
Contraindications: Absolute NO in penetrating eye injuries or active eye infections! NEVER percuss the eye.

Goldmann Applanation Tonometry (Gold Standard)

Fluorescein dye instilled. Biprism touches cornea. Dial rotated until inner margins of semicircular mires touch.

IOP Result = Dial Reading × 10

Pachymetry (Corneal Thickness)

  • Thick Corneas: Require more force to flatten → Falsely HIGH IOP readings.
  • Thin Corneas: Require less force → Falsely LOW IOP readings.
2. Gonioscopy & Angle Landmarks Must Know

Gonioscopy overcomes total internal reflection of the cornea using a mirrored lens to visualize the anterior chamber drainage angle.

Gonioscopic Landmarks (Posterior to Anterior / Top to Bottom)
1. Ciliary Body Band (CBB) - Most Posterior
2. Scleral Spur (SS)
3. Trabecular Meshwork (TM) - Pigmented Band
4. Schwalbe's Line (SL) - Most Anterior
Mnemonic: CSTS ("Can See Till Schwalbe's Line") - Lists structures from Posterior to Anterior.
3. Optic Nerve Features in Glaucoma 90% Violation Rule

Normal vs Glaucomatous Disc

  • Normal vCDR: 0.2 to 0.5. Asymmetry between eyes > 0.2 is highly suspicious!
  • ISNT Rule (Normal Eyes): Neuroretinal rim thickness order: Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal.
  • ISNT Rule Violation: Present in 90% of glaucoma cases (e.g., N > S > T > I due to inferior/superior rim thinning). Note: 10-20% may still follow ISNT rule!

Hallmark Signs of Glaucomatous Damage

  • Rim Notching: Focal thinning of neuroretinal rim (arrowhead sign).
  • Baring of Vessels: Thinning of supporting connective tissue exposing vessel borders.
  • Nasalization: Retinal vessels pushed nasally by expanding central cup.
  • Disc Hemorrhage (Splinter/Flame): Hallmark of worsening/progression; very common in Low Tension Glaucoma!
4. Visual Field Defect Mapping Meridian Rule

Structural optic nerve damage translates directly into functional visual field defects. Glaucomatous defects respect the horizontal meridian (whereas neurological lesions respect the vertical meridian).

Bjerrum's Scotoma

Classic arcuate defect extending from blind spot around fixation (10–20°) to the horizontal raphe.

Seidel Scotoma

Sickle-shaped arcuate extension attached directly to the blind spot.

Nasal Step

Asymmetric loss respecting the horizontal meridian in the nasal visual field.

Primary Open Angle Glaucoma (POAG) Pathophysiology Must Know

In POAG, the anterior chamber angle is open on gonioscopy, but aqueous outflow is obstructed by high resistance at the level of the Trabecular Meshwork. Volume buildup leads to elevated IOP, chronic optic nerve compression, and insidiously progressive visual field loss.

💡
Classic Patient Complaints in POAG: "I bump into doorframes," "I can't drive well," "My progressive lenses aren't working," "I keep knocking over my glass of water," or "I can't see the side of your face."

Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT)
  • Indication: Open Angle Glaucoma ONLY (never in angle closure!).
  • Mechanism: Cool laser selectively absorbed by pigmented melanin TM cells. Biological Effect > Mechanical Effect. Stimulates macrophages to clean TM debris.
  • Efficacy: Drops-free for 6–8 years; 75–80% success rate; repeatable ~3x.
Surgical Options: Trabeculectomy vs GDD
  • Trabeculectomy: Creates subconjunctival drainage canal (bleb). Uses bridle sutures, scleral flap, punch. High risk of scarring.
  • Glaucoma Drainage Device (GDD): Implanted silicon tube + plate valve. Maintains 8–14 mmHg pressure. Indicated in failed trab, NVG, or inflammatory glaucoma.
  • Diode TSCP: Laser destroys ciliary body ("turning off faucet"). Reserved as last resort for blind, painful eyes.
Primary Angle Closure Glaucoma (PACG) Pathophysiology Must Know

PACG is driven by Pupillary Block. Blockade between lens and posterior iris traps aqueous humor behind the pupil, bowing the iris forward (iris bombé) to occlude the trabecular meshwork ("sarado ang drainage canal"). IOP spikes to 40 – 70 mmHg.

Epidemiological Profile:
  • 3x more common in Females.
  • High prevalence in Chinese / East Asians (smaller eyes).
  • Predominantly affects Hyperopes aged 55–65.
Dynamic Imaging Cues:
  • UBM & AS-OCT: Demonstrate angle closure in dark rooms.
  • Lights OFF (Dark): Pupils dilate → Angles CLOSE.
  • Lights ON (Bright): Pupils constrict → Angles OPEN.
Primary Intermittent Angle Closure & Triggers

Characterized by recurrent, self-limiting (<1 hour) episodes of blurry vision, eye ache, and halos around lights. Almost always relieved by sleep (decreased aqueous production during sleep).

📊
Trigger Mechanism Rankings (Lowe & Lim 1989 Study):
1. Watching TV (Highest - 13) • 2. Fatigue (12) • 3. Reading (8) • 4. Sewing (7) • 5. Excitement/Worry (6) • 6. Night out (4) • 7. Heat / Sickness (Lowest - 1).
Primary Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma (PAACG) Ocular Emergency Ocular Emergency

Clinical Presentation & Findings

  • Symptoms: Sudden severe eye pain, headache, severe blurring, rainbow halos, nausea and vomiting.
  • IOP: Massively elevated (>50 to >70 mmHg).
  • Cornea: Hazy/edematous (causes halos).
  • Pupil: Mid-dilated, vertically oval, unreactive.
  • Vessels: Ciliary and conjunctival injection, chemosis.

Emergency Management Pathway

  1. Immediate IOP Reduction: Stat topical drops (Timolol, Brimonidine).
  2. Systemic Hyperosmotics: IV Mannitol or Oral Glycerine. (CAUTION: Mannitol increases intravascular volume → avoid in CHF!).
  3. Oral CAI Note: Acetazolamide NOT given in ER if patient is actively vomiting.
  4. Definitive Surgical Cure: Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) creates through-and-through bypass hole. Followed by Laser Peripheral Iridoplasty (ALPI) if angle remains narrow.
Secondary Glaucoma Spectrum

IOP ranges between 21 – 70 mmHg secondary to identifiable underlying ocular or systemic pathologies:

Steroid-Induced

Prolonged topical, oral, or inhalational steroids increase TM outflow resistance.

Neovascular (NVG)

Retinal ischemia (Diabetic retinopathy/CRVO) → fibrovascular membrane over TM.

Trauma / Phacomorphic

Blunt injury (shuttlecock) or intumescent cataract swollen lens blocking pupil.

Childhood / Congenital Glaucoma Must Know

Rare condition (1:10,000 births; higher in Middle East due to consanguinity) resulting from Trabeculodysgenesis / Angle Dysgenesis ("factory defect" of TM / Barkan's membrane).

Clinical Triad & Physical Signs

  • Classic Triad: Tearing (Epiphora), Photophobia, Blepharospasm (child hides head in pillow).
  • Corneal Enlargement: Corneal diameter ≥ 13 mm is highly suspicious! Causes progressive myopia in older children as eyeball expands like a balloon.
  • Haab's Striae (Hallmark): Horizontal white lines on cornea representing breaks in Descemet's membrane. (Contrast: Birth trauma from forceps causes VERTICAL breaks!).
  • Optic Cupping: Pathognomonic and uniquely REVERSIBLE in infants once IOP is normalized!

Classification & Management

  • Congenital / Infantile: Onset < 3 years of age.
  • Juvenile Open Angle: Onset > 3 years to 30 years of age.
  • Primary Treatment: SURGICAL! Goniotomy or Trabeculotomy (clears corneal opacity and opens dysgenetic angle).
Clinical Case Walkthrough: 51/M District Engineer Board Exam Pattern

Chief Complaint: Frequently bumping into objects on the left side for 7 months. Gradual onset, painless. Wears eyeglasses since high school (-6.00 Myopia). Screen time 12 hrs/day. Maintenance for borderline diabetes.

Clinical Examination Findings

  • Visual Acuity: 20/20 OU with myopia correction.
  • Tonometry (IOP): Right Eye (OD): 15 mmHg (Normal) | Left Eye (OS): 42 mmHg (ABNORMAL!)
  • Fundoscopy (vCDR): OD: 0.5 (Normal margins) | OS: 0.8–0.9 with marked cupping & notching.
  • Gonioscopy: Open angles OU (All bands visible: SL, TM, SS, CBB).
  • Visual Field: OD Normal | OS Severe Tunnel Vision.

Definitive Diagnosis & Reasoning

DIAGNOSIS: Primary Open Angle Glaucoma (POAG) - OS

Reasoning Chain: High IOP (42 mmHg) + High vCDR (0.9) + Tunnel Vision VF defect + Open Angles on Gonioscopy + High Myopia history = Classic POAG pattern.
