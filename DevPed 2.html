🧩 Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Neurodevelopmental

1. Historical Evolution of ASD Diagnosis

Understanding how ASD evolved from schizophrenia to a unified spectrum is frequently tested in pediatric board exams.

Year Key Figure / Milestone Historical Concept & Clinical Significance
1908 Eugen Bleuler Coined "autism" (from Greek autos = self) to describe withdrawn schizophrenic adult patients.
1943 Leo Kanner Published landmark study on 11 highly intelligent children with "early infantile autism," describing extreme aloneness and obsessive desire for sameness.
1944 Hans Asperger Described a "milder" form of autism with high functioning capabilities, later known as Asperger's Syndrome.
1967 Bruno Bettelheim Falsely attributed autism to poor parenting ("refrigerator mothers" lacking warmth). Completely debunked!
1977 First Twin Study Proved strong genetic foundation for autism. Monozygotic twins concordance: 60-96%.
1980 DSM-III "Infantile Autism" listed as a distinct entity separate from childhood schizophrenia under PDD.
1998 Andrew Wakefield Fraudulent paper in The Lancet alleging MMR vaccine causes autism. Fully retracted and discredited.
2013 DSM-5 Eliminated separate subcategories (Asperger's, PDD-NOS, CDD); established single umbrella diagnosis: Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) with severity specifiers.
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Autism is NOT caused by vaccines or "refrigerator mothers"! Thimerosal (a ethylmercury preservative) was removed from routine childhood vaccines in 2000 as a precaution, yet autism rates continued to climb. Furthermore, MMR vaccine is a live attenuated vaccine and never contained thimerosal.

2. Etiology & Major Brain Structures Implicated

ASD is a biologically based neurodevelopmental disorder with high heritability and complex polygenic architecture.

🧠 Neuropathological Circuitry in ASD

Cerebellum
(Motor fine-tuning, balance, speech muscles) ↔ Limbic System (Amygdala/Hippocampus)
(Emotional processing, social cues, memory) ↔ Cerebral Cortex / Frontotemporal
(Social cognition, facial recognition, higher mental function)

Genetic & Biological Factors

  • Twin Concordance: Identical (monozygotic) twins: 60–96%; Non-identical (dizygotic): 0–24%.
  • Sibling Recurrence Risk: Approximately 5% (1 in 20), up to 19% in recent data.
  • Chromosomal Abnormalities: Chromosome 16p11-p12 duplication/deletion found in ~1% of ASD cases. High association with Fragile X Syndrome, Tuberous Sclerosis, Rett Syndrome, Down Syndrome.
  • Neurochemistry: Raised blood serotonin levels (hyperserotonemia) and altered GABAergic/glutamatergic transmission.

Environmental & Intrauterine Factors

  • Parental Age: Advanced maternal and paternal age (de novo spontaneous mutations/imprinting).
  • Perinatal Stressors: Low birth weight, prematurity, C-section delivery (associated with 12-13% of cases).
  • CNS Teratogens: Intrauterine valproate exposure, maternal hyperfolatemia.

3. DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria for ASD

⭐ Diagnostic Criteria Checklist (Must Fulfill Criteria A + B + C + D)

Criterion A: Persistent Deficits in Social Communication & Interaction (Must meet ALL 3/3):

  1. Deficits in social-emotional reciprocity (e.g., abnormal social approach, failure of normal back-and-forth conversation, reduced sharing of interests/emotions).
  2. Deficits in nonverbal communicative behaviors (e.g., poorly integrated verbal/nonverbal communication, abnormal eye contact, lack of facial expressions, absence of gestures).
  3. Deficits in developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships (e.g., difficulty adjusting behavior to social context, difficulty sharing imaginative play, absence of interest in peers).

Criterion B: Restricted, Repetitive Patterns of Behavior, Interests, or Activities (Must meet AT LEAST 2/4):

  1. Stereotyped or repetitive motor movements, use of objects, or speech (e.g., hand flapping, toe walking, lining up toys, echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases).
  2. Insistence on sameness, inflexible adherence to routines, or ritualized patterns (e.g., extreme distress at small changes, rigid greeting rituals, needing same route/food).
  3. Highly restricted, fixated interests abnormal in intensity or focus (e.g., strong attachment to unusual objects, excessively circumscribed interests).

Criterion C & D: Symptoms must be present in the early developmental period and cause clinically significant impairment in functioning.

4. Severity Levels for ASD (DSM-5)

Severity Level Social Communication Domain Restricted, Repetitive Behaviors Domain
Level 3
"Requires Very Substantial Support"		 Severe deficits in verbal/nonverbal social communication skills causing severe impairments in functioning, very limited initiation of social interactions. Inflexibility of behavior, extreme difficulty coping with change, or other restricted/repetitive behaviors markedly interfere with functioning in all spheres.
Level 2
"Requires Substantial Support"		 Marked deficits in verbal/nonverbal social communication; social impairments apparent even with supports in place; limited initiation of social interactions. Inflexibility of behavior, difficulty coping with change, or other repetitive behaviors appear frequently enough to be obvious to casual observer.
Level 1
"Requires Support"		 Without supports in place, deficits in social communication cause noticeable impairments. Difficulty initiating social interactions and clear examples of atypical responses. Inflexibility of behavior causes significant interference with functioning in one or more contexts. Resistance to attempts to interrupt repetitive behaviors.

5. Screening Tools & Absolute Red Flags

🧠 Absolute Indications for Immediate ASD Evaluation

If any of the following are noted during developmental surveillance, immediate referral to a neurodevelopmental specialist is mandatory:

  • No babbling or pointing / gestures by 12 months
  • No single words by 16 months
  • No 2-word spontaneous (non-echolalic) phrases by 24 months
  • ANY loss of ANY language or social skills at ANY age (Regression)

M-CHAT-R/F (16–30 Months)

Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up:

  • 23 Yes/No screening questions for parents.
  • High specificity (0.93-0.99), moderate sensitivity (0.85-0.87).
  • Discriminative Item #7: "Does your child ever use his/her index finger to point to indicate interest in something?" (Joint Attention test).

Pharmacotherapy for ASD Symptoms

No medication cures core ASD social deficits. Drugs target comorbid behavioral disruption:

  • Irritability, Aggression & Severe Temper Tantrums: FDA-approved atypical antipsychotics: Risperidone and Aripiprazole.
  • Hyperactivity/Impulsivity: Methylphenidate or Atomoxetine.
  • Seizures: Antiepileptic drugs (EEG required if sudden behavioral regression or self-injury occurs).

⚡ Attention-Deficit / Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

Neurobehavioral

1. Epidemiology, Heritability & Pathophysiology

ADHD is a lifelong, highly heritable neurobehavioral disorder characterized by developmental deficits in executive function, impulse control, and motor regulation.

⭐ Key Epidemiologic Facts
  • Prevalence: 8–10% in children, 9.6% in adolescents, 4.4% in adults. Mean global prevalence: ~5.2%.
  • Gender Ratios: Males dominate clinical referrals (4:1 to 5:1). In community samples, ratio is ~2:1.
  • Presentation by Sex: Boys present with externalizing symptoms (hyperactivity, aggression, combined type). Girls present with internalizing symptoms (inattention, daydreaming, academic underachievement; often diagnosed later).
  • Persistence: Up to 65% of children with ADHD continue to meet criteria into adulthood.
💡 Neurobiology & Genetics
  • Heritability Index: 0.75 (Extremely high genetic contribution—higher than schizophrenia and depression!).
  • Neurotransmitter Imbalance: Deficit of Dopamine (DA) and Norepinephrine (NE) in the prefrontal cortex and basal ganglia networks.
  • Functional Imaging (PET Scans): Demonstrates decreased glucose cerebral metabolism in frontostriatal attention networks.
  • Environmental Risk Factors: Prenatal exposure to nicotine/cigarettes (OR 2.1), alcohol (OR 2.5), maternal depression (OR 2.8), psychosocial adversity (OR 4.1).

2. DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria & Clinical Presentations

Symptoms must persist for $\ge 6$ months, be developmentally inappropriate, be present in $\ge 2$ settings (e.g., home and school), and cause direct functional impairment. Symptom onset must be before 12 years of age (changed from age 7 in DSM-IV).

Domain Diagnostic Criteria Items (Child: $\ge 6$ required; $\ge 17$ yrs: $\ge 5$ required)
Inattention Domain 1. Fails to give close attention to details / makes careless mistakes.
2. Difficulty sustaining attention in tasks or play activities.
3. Does not seem to listen when spoken to directly.
4. Does not follow through on instructions / fails to finish tasks.
5. Difficulty organizing tasks and activities.
6. Avoids, dislikes, or reluctant to engage in tasks requiring sustained mental effort.
7. Loses things necessary for tasks or activities.
8. Easily distracted by extraneous stimuli.
9. Forgetful in daily activities.
Hyperactivity & Impulsivity Domain 1. Fidgets with or taps hands/feet or squirms in seat.
2. Leaves seat in situations when remaining seated is expected.
3. Runs about or climbs in situations where it is inappropriate.
4. Unable to play or engage in leisure activities quietly.
5. "On the go," acting as if "driven by a motor."
6. Talks excessively.
7. Blurts out an answer before a question has been completed.
8. Has difficulty waiting his or her turn.
9. Interrupts or intrudes on others.

3. Clinical Subtypes (DSM-5 Presentations)

  • Combined Presentation (50–75%): Meets criteria for both inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity over past 6 months. Most common clinical subtype.
  • Predominantly Inattentive Presentation (20–30%): Meets inattention criteria but not hyperactivity-impulsivity. More common in females.
  • Predominantly Hyperactive/Impulsive Presentation (<15%): Meets hyperactivity-impulsivity criteria but not inattention. Most common in preschool children.

4. Multimodal Management & Pharmacotherapy

A multimodal approach combining psychoeducation, behavioral modification, environmental structure, and pharmacotherapy yields the highest long-term success.

Drug Class / Agent Mechanism of Action Clinical Pearls & Monitoring
1st Line: Psychostimulants
• Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta)
• Amphetamine preparations		 Blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake; stimulates presynaptic release into synaptic cleft. • Most effective treatment for core symptoms.
• Side Effects: Insomnia, anorexia/weight loss, growth suppression, headache, rebound irritability.
• Caution: Pre-treatment cardiac screening (ECG) if history of syncope, arrhythmia, or cardiomyopathy.
• Requires Philippine Dangerous Drugs Yellow Prescription pad.
2nd Line: Non-Stimulants
• Atomoxetine (Strattera)		 Selective Noradrenergic Reuptake Inhibitor (NRI). • Non-controlled substance (no abuse potential).
• Ideal for patients with comorbid anxiety, tics, or substance abuse history.
• Full therapeutic effect takes 2–4 weeks.
3rd Line / Off-Label
• Alpha-2 Agonists (Clonidine, Guanfacine)
• Bupropion		 Alpha-2 adrenergic stimulation / DA-NE reuptake inhibition. • Useful for severe impulsivity, aggression, or sleep disturbance.
• Monitor blood pressure and sedation.

📖 Specific Learning Disorders (Learning Disabilities)

Academic & Cognition

1. Concept & Definition

Specific Learning Disorders (LD) represent intrinsic neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by persistent difficulties acquiring specific academic skills (reading, writing, mathematics) in children with normal or superior overall intelligence and adequate educational exposure.

⚠️ Exclusionary Criteria for Learning Disability

Academic failure must NOT be primarily attributable to:

  • Intellectual Disability (IQ < 70)
  • Uncorrected visual or hearing impairments
  • Lack of educational opportunity or ineffective schooling
  • Primary emotional/psychiatric disturbance
  • Linguistic differences or socio-economic disadvantage

2. Specific Subtypes of Learning Disabilities

Type Core Academic Deficit Manifestations & Soft Neurological Signs
Dyslexia
(Reading Disability)		 Phonological processing deficit (~80% of all LDs) Difficulty decoding words, slow labor-intensive reading, letter reversals ($b$ vs $d$, $p$ vs $q$), word inversions ("was" vs "saw"), poor reading comprehension.
Dysgraphia
(Written Expression)		 Fine motor output & written organization deficit Illegible handwriting, erratic spacing, severe spelling errors, inability to translate thoughts into written sentences despite fluent oral speech.
Dyscalculia
(Math Disability)		 Deficit in number sense, facts & calculation Difficulty understanding math concepts, alignment of equations, operational symbols ($+$ vs $-$), telling time, handling money.
Dyspraxia Motor planning & coordination deficit Clumsiness, poor balance, manual dexterity issues (buttoning shirt, tying shoelaces, holding utensils).
Dysphasia Receptive/Expressive language deficit Difficulty comprehending complex spoken instructions or expressing thoughts verbally.

3. Cognitive Assessment: WISC Profile Analysis

Psychological evaluation utilizing the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) is critical for diagnosing LD and differentiating it from Intellectual Disability.

📊 WISC Profile in Specific Learning Disability vs Intellectual Disability

Specific Learning Disorder
  • Full Scale IQ: Normal/Above Normal ($90-109+$)
  • Verbal vs. Performance IQ: Significant discrepancy ($\ge 15$ points difference)
  • Example: Verbal IQ = 128, Performance IQ = 88 $\rightarrow$ Visual-spatial LD ("Street Smart")
Intellectual Disability
  • Full Scale IQ: $\ge 2$ Standard Deviations below population mean (IQ < 70)
  • Subtest Scores: Diffusely subnormal across all cognitive domains
  • Deficits in adaptive functioning.

4. Educational Accommodations & Bypass Interventions

  • Dyslexia Accommodations: Oral testing methods, extra exam time, audiobooks, avoiding forced oral reading in front of peers.
  • Dysgraphia Accommodations: Use of word processors/laptops, dictation/scribes, grading on content rather than handwriting/spelling, minimizing copying from blackboard.
  • Dyscalculia Accommodations: Use of calculators, visual manipulatives, simplified step-by-step math instructions.

🌱 Adolescent Health & Development

Adolescent Medicine

1. Delivery of Health Care & The HEEADSSS Screening Tool

Adolescence (ages 11–21) represents a critical developmental period marked by physical, cognitive, and psychosocial transitions. The HEEADSSS screening framework is the gold standard for history taking.

HEEADSSS Mnemonic Breakdown

  • H - Home: Living arrangements, family relationships, safety at home.
  • E - Education / Employment: School performance, attendance, career goals.
  • E - Eating: Body image, meal patterns, dieting behaviors.
  • A - Activities: Peer relationships, sports, hobbies, screen time.
  • D - Drugs / Alcohol: Tobacco, alcohol, e-cigarettes, illicit drugs.
  • S - Sex / Sexuality: Sexual activity, contraception, romantic partners.
  • S - Self-Harm / Suicide: Mood disturbances, anxiety, suicidal ideation.
  • S - Safety & Social Media: Seatbelts, violence, cyberbullying, privacy.

Preventive Immunization Schedule (11–12 Years)

  • Tdap: Tetanus, Diphtheria, Acellular Pertussis booster.
  • Meningococcal Conjugate (MCV4): Dose 1 at 11–12 yrs; Booster at age 16.
  • HPV Vaccine Series: Prevents cervical/anal dysplasia & genital warts. 2-dose series if started before 15 yrs.
  • Annual Influenza: Yearly dose for all adolescents.

2. Pubertal Variations & Delayed Puberty

Condition Clinical Definition Diagnostic Rationale & Management
Premature Thelarche Isolated breast development in females < 8 years old. Benign normal variant; no pubic hair or accelerated growth; reassurance.
Premature Adrenarche Isolated pubic/axillary hair in females < 8 yrs or males < 9 yrs. Mild premature adrenal androgen secretion; monitor linear growth.
Isosexual Precocious Puberty Full pubertal onset in females < 8 yrs or males < 9 yrs. Requires endocrine evaluation (GnRH stimulation test, bone age X-ray).
Delayed Puberty No pubertal signs in females by age 13 or males by age 14.5. Requires evaluation for constitutional delay vs. hypogonadism.
Constitutional Delay of Puberty (CDP) Most common cause of pubertal delay; delayed bone age with normal growth rate. Delayed bone age by 1.5–4 years compared to chronological age. Family history (+). Normal final adult height. Reassurance.

3. Adolescent Gynecological & Reproductive Issues

  • Secondary Amenorrhea: Absence of menses for 3 consecutive cycle lengths. Rule #1: Always rule out PREGNANCY first!
  • Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB): Most common cause in early post-menarcheal years is anovulatory cycles due to immaturity of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian (HPO) axis.
  • Gynecomastia in Males: Benign proliferation of glandular breast tissue occurring in up to 65% of adolescent males during SMR 3–4 (ages 10–13). Caused by transient estrogen/androgen imbalance. Regresses spontaneously within 18–24 months. Reassurance needed!

4. High-Yield Screening Tools: CRAFFT & FISTS

🧠 CRAFFT Substance Abuse Screening

Score $\ge 2$ indicates high risk for substance abuse:

  • C - Car: Ever ridden in a CAR driven by someone high/drunk?
  • R - Relax: Do you use drugs/alcohol to RELAX or fit in?
  • A - Alone: Do you ever use drugs/alcohol ALONE?
  • F - Forget: Do you ever FORGET things while using?
  • F - Family/Friends: Do family/friends tell you to cut down?
  • T - Trouble: Have you ever gotten into TROUBLE while using?
⭐ FISTS Violence Assessment

Assesses adolescent risk for interpersonal violence:

  • F - Fighting: How many physical fights in past year?
  • I - Injuries: Have you been injured or injured others?
  • S - Sex: In forced sex or intimate partner violence?
  • T - Threats: Has anyone threatened you with a weapon?
  • S - Self-defense: Do you carry a weapon for self-defense?

🩺 High-Yield Lecture Clinical Cases

Case Vignettes
Case Vignette 1: Severe Intellectual Disability & Hydrocephalus (GE, 6y11m Male) ▼

Presentation: Chief complaint of speech delay. History of congenital hydrocephalus (aqueductal stenosis) and seizures. Delayed in all milestones: sat alone at 3 yrs, walked at 3y6m, single words at 3 yrs, 2-word phrases at 6 yrs. Physical exam shows microcephaly.

💡 Diagnostic Rationale

Persistent global developmental delays beyond 5 years of age are classified as Intellectual Disability (ID). Under DSM-5, the severity of ID (Mild, Moderate, Severe, Profound) is classified strictly based on Adaptive Functioning (conceptual, social, practical domains) and NOT by IQ scores alone.

Case Vignette 2: Global Developmental Delay & Learning Difficulties (CG, 8.1y Female) ▼

Presentation: Born premature at 34 weeks (BW 1.8kg, meconium-stained, preeclampsia). Walked at 2 yrs, simple sentences at 5 yrs. Enrolled in SPED at 7 yrs due to severe academic difficulty learning letters, numbers, colors, shapes. Toilet trained by day at 5 yrs with night accidents.

💡 Diagnostic Rationale

Global Developmental Delay secondary to prematurity and perinatal hypoxic insult. Pervasive delays across motor, speech, cognitive, and adaptive spheres differentiate global intellectual delay from an isolated Specific Learning Disability.

Case Vignette 3: Classic Autism Spectrum Disorder (3yo Male) ▼

Presentation: Speech delay, tip-toe walking, spinning around in circles, no eye contact, "dedma" when name is called, absence of joint attention, pulls parent's hand to reach objects (hand-leading), distress with loud noises, no symbolic play.

💡 Diagnostic Rationale

Fulfills all DSM-5 Criteria for ASD: Deficits in social reciprocity (no name response/eye contact), nonverbal communication (hand-leading instead of pointing), restricted repetitive behaviors (spinning, tip-toe walking), sensory hypersensitivity (distress to loud noise).

Case Vignette 4: Combined Type ADHD (6yo Male) ▼

Presentation: Hyperactivity since age 3 (running, climbing furniture). In school, cannot remain seated, blurts answers, interrupts classmates, loses school materials, normal overall cognitive ability.

💡 Diagnostic Rationale

Classic ADHD, Combined Presentation. Symptoms present before age 12, manifest across $\ge 2$ settings (home and school), and involve both inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive domains.
