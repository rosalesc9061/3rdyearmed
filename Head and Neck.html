Head and Neck Surgery Core Overview SURG 2 • LE 1

The head and neck region accounts for only 20% of total body surface area, yet pathologies in this area exert a disproportionate functional, psychosocial, and economic impact. Disorders affect critical upper aerodigestive tract functions including vision, olfaction, audition, phonation, deglutition, and respiration.

Functional Impact

Disruption of breathing, swallowing, and speech can lead to immediate life-threatening airway compromise or severe malnutrition.

Cosmetic & Psychosocial

Facial disfigurement from aggressive tumors or resections carries substantial psychological burden and social stigma.

Infectious Sequelae

Head & neck soft tissue infections can rapidly spread into deep neck spaces, causing mediastinitis, necrotizing fasciitis, or airway closure.

⭐
Golden Rule of Head and Neck Oncology Management:
If a head and neck tumor is resectable, surgery with adequate gross margins is the primary treatment of choice. Radiation or chemotherapy serves as primary treatment when tumors are unresectable, in preservation of speech/voice (e.g., early glottic cancer, nasopharynx), or as post-operative adjuvant therapy for high-risk features.

Non-Tumor Disorders (Ear, Sinuses, Larynx, Vascular) Inflammatory & Infectious

1. Ear Infections

Otitis Externa ("Swimmer's Ear")

Definition: Infection of the external auditory canal skin initiated by persistent moisture retention.

Etiology: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (most common bacterial agent).

Phases & Presentation:

  • Initial Phase: Severe otalgia, pruritus, tragic tenderness, soft tissue swelling.
  • Chronic Phase: Subsided pain, profound itching, skin thickening.

Management: Otomicroscopic debris clearing, topical quinolone ear drops (e.g., Ciprofloxacin), topical steroids for severe edema, 2% acetic acid. Systemic antibiotics reserved for severe/immunocompromised cases.

Otitis Media

Definition: Bacterial infection of the middle ear space; accounts for 25% of pediatric antibiotic prescriptions.

Pathophysiology: Peak incidence <2 years of age due to anatomical immaturity and horizontal orientation of the Eustachian tube.

Key Pathogens: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis.

Complications:

  • Intratemporal: Acute coalescent mastoiditis, Petrositis (Gradenigo syndrome: retro-orbital pain + CN VI palsy), Labyrinthitis (vertigo + SNHL), Facial nerve palsy.
  • Intracranial: Meningitis (#1 intracranial complication, associated with H. influenzae type B), brain abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis.
⚠️
Malignant Otitis Externa (Necrotizing Otitis Externa):
Fulminant, necrotizing infection of otologic soft tissues with osteomyelitis of the skull base, seen characteristically in elderly diabetic or immunocompromised patients presenting with persistent otalgia (>1 month) and granulation tissue in the ear canal. Requires aggressive IV anti-pseudomonal antibiotics and tight glycemic control.
💡 Clinical Reasoning Cue: If an infant/child presents with acute otitis media that fails to resolve and develops severe post-auricular tenderness, erythema, and outward displacement of the pinna → Think Acute Coalescent Mastoiditis. Goal: Sterilize middle ear with IV antibiotics + Myringotomy tube placement.

2. Sinus Inflammatory Disorders

Classification Duration Pathogens Diagnostic Clues & Management
Acute Sinusitis 7-10 days to <4 weeks S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis (Nosocomial: P. aeruginosa, S. aureus) Follows viral URTI; mucosal swelling closes sinus ostium. Tx: Targeted oral antibiotics, decongestants, saline spray, steroids.
Subacute Sinusitis 4 to 12 weeks Similar to acute pathogens Continuation of symptoms despite initial therapy. Similar medical management.
Chronic Sinusitis >12 weeks Polymicrobial, antibiotic-resistant flora, fungi Requires objective mucosal inflammation. Diagnostic Gold Standard: Nasal Endoscopy & CT Scan (shows sinus opacification/mucosal thickening). Tx: 3-6 weeks antibiotics, saline irrigation, endoscopic sinus surgery.

3. Benign Laryngeal Disorders

Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis (RRP)

Etiology: HPV Subtypes 6 and 11 in mucosal epithelium.

Transmission: Acquisition during vaginal delivery (juvenile onset) or adult-onset in 3rd-4th decade.

Presentation: Progressive hoarseness, stridor, airway obstruction.

Tx: Operative microlaryngoscopy with excision/laser ablation (frequent recurrences requires multiple procedures); Medical adjuvant: intralesional cidofovir, oral indole-3-carbinol.

Reinke's Edema (Polypoid Corditis)

Pathophysiology: Accumulation of fluid in the superficial lamina propria (Reinke's space) of vocal cords due to capillary shearing injury.

Associations: Heavy smoking, female predominance, laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR), vocal abuse.

Presentation: Bilateral, progressive development of a rough, low-pitched, deep voice.

Tx: Smoking cessation, reflux management, voice therapy, superficial excision under microlaryngoscopy.

Idiopathic Vocal Cord Paralysis

Key Fact: Left vocal cord is more commonly involved due to the longer intrathoracic course of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (looping around aortic arch).

Exam Trap: Vocal cord paralysis can be the first presenting sign of occult lung, thyroid, or esophageal carcinoma!

Diagnostics: Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy (confirmatory); Laryngeal EMG to distinguish nerve paralysis from cricoarytenoid joint fixation or scar tissue.

Laryngeal Granuloma

Location: Posterior larynx on the arytenoid mucosa.

Etiology: Gastroesophageal/laryngopharyngeal reflux, endotracheal intubation, chronic throat clearing, vocal misuse.

Presentation: Patients report pain on swallowing (odynophagia) more prominently than voice changes.

Tx: Anti-reflux therapy, voice rest. Surgery reserved for airway compromise or suspected carcinoma.

Benign Tumors of the Oral Cavity and Jaws Oral & Maxillofacial

1. Soft Tissue & Mucosal Lesions

Mucocele vs. Ranula

Mucocele (Mucous Retention/Extravasation Cyst):

  • Arises in minor salivary glands (lower lip #1, cheek, ventral tongue).
  • Caused by mechanical trauma (e.g., lip biting).
  • Fluid-filled bluish nodule in patients <20 years.
  • Tx: Excision of the cyst wall and capsule.

Ranula:

  • Retention/extravasation cyst arising from sublingual major salivary gland in the floor of mouth.
  • Tx: Marsupialization or excision of sublingual gland.

Epulis, Granuloma, Papilloma

Epulis: Granulomatous lesion on the gingiva secondary to chronic irritation; can bleed easily and mimic malignancy. Tx: Excision biopsy.

Granuloma: Mass of granulation tissue responding to repeated trauma or foreign body. Biopsy necessary to rule out cancer.

Papilloma: Soft, irregular, pedunculated wart-like mucosal growth derived from epidermis. Tx: Surgical excision or cauterization.

2. Premalignant Lesions of the Oral Mucosa

Entity Clinical Appearance Malignant Potential Key Associations & Management
Leukoplakia White patch/plaque that CANNOT be scraped off 5 - 25% transformation risk Associated with smoking & alcohol. (Contrast with Oral Candidiasis which can be scraped off). Tx: Excision biopsy.
Erythroplakia Bright red, velvety mucosal patch HIGHEST malignant potential (>50% dysplasia/CIS) High-risk sites: ventral tongue, floor of mouth. Demands immediate excision biopsy!
Oral Submucosal Fibrosis (OSMF) Fibrotic, blanching, rigid mucosa; severe trismus (reduced mouth opening) High risk for SCCA transformation Strongly linked to Betel Nut chewing (common in Ilocos region). Tx: Stop betel nut, intra-lesional steroids, jaw physiotherapy.
Oral Lichen Planus (OLP) White lace-like patches (Wickham striae) on erythematous tissue Low-to-moderate potential Chronic autoimmune disease. Tx: Topical corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (cyclosporine).

3. Odontogenic vs. Non-Odontogenic Jaw Tumors

Ameloblastoma (Most Common Odontogenic Tumor)

Definition: Benign but locally aggressive epithelial odontogenic neoplasm arising from dental lamina associated with an impacted 3rd molar.

Demographics: Young adults; 80% located in the mandible (molar-ramus area).

Radiographic Hallmark: "Soap bubble" or "Honeycomb" multilocular radiolucency with bony expansion.

Management: Surgical resection with wide margins (Segmental Mandibulectomy) due to high recurrence rates with simple curettage.

Non-Odontogenic Bony Exostoses & Lesions

Torus Palatinus: Harmless exostosis along the midline of hard palate. Asymptomatic unless interfered with by dentures. Tx: None unless symptomatic.

Torus Mandibularis: Bony overgrowth on lingual surface of mandible opposite premolars.

Osteoma: Slow-growing benign bone lesion attached to cortex by a dense pedicle.

Fibrous Dysplasia: Normal cortical bone replaced by immature fibrous matrix.

Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSCC) Oncology & Staging

Over 90-95% of head and neck cancers are Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCCA), originating from the non-keratinizing stratified squamous mucosal lining of the upper aerodigestive tract.

Etiology & Carcinogenesis

  • Tobacco & Alcohol Abuse: Synergistic major risk factors.
  • Betel Nut Chewing: Major cause in Southeast Asia / India.
  • HPV (Subtypes 16 & 18): Increasingly linked to oropharyngeal cancers.
  • EBV (Epstein-Barr Virus): High association with Nasopharyngeal CA.
  • p53 Gene Mutation: Leads to loss of cell cycle regulation.

Natural History Cascade

↓
3
Carcinoma In Situ (CIS): Full thickness atypia, intact basement membrane

⭐
Second Primary Tumors in HNSCC:
Approximately 14% of HNSCC patients develop a second primary tumor due to field cancerization.
• Synchronous Tumor: Diagnosed within 6 months of primary CA (3-4%).
• Metachronous Tumor: Diagnosed after 6 months from primary CA (80% of second primaries; 50% occur within 2 years).

Anatomical Site Sub-Types & Surgical Strategy

1. Lip Malignancy

Demographics: Caucasian males, 50-70 years, chronic UV/sunlight exposure.

Distribution: Lower lip (88-90% - predominantly SCCA); Upper lip (2-7% - Basal Cell CA more frequent); Oral commissure (1%).

Surgical Management: Wide local excision with 1 cm gross clear margins.

  • Defects ≤ 1/3 of lip length: Primary closure via wedge resection.
  • Defects up to 75% of lip: Karapandzic flap.
  • Lip switch technique: Abbe-Estlander flap.
  • Potential Complication: Microstomia (abnormally small oral aperture).

2. Oral Cavity & Mandible Cancer

Distribution: Most common sites = Anterior 2/3 tongue and Floor of the mouth. Least common site = Hard palate (Adenoid Cystic CA is most common here).

Tongue CA: Arises typically on lateral midportion. High metastatic rate—even T1 lesions have a ~30% risk of occult cervical lymph node metastasis! Tx: Transoral wide excision (>1.5 cm margin) + Neck Dissection.

Floor of Mouth CA: 3rd most common site (13-17%). 50% present at Stage III/IV. Tumors easily invade mandible through dental sockets (in dentate) or alveolar pores (in edentulous).

Mandibular Resection Types:

  • Marginal Mandibulectomy: Removal of periosteum/cortex while keeping the bony arch intact; done when tumor abuts periosteum without bony invasion.
  • Segmental Mandibulectomy: Full-thickness bony resection; mandatory when cortical bone is invaded by cancer. Requires microvascular free flap reconstruction (e.g., fibula free flap).
  • COMMANDO Procedure: COmbined Mandibulectomy And Neck Dissection Operation.

3. Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC)

Anatomy: Rosenmuller fossa, Eustachian tube orifices, adenoid pad. Endemic in Southern China and Eskimos.

Symptoms: Nasal stuffiness, epistaxis, unilateral serous otitis media / hearing loss, neck mass. Cranial nerve palsies (CN II, IV, V, VI) indicate cavernous sinus/skull base invasion.

CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: Surgical resection is NOT the primary treatment for Nasopharyngeal CA! Primary Radiotherapy ± Chemotherapy is the gold standard. Surgery is reserved strictly for RT failure.

4. Hypopharyngeal & Laryngeal CA

Hypopharyngeal CA Triad: Throat Pain + Referred Otalgia + Dysphagia (>50%). Poor prognosis (30% 5-year survival). Tx: Laryngopharyngectomy + Radical Neck Dissection + RT. Reconstruction via Pharyngolaryngoesophagectomy (PLO) with Gastric Pull-up.

Laryngeal CA: Early symptom is Hoarseness. Glottic cancers diagnosed early have high cure rates. Voice-sparing RT used for early stages; Total Laryngectomy + Bilateral Neck Dissection for advanced (T3/T4) disease.

Neck Masses & Neck Dissections Diagnostic Rules & Surgical Staging

1. Clinical Rules of Thumb for Neck Masses

The Rule of 80s (Shaha / MSKCC)

Applies to non-thyroid lateral neck masses in adults:

  • 80% are Neoplastic (20% inflammatory/congenital)
  • 80% of neoplasms are Malignant (20% benign)
  • 80% of malignant neck masses are Metastatic (20% primary)
  • 80% metastasize from primaries ABOVE the clavicle
  • 80% of metastases are Squamous Cell Carcinoma
  • 80% are detected during the initial clinical exam

The Rule of 7s (Duration of Mass)

  • 7 Days (Acute): Inflammatory / Infectious origin (bacterial, viral lymphadenopathy, abscess).
  • 7 Months (Subacute): Neoplastic / Malignant origin (Lymphoma, Carotid Body Tumor, Metastatic Squamous or Thyroid CA).
  • 7 Years or more (Chronic): Congenital origin (Thyroglossal duct cyst, Branchial cleft cyst, Dermoid cyst).
💡 Cue: A neck mass present for 5 days in a 10-year-old → Think Infection. A mass present for 6 months in a 60-year-old smoker → Think Malignancy!

Selective Neck Dissections (Preserves select node levels)

  • Supraomohyoid Neck Dissection (Levels I, II, III): Standard staging neck dissection for clinical N0 oral cavity SCCA.
  • Extended Supraomohyoid Neck Dissection (Levels I, II, III, IV): Used for skin cancers anterior to ear tragus or combined with parotidectomy.

  • Posterolateral Neck Dissection (Levels II, III, IV, V): Used for skin cancers/melanoma posterior to tragus on scalp.
  • Central Compartment Neck Dissection (Level VI): Used for differentiated and medullary thyroid cancers.

2. Parotid Anatomy & The Facial Nerve (CN VII)

Anatomical Relationships

• The parotid is a unilobular gland sitting in the retromandibular fossa, traversed by the Facial Nerve (CN VII).

• CN VII divides the parotid into two clinical zones:

  • Superficial Lobe: Contains 70% of gland tissue (located superficial to CN VII). Most tumors arise here.
  • Deep Lobe: Contains 30% of gland tissue (located deep to CN VII).

• Drained by Stensen's duct opening opposite the upper 2nd molar.

5 Branches of Facial Nerve (Top to Bottom)

1
Temporal (Frontal) Branch: Forehead wrinkling
2
Zygomatic Branch: Eye closure (orbicularis oculi)
3
Buccal Branch: Cheek movement / smiling
4
Marginal Mandibular Branch: Lower lip depression
5
Cervical Branch: Platysma innervation

3. Surgical Procedures for Parotid Tumors

Superficial Parotidectomy

Indication: Benign tumors (e.g., Pleomorphic Adenoma) ≤ 4 cm restricted to the superficial lobe.

Technique: Pre-auricular modified Blair incision, identification of CN VII main trunk at stylomastoid foramen, dissection along the nerve plane, and removal of superficial lobe while preserving all 5 branches of CN VII.

Total Parotidectomy

Indication: Malignant parotid tumors, deep lobe tumors, or benign tumors > 4 cm (to prevent high recurrence rates).

Technique: Complete removal of both superficial and deep lobes. In malignancy without direct nerve invasion, CN VII is dissected and preserved. If directly involved by cancer, sacrifice with nerve grafting is performed.

10-Minute Pre-Exam High-Yield Cram Sheet Ultra-Condensed

Infections & Inflammatory Rapid Review

  • Otitis Externa: P. aeruginosa #1; Ciprofloxacin ear drops + debris cleaning.
  • Otitis Media: Peak <2 yrs due to horizontal Eustachian tube. S. pneumoniae #1.
  • Malignant Otitis Externa: Elderly diabetics; necrotizing osteomyelitis; IV anti-pseudomonals.
  • Chronic Sinusitis: >12 weeks; Nasal endoscopy + CT scan confirmatory.
  • Reinke's Edema: Fluid in superficial lamina propria; female heavy smokers; rough low voice.
  • Vocal Cord Paralysis: Left VC more common (longer RLN course); fiberoptic laryngoscopy + EMG.
  • RRP: HPV 6 & 11; juvenile vaginal delivery acquisition; multiple laser procedures required.

Tumors & Premalignant Rapid Review

  • Hemangioma vs Malformation: Hemangiomas are TRUE neoplasms, regress spontaneously. Malformations are NOT neoplasms, DO NOT regress.
  • Cystic Hygroma: Lymphatic malformation; midline neck/forehead; airway emergency risk → Early surgery!
  • Mucocele vs Ranula: Mucocele = minor salivary glands (lower lip #1). Ranula = sublingual gland floor of mouth.
  • Leukoplakia: White patch CANNOT be scraped off (vs Candidiasis which CAN be scraped off).
  • Erythroplakia: Red velvety patch; HIGHER malignant potential (>50%).
  • Ameloblastoma: Most common odontogenic tumor; molar ramus mandible; "Soap bubble / Honeycomb" X-ray; Mandibulectomy.
  • Betel Nut: Causes Oral Submucosal Fibrosis (OSMF) with severe trismus.

Oncology & Neck Dissection Rapid Review

  • HNSCC: 90-95% SCCA; Tobacco + Alcohol synergistic; p53 mutation.
  • Nasopharyngeal CA: Rosenmuller fossa; EBV; Hearing loss + Epistaxis; PRIMARY TX IS RADIOTHERAPY (NOT surgery!).
  • Hypopharyngeal CA Triad: Throat pain + Referred Otalgia + Dysphagia (>50%). PLO + Gastric pull-up.
  • Rule of 80s: Lateral neck mass in adult → 80% Neoplastic, 80% Malignant, 80% Metastatic, 80% Above clavicle, 80% SCCA.
  • Rule of 7s: 7 days = Infection; 7 months = Malignancy; 7 years = Congenital.
  • RND vs MRND: RND removes CN XI, SCM, and IJV. MRND Type 1 preserves CN XI. MRND Type 2 preserves CN XI & SCM. MRND Type 3 preserves CN XI, SCM, and IJV.

Salivary Glands Rapid Review

  • Size-Malignancy Rule: Parotid (80% benign), Submandibular (60% benign), Sublingual/Minor (60% MALIGNANT).
  • Pleomorphic Adenoma: #1 benign tumor of parotid & submandibular glands.
  • Mucoepidermoid CA: #1 malignant tumor of parotid gland.
  • Adenoid Cystic CA: #1 malignant tumor of submandibular, sublingual, and minor salivary glands (hard palate).
  • Parotidectomy: Superficial parotidectomy for benign tumors ≤4 cm. Total parotidectomy for malignant or >4 cm benign tumors. Always preserve 5 branches of CN VII!
