Benign & Malignant Tumors of the Skin; Cutaneous Drug Eruption Infection

Based on the synchronous lecture by Dr. Carmela Co Buenviaje, MD, DPDS, FPSV | UST Faculty of Medicine and Surgery

🎯 Core Learning Objectives

  • Recognize cutaneous drug reactions and identify culprit agents using a detailed drug timeline (up to 2-3 months prior).
  • Differentiate benign skin tumors (Seborrheic Keratosis, Milia, Syringoma, Sebaceous Hyperplasia, Nevi) from malignant neoplasms.
  • Master high-yield clinical features and diagnostic criteria for BCC, SCC, Keratoacanthoma, and Cutaneous Melanoma.
  • Apply prognostic scoring systems (SCORTEN for Epidermal Necrolysis; AJCC TNM & NCCN risk stratifications for skin cancers).

⭐ Key High-Yield Takeaways

  • Exanthematous Drug Eruption: Most common CADR (~95%), trunk to extremities, T-cell mediated.
  • SCORTEN: 7 prognostic criteria calculated in first 24h for EN. Score ≥2 requires ICU/Burn Unit.
  • BCC vs Sebaceous Hyperplasia: BCC is firm; Sebaceous Hyperplasia is soft with central umbilication + expressible sebum globule.
  • NCCN Area H ("Mask Area"): Head, eyelids, ears, genitalia, hands, feet = High risk for SCC/BCC → Requires Mohs Micrographic Surgery.

⚠️ Critical Board Pitfalls

  • Never Rechallenge or Desensitize: Re-exposure in severe CADR (SJS/TEN/DRESS) can be rapidly fatal.
  • Incomplete Nevus Excision: Causes "Recurrent Melanocytic Nevus" which mimics melanoma histologically and clinically.
  • Hutchinson Sign: Pigment extending onto the proximal nail fold indicates Subungual Melanoma (NOT benign nevus).
  • Ugly Duckling Sign: A pigmented lesion distinctly different from a patient's other nevi must be biopsied.
💡 Clinical Pearl: General Approach to CADR
Even with written average onsets for drug reactions, obtain medication history as far back as 2 to 3 months. Identify new drugs started within preceding 3 months, especially within 6 weeks, or those taken intermittently.

Cutaneous Adverse Drug Reactions (CADR)

Immunologic mechanisms, diagnostic approach, clinical spectrum, and management of severe drug eruptions

⭐ Must Know: Types of Immunologic Drug Reactions (Fitzpatrick Table 45-8)
  • Type I (IgE-Mediated): Immediate response. Penicillin, antibiotics. Urticaria, angioedema, mucosa edema, anaphylactic shock.
  • Type II (Cytotoxic): IgG/IgM + drug cause cell lysis. Penicillin, sulfonamides, quinidine, isoniazid. Petechiae (thrombocytopenic purpura), drug-induced pemphigus.
  • Type III (Immune Complex): IgG/IgM immune complexes deposit in vessels. Immunoglobulins, antibiotics, rituximab, infliximab. Vasculitis, urticaria, serum sickness.
  • Type IV (Cell-Mediated): Sensitized T-cells release cytokines. Sulfamethoxazole, anticonvulsants, allopurinol. Morbilliform exanthem, FDE, lichenoid eruptions, SJS, TEN.
1. Exanthematous Drug Eruption (Morbilliform / Maculopapular) ▼

Incidence: Most common form of drug eruptions, accounting for approximately 95% of cases.

Onset: Usually within 1 week of initiation or for therapy taken up to 2 months ago. May appear 1-2 days after drug discontinuation.

Clinical Features: Symmetric erythematous coalescing macules and papules starting on trunk and spreading peripherally. Pruritus is almost always present. Absence of blistering or pustulation. Resolves in 7-14 days with color change from bright red to browning-red followed by desquamation.

Etiology: Beta-lactams, sulfonamide antimicrobials, NNRTIs (e.g. nevirapine), antiepileptics. Risk increases dramatically in HIV patients given sulfonamides.

2. Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) ▼

AKA: Hypersensitivity Syndrome Reaction (HSR), Drug-Induced Hypersensitivity Reaction.

Incidence & Onset: 1 in 3000 exposures. Starts 1 to 6 weeks after drug exposure (most frequently on first exposure).

Hallmark Triad: Fever/Malaise + Extensive Exanthematous Rash (can look purpuric) + Internal Organ Involvement (Liver, Kidney, CNS) + Hematologic abnormalities (atypical lymphocytosis followed by eosinophilia).

⚠️ Late Complication Warning
Immune-mediated thyroid dysfunction (hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism) is a key long-term complication of DRESS that may not present until 3 to 12 months after initial symptoms!

Culprit Drugs: Aromatic anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine), lamotrigine, sulfonamides, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, nevirapine, minocycline, metronidazole, allopurinol.

3. Urticarial Drug Reactions & Angioedema ▼

Urticaria: Pruritic red wheals lasting <24 hours for individual lesions (new lesions continuously develop). Can be IgE-mediated (Type I) or Non-IgE / direct histamine release (Type IV / nonspecific release).

Angioedema: Swelling of deep dermal and subcutaneous tissues. Frequently unilateral and nonpruritic. Lasts 1-2 hours up to 2-5 days. Highly associated with NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors.

4. Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis (AGEP) ▼

Onset & Incidence: Rapid onset 24 to 48 hours after drug initiation. Rare (1-5 cases/million/yr).

Clinical Presentation: High fever + hundreds of small, monomorphous, nonfollicular, sterile pustules on an erythematous base, concentrated on trunk and intertriginous regions. Desquamation occurs after ~2 weeks.

Systemic Features: Leukocytosis with neutrophilia, elevated liver enzymes with steatosis/hepatomegaly, renal insufficiency, pleural effusions with hypoxemia.

Culprit Drugs: Beta-lactams, macrolides, quinolones, CCBs (diltiazem), terbinafine, sulfonamides, anticonvulsants.

5. Fixed Drug Eruption (FDE) ▼

Onset: 30 minutes to 8–16 hours post-ingestion.

Hallmark Feature: Solitary or multiple round/ovoid, dusky red macules/plaques that recur at the EXACT SAME ANATOMIC SITE upon re-exposure. May evolve into bullae. Predilection: genitalia, perianal, hands, feet. Symptoms: burning/stinging.

Resolution & Rechallenge: Acute phase lasts days to weeks, resolving into residual grayish or slate-colored hyperpigmentation. On rechallenge, lesions flare up at the original site AND new lesions often appear.

Culprit Agents: NSAIDs, acetaminophen, antibiotics (TMP-SMX), pseudoephedrine, food dyes/additives.

6. Epidermal Necrolysis (SJS and TEN) & SCORTEN Scale ▼

Spectrum Definition: Acute life-threatening mucocutaneous reactions with cell-mediated keratinocyte apoptosis leading to extensive epidermal detachment. Risk is highest within first 8 weeks (onset usually between 4th and 28th day of continuous drug exposure).

Clinical Signs: Prodrome (1-3 days): fever, sore throat, cough. Followed by dusky purpuric macules, flaccid blisters, (+) Nikolsky sign (lateral pressure dislodges epidermis), (+) Asboe-Hansen sign. Mucous membrane involvement in ~90% (2+ sites: oral pseudomembranes/crusts, genital synechiae, eye symblepharon/ulcers).

Drug Risk Stratification for SJS/TEN

  • HIGH RISK: Allopurinol, Lamotrigine, Cotrimoxazole (and antibacterial sulfonamides, sulfasalazine), Carbamazepine, Nevirapine, Oxicam NSAIDs (meloxicam), Phenobarbital, Phenytoin.
  • MODERATE RISK: Cephalosporins, Macrolides, Quinolones, Tetracyclines, Acetic acid NSAIDs (diclofenac).
  • NO INCREASED RISK: Propionic NSAIDs (ibuprofen), Beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, CCBs, Thiazides, Sulfonylureas, Insulin, Valproic acid.

🧠 Exam Tip: SJS/TEN Management Protocol
  • Immediate Drug Withdrawal: Stop all suspect drugs started in past 8 weeks.
  • Supportive Care: Fluid replacement via peripheral venous lines (avoid central lines due to infection risk from detached skin). Raise room temp to 28°C–30°C. Early NGT nutrition to prevent gut bacterial translocation.
  • Specific Pharmacotherapy: Cyclosporine A (3 mg/kg/day for 10 days) improves survival. Etanercept (anti-TNF) may benefit. DO NOT give IVIG (recent evidence shows no benefit). DO NOT give prophylactic antibiotics. NO aggressive debridement.

Benign Tumors of the Skin

Clinical identification, histologic characteristics, and differential diagnosis of common benign cutaneous lesions

1. Seborrheic Keratosis (SK)

Epidemiology: Most common acquired benign epithelial tumor of the skin. Common in middle-aged and elderly. Spares palms and soles.

Pathogenesis: Monoclonal neoplasm tracked by polymorphisms in human androgen receptors.

Clinical Features: Circumscribed tan-brown patches to papules/plaques with a verrucous, greasy scale and a stuck-on appearance.

⭐ Leser-Trélat Sign
Sudden eruption of multiple seborrheic keratoses in large numbers is a cutaneous marker of internal malignancy — most commonly Gastrointestinal Adenocarcinoma, followed by lymphoproliferative disorders.

Clinical Variants of SK:

  • Common SK: Verrucous stuck-on papules/plaques.
  • Reticulated SK: Thin, flat, pigmented patches arising from solar lentigines.
  • Dermatosis Papulosa Nigra (DPN): Multiple small dark brown/black papules on malar face in dark skin (Fitzpatrick IV+, common in Filipinos).
  • Polypoid SK: Pedunculated, hyperpigmented lesions on neck/intertriginous areas (mimics skin tags).

Treatment: Cryotherapy, electrodessication & curettage (EDC), laser ablation.

2. Milium / Milia

Definition: 1 to 2 mm, white, subepidermal domed papules commonly located on cheeks and eyelids.

Histopathology: Minute epidermoid cysts lined by stratified squamous epithelium and filled with keratin. Bacteria are absent.

Etiology: Plugs in pilosebaceous units or eccrine sweat ducts.

  • Primary Milia: Spontaneous in infants and adults.
  • Secondary Milia: Follows trauma or basement membrane injury (burns, dermabrasion, epidermolysis bullosa, porphyria cutanea tarda, friction).
  • Milia en plaque: Inflammatory plaque variant on the ear.

Treatment: Simple extraction via a small nick/incision with a needle or lancet. (Electrocautery is not first-line due to recurrence risk).

3. Syringoma

Definition & Demographics: Benign appendage tumor derived from intradermal eccrine sweat ducts. Predominantly affects adolescent and young adult females.

Clinical Features: Multiple 1 to 3 mm, flesh-colored to yellow papules symmetrically distributed in the periorbital region (lower eyelids).

Histopathology: Cords and nests of epithelial cells in a sclerotic stroma with ductal structures exhibiting a classic "tadpole-shaped" or "comma-shaped" tail. Lumens contain PAS-positive material.

Differential: Milia, Xanthelasma, Angiofibroma.

Management: Optional / Cosmetic (Laser, EDC, excision).

4. Sebaceous Hyperplasia

Definition: Benign hypertrophy of sebaceous glands in older adults, frequently confused with Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC).

Clinical Features: 1 to 3 mm yellow papules with central umbilication and surrounding telangiectasias on the face (forehead, nose, cheeks).

💡 Distinguishing Sebaceous Hyperplasia vs. Nodular BCC
  1. Consistency: Sebaceous hyperplasia is SOFT on palpation; Nodular BCC is FIRM.
  2. Lateral Compression Test: Firm lateral compression on sebaceous hyperplasia elicits a tiny globule of sebum in the central valley of umbilication.

Treatment: Light electrocautery.

5. Common Acquired Nevomelanocytic Nevus (NMN)

Acquired benign proliferation of nevus cells. Peak number occurs in 3rd decade of life and declines thereafter. Classically <6 mm, round/oval, sharply demarcated, homogenous color.

Histologic Stages of Nevus Evolution

Nevus Stage Clinical Appearance Histologic Location of Nevus Cell Nests Melanization Capacity
Junctional Nevus Flat, dark macule (<1 cm), uniform tan/brown/black. Children/teens. Epidermal side of dermal-epidermal junction. Highest (nests in epidermis produce abundant melanin).
Compound Nevus Raised, dark, dome-shaped or cobblestone papule. Children/teens. Nests both intraepidermally AND in papillary dermis. Intermediate (mottled color as deeper dermis is invaded).
Dermal Nevus Raised, light/skin-colored/flesh papule or nodule. 3rd-4th decade. Purely intradermal (complete "dropping off" into dermis). Lowest/None (nevus cells lose melanization capacity deep in dermis).

Halo Nevus (Sutton Nevus)

Preexisting nevus surrounded by a depigmented ring/halo. Signifies self-immunologic regression of nevus cells. Central nevus eventually disappears, followed by repigmentation. (In older adults, an atypical halo lesion should raise suspicion for dysplastic nevus or melanoma).

Nail Matrix Nevi & Hutchinson Sign

Nevus of the nail apparatus appears as a linear longitudinal brown streak (melanonychia) extending from matrix to distal nail edge. Extension of pigment onto the proximal nail fold is Hutchinson Sign — a pathognomonic clue for Subungual Melanoma!

⚠️ Avoid Incomplete Removal & Destructive Methods
Partial excision or application of caustic substances (e.g. kasoy oil, destructive lasers without biopsy) results in Recurrent Melanocytic Nevus, which shows marked clinical and histologic atypia mimicking melanoma! Always perform complete surgical excision if removal is indicated.

Malignant Tumors of the Skin

Keratoacanthoma, Cutaneous SCC, Basal Cell Carcinoma, and Cutaneous Melanoma

1. Keratoacanthoma (KA)

Classification: Considered a subtype of highly differentiated Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) with distinct rapid clinical growth and potential for spontaneous regression. Classified as malignant in current literature.

Clinical Hallmark: Sharply circumscribed, dome-shaped firm nodule with a central keratin-filled horn/crater. Erupts rapidly over 2-6 weeks on sun-exposed head/extremities.

2. Cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma (cSCC)

Epidemiology: 2nd most common skin cancer in immunocompetent individuals. Higher incidence in men >60 years old, fair skin, albinism, or organ transplant recipients.

Pathophysiology: UV-induced mutations in TP53 tumor suppressor gene. Multistep progression: Arises from preinvasive precursor lesions — Actinic Keratosis or Bowen Disease (cSCC rarely arises de novo on undamaged skin!).

Clinical Presentation: Slowly enlarging, firm skin-colored to erythematous plaque/nodule with marked hyperkeratosis, ulceration, or exophytic growth.

NCCN Risk Stratification by Anatomic Location

Anatomic Location AreaAnatomic Subsites IncludedHigh Risk ThresholdPrimary Recommended Surgery
Area H ("Mask Areas") Central face, eyelids, eyebrows, periorbital, nose, lips, chin, mandible, pre/postauricular, temple, ear, genitalia, hands, feet. High Risk Regardless of Size! Mohs Micrographic Surgery (for maximal tissue conservation & margin clearance)
Area M Cheeks, forehead, scalp, neck, pretibia. Size ≥ 10 mm Mohs or Excision with wider margins
Area L Trunk & extremities (excluding pretibia, hands, feet, nail units, ankles). Size ≥ 20 mm Standard Excision (4-6 mm margins for low risk)
⭐ cSCC High-Risk Features for Recurrence / Metastasis
  • Tumor thickness > 2 mm or Clark Level ≥ IV
  • Perineural, vascular, or lymphatic invasion
  • Primary site: Lip or Ear
  • Histologic grade: Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated (G3/G4)
  • Subtypes: Acantholytic, desmoplastic, or spindle-cell

3. Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Epidemiology: Most common cancer in humans (~75% of all non-melanoma skin cancers). Typical latency of 20–50 years post-UV exposure. Head & neck predominant.

Molecular Pathogenesis: Mutations activating the Hedgehog Signaling Pathway:

  • PTCH1 (73%): Receptor for Hedgehog ligand (tumor suppressor).
  • SMO (20%): Oncogene component of Hedgehog signaling.
  • SUFU (8%): Tumor suppressor.
  • TP53 (61%): Co-mutation.

Clinical & Histologic Subtypes of BCC

SubtypeClinical DescriptionKey Distinguishing Feature
Nodular BCC Most common subtype. Head & neck. Translucent, pearly papule/nodule with rolled borders and telangiectasias. Central ulceration forms classic "Rodent Ulcer".
Pigmented BCC Variant of nodular BCC with increased melanization. Translucent, hyperpigmented papule. Differential: Nodular Melanoma, Seborrheic Keratosis.
Superficial BCC Well-demarcated erythematous patch on trunk/extremities. Resembles eczema or psoriasis. Isolated "eczema" non-responsive to topical steroids. Tx: Imiquimod 5% or 5-FU.
Morpheaform BCC Aggressive variant. Ivory-white, indurated, scar-like plaque without prior trauma/surgery. Infiltrative growth with ill-defined margins. Requires Mohs Surgery!
Basosquamous Carcinoma Aggressive growth. Shows dual differentiation (basal cell + squamous cell). High metastatic potential compared to typical BCC.
Fibroepithelioma of Pinkus Pink, solitary papule/nodule on the lower back. Confused with acrocordon (skin tag) or dermal nevus.

Medical / Targeted Therapy for BCC: Vismodegib & Sonidegib (FDA-approved Hedgehog pathway inhibitors for locally advanced or metastatic BCC).

4. Cutaneous Melanoma

Potentially lethal malignant tumor of melanocytes. Median age at diagnosis: 63. Two main risk factors: UV light exposure (intermittent blistering sunburns) and genetic susceptibility (CDKN2A / p16INK4a mutations in ~40% familial cases).

🧠 ABCDE Clinical Screening Criteria
  • A – Asymmetry: One half does not match the other.
  • B – Border: Irregular, scalloped, notched, or ill-defined edges.
  • C – Color: Variation in shade (brown, black, blue-gray, red, white).
  • D – Diameter: > 5 mm or > 6 mm.
  • E – Evolution: Change in size, shape, color, or new elevation/symptoms over time.
*Note: ABCDE criteria do NOT reliably apply to Nodular Melanoma or Desmoplastic Melanoma! Use "Ugly Duckling Sign" (a lesion distinct from all other nevi in the individual).

Major Clinical Subtypes of Cutaneous Melanoma

SubtypeFrequencyPredilection SiteClinical Features & Behavior
Superficial Spreading Melanoma (SSM) 70% (Most common) Lower extremities in women; Upper back in men. Associated with preexisting nevi. Long radial growth phase (months/years) before vertical invasion. Variable colors.
Nodular Melanoma (NM) 15% – 30% Trunk Lacks radial growth phase! Rapid vertical growth (weeks). Uniformly dark blue-black or red dome nodule. Arises de novo.
Lentigo Maligna Melanoma (LMM) 10% – 15% Chronically sun-damaged face/neck in elderly (7th-8th decade). Arises from Lentigo Maligna (in situ). Flat, slowly expanding freckle-like macule with irregular borders.
Acral Lentiginous Melanoma (ALM) Most common in dark skin (60-72% in Black patients, 29-46% in Asians) Palms, soles of feet, subungual nail bed. NOT associated with sun exposure! Subungual variant shows melanonychia + Hutchinson sign.
Desmoplastic Melanoma (DM) Rare Head and neck in elderly. Sclerotic/indurated plaque. 50% are amelanotic. Deeply invasive with perineural neurotropism.
Mucosal Melanoma 1.3% Nasal cavity (most common), oral, genital, anorectal. Irregular pigmented bleeding mass. High rate of occult metastasis.

Systemic & Targeted Therapies for Advanced Melanoma:

  • BRAF Inhibitors: Vemurafenib, Dabrafenib (given with MEK inhibitors: Trametinib, Cobimetinib).
  • Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors: Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA4), Nivolumab / Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1).
  • Chemotherapy: Dacarbazine (DTIC) — only FDA-approved single chemotherapeutic agent.

⚡ Ultra-Condensed Last-Minute Cram Sheet

High-yield memory tricks, buzzwords, and clinical decision triggers readable in under 10 minutes

🚨 "IF YOU SEE X → THINK Y" TRIGGERS
  • Trunk-to-limbs maculopapular rash, no blisters → Exanthematous Drug Eruption (~95%)
  • Fever + rash + eosinophilia + organ failure → DRESS / HSR
  • Hypothyroidism 6 months after drug reaction → Late DRESS Thyroiditis
  • Hundreds of sterile nonfollicular pustules + neutrophilia → AGEP
  • Dusky red plaque recurring at EXACT same spot → Fixed Drug Eruption
  • Epidermal detachment <10% BSA + (+) Nikolsky → Stevens-Johnson Syndrome
  • Epidermal detachment >30% BSA + (+) Nikolsky → Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis
  • Sudden outbreak of multiple seborrheic keratoses → Leser-Trélat Sign (GI Malignancy)
  • 1-2mm white eyelid cyst with stratified squamous & keratin → Milium / Milia
  • Periorbital yellow papules with "tadpole tail" ducts → Syringoma
  • Yellow facial papule, soft, expressible sebum → Sebaceous Hyperplasia
  • Pearly translucent papule + rolled border + telangiectasia → Nodular BCC
  • Ivory-white scar-like plaque on face without trauma → Morpheaform BCC
  • Hyperkeratotic nodule arising on Actinic Keratosis → Squamous Cell Carcinoma
  • Rapidly erupting nodule with central keratin crater → Keratoacanthoma
  • Pigment extending onto proximal nail fold → Hutchinson Sign (Subungual Melanoma)
  • Pigmented lesion looks completely different from all others → Ugly Duckling Sign (Melanoma)
🎯 SCORTEN CRITERIA MEMORY AID

Calculated in first 24h for Epidermal Necrolysis (1 pt each):

  • 1. Age > 40 years
  • 2. Heart Rate > 120 bpm
  • 3. Malignancy present
  • 4. BSA Detached > 10%
  • 5. Serum Urea > 10 mmol/L
  • 6. Serum Bicarbonate < 20 mmol/L
  • 7. Serum Glucose > 14 mmol/L

Mortality: Score 0-1 (3.2%) → Score 2 (12.1%) → Score 3 (35.8%) → Score 4 (58.3%) → Score 5+ (90%)

⚡ HIGH-RISK DRUG LISTS
  • SJS / TEN High Risk: Allopurinol, Lamotrigine, Cotrimoxazole, Carbamazepine, Nevirapine, Meloxicam (Oxicams), Phenobarbital, Phenytoin.
  • SJS / TEN NO Risk: Ibuprofen (Propionic), Beta-blockers, ACEi, CCBs, Thiazides, Sulfonylureas, Insulin, Valproic acid.
  • DRESS Culprits: Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine, Sulfonamides, Dapsone, Allopurinol, Nevirapine, Minocycline.
  • AGEP Culprits: Beta-lactams, Macrolides, Quinolones, Diltiazem, Terbinafine.
🩸 CANCER GENETICS & STAINING SUMMARY
  • BCC Genetics: Hedgehog Pathway — PTCH1 (73%), SMO (20%), SUFU (8%), TP53 (61%).
  • cSCC Genetics: UV-induced TP53 mutation.
  • Melanoma Genetics: CDKN2A (p16INK4a/p14ARF) in 40% familial cases; BRAF V600E mutations.
  • Melanoma IHC Stains:
    • S100 / Sox10: Sensitive, expressed by all melanomas.
    • HMB-45: Highly specific for melanoma cells.
    • Melan-A / MART-1: More sensitive than HMB-45.
    • MITF: Useful for amelanotic melanoma.
